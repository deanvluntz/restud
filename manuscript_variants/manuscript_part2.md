# 5. Empirical Strategy

## 5.1 Specification

The estimating equation is the empirical counterpart of the policy rule derived in Section 3: an autoregressive distributed lag model, ARDL(1), in log-level form,

[[EQ]]
ln mₜ = α + ρ ln mₜ₋₁ + β₁ Δ̂dₜ(10) + β₂ τₜ₋₁ + β₃ Δln Bₜ₋₁ − β₄ uₜ₋₁ + εₜ,
[[/EQ]]

where mₜ is non-EU inflows per thousand (lagged) working-age residents, Δ̂dₜ(10) is the projected ten-year change in the OADR from the current NPP vintage, τₜ₋₁ is the lagged tax take per working-age person, Δln Bₜ₋₁ the lagged annual log change in the debt-to-GDP ratio and uₜ₋₁ the lagged unemployment rate. The log-level form implies that policy responds proportionally to fiscal state variables, so that a given projected deterioration calls forth a given percentage adjustment of the inflow, and, as reported in Section 6.1, it is also the form favoured by specification tests. The lagged dependent variable captures the adjustment costs of the model and the persistence of flows documented throughout the UK literature (Mitchell and Pain, 2003; Hatton, 2005). A second specification replaces τₜ₋₁ with a deterministic trend; the two cannot be separated statistically (their correlation over the sample is 0.98), so both are reported in Table 2, and the fiscal specification is the one carried through the remainder of the paper.

The choice of estimator deserves brief defence against the alternatives. An instrumental-variables design would be preferable in principle, but Section 4.4 documented that no valid instrument for UK migration exists in the literature. Difference-in-differences and regression-discontinuity designs are unavailable for want of a control group or an assignment threshold: the policy regime applied to the whole non-EU margin at once, and its changes were continuous rather than discrete. A cross-country panel would trade the UK's institutional depth for breadth the hypothesis does not yet need; it is the natural second study (Section 8), not the first. Within the single-country time-series setting, the ARDL framework is the standard recommended for UK migration data (Mitchell and Pain, 2003): it accommodates the persistence of flows, nests the static model, supports bounds testing for level relationships among I(0)/I(1) variables, and delivers long-run multipliers with interpretable structural content under the model of Section 3.

Inference throughout uses heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors, with Newey–West errors reported as a check; conventional OLS standard errors are shown for comparison in Online Appendix Table B2.

## 5.2 Identification: timing, threats, and partial remedies

The design claims that the regressors are predetermined with respect to the current year's inflow shock: the fiscal controls are lagged realisations, and the projection variable is a forecast fixed before the flows it is asked to predict. Within-year reverse causality from inflows to the regressors is thereby excluded by construction. Three threats survive this timing argument and are taken in increasing order of seriousness.

The first is the lagged dependent variable. Ordinary least squares is consistent here but biased in finite samples: the lagged regressor is not strictly exogenous, and the resulting attenuation of the autoregressive coefficient is of order −(1 + 3ρ)/T (Hurwicz, 1950; Kendall, 1954). At T = 43 and ρ ≈ 0.5 this is roughly −0.06, so the persistence estimates below are lower bounds, and with them the long-run multipliers, which divide by (1 − ρ). The direction is therefore conservative for the paper's magnitudes. A recursive-design residual bootstrap of the fiscal specification (2,000 replications, exogenous regressors held at their observed values) estimates the bias at −0.11, raising the persistence estimate from 0.47 to approximately 0.58 and the implied long-run response to the projection from 1.5 to approximately 2.0 per cent per point, with a recentred bootstrap percentile interval of [0.9, 2.8] per cent. The estimated bias exceeds the analytic value of −0.058 because the latter is derived for a pure autoregression, while the estimating equation adds four persistent and mutually correlated regressors. A Monte Carlo calibration confirms the procedure: on simulated data with known persistence at the same sample size, mean OLS bias reproduces the analytic prediction and the bootstrap correction is nearly unbiased. The bias operates on the autoregressive term and cannot manufacture significance of the projection term. It is also distinct from consistency: serially correlated errors would correlate the lagged term with the *contemporaneous* disturbance, which is why the Breusch–Godfrey tests reported throughout are a precondition for treating it as predetermined.

The second is publication timing. NPP vintages are labelled by base year but released roughly a year later; assigning them by base year assumes the policymakers saw the projections before the public did. For the GAD era (1954–2006), when the projections were produced inside government on Treasury-agreed assumptions, the assumption is institutionally grounded; it is weaker for the ONS era. The conservative alternative dates each vintage from the year after its base, so that the projection in force is the latest vintage published by the time the year begins. This reassigns the vintage in 22 of the 43 sample years; Section 7 re-estimates on that basis.

The third and most fundamental threat is that the projections are not mechanically exogenous: every NPP vintage embeds an assumption about future net migration, so the regressor contains a function of the expected path of the dependent variable. Three points bound the resulting concern. First, the direction of the mechanical contamination is attenuating for the hypothesis: higher assumed migration lowers the projected OADR (migrants enter the working-age denominator), so a vintage that anticipates the current expansion of inflows produces a smaller Δ̂dₜ(10), biasing β₁ toward zero, not away from it. Second, the demographic arithmetic limits the contamination in the numerator: the old-age population ten years ahead consists almost entirely of persons aged 55 and over who are already resident, so the numerator of the projected ratio is essentially invariant to migration assumptions at the ten-year horizon. Third, the clean remedy is implemented: from each vintage's published net-migration assumptions, Section 7 rebuilds the projected OADR under zero net migration from the observation year forward and re-estimates both specifications with the purged regressor. The results are essentially unchanged, so the finding does not depend on the migration content of the projections.

The design's identification status, stated plainly: the estimates are not experimental, and the paper does not claim they are. The claim is narrower and, within its limits, strong: a predetermined official expectation of ageing predicts subsequent policy-controlled inflows conditional on the fiscal state; the mechanical channel from migration into the expectation is removed outright by the zero-migration reconstruction; and the confounding structures most naturally levelled at the result are tested and rejected by the placebo margins of Section 7. The agenda of Section 8 specifies the designs that could carry the claim the rest of the way.

Two further design choices bear on identification. Classical measurement error in the IPS dependent variable inflates standard errors without biasing coefficients, and the LTIM check (Section 7) addresses the non-classical components the ONS has documented; and the working-age denominator is lagged one year so that current inflows cannot enter both sides of the equation through the population count.

A fourth, generic threat is an omitted common driver: some force that both worsened projected ageing and attracted non-EU migrants through non-policy channels. This is the threat the falsification tests are built to address: a confounder operating through labour demand or general attractiveness should load onto EU inflows, which were uncontrolled and highly elastic, and onto the total dependency ratio insofar as the driver is demographic rather than fiscal. Section 7 shows it does neither.

## 5.3 Diagnostics and specification protocol

Every reported specification is accompanied by a fixed battery of diagnostics, each of which earns its place in this setting. Ramsey's RESET test screens for neglected nonlinearity and, indirectly, omitted-variable bias expressible as functions of the fitted values; it rejected the level-form specification and forced the move to logs (Section 6.1). The Breusch–Godfrey test, at first and second order, and preferred to Durbin–Watson, which is invalid with a lagged dependent variable, is a condition for consistency, not a formality: serially correlated errors would correlate ln mₜ₋₁ with the disturbance, so passing it is what licenses treating the lagged term as predetermined. Breusch–Pagan tests for heteroskedasticity, with HAC inference as a second line of defence. The Jarque–Bera test on residuals matters more than usual because, at 43 observations, exact t- and F-inference leans on approximate normality rather than asymptotics. Maximum variance inflation factors monitor the multicollinearity that a short trending sample invites.

Because the variables are trending, the time-series properties receive separate treatment. Orders of integration are established by sequential testing (ADF with trend on levels and drift on differences, lags selected by information criterion, with KPSS as the confirmatory mirror), and a series that fails both stages is flagged rather than forced into a class (Online Appendix Table B1); every regressor is I(0) or I(1), the condition for validity of bounds testing, with one non-regressor series left unresolved. The Pesaran–Shin–Smith bounds test provides the formal test of a level relationship, distinguishing a genuine long-run relation from the spurious co-movement of drifting series, with the associated error-correction form yielding long-run multipliers. The power of all unit-root procedures at this sample size is limited, and the reading of their results in Section 6.3 is correspondingly restrained.

Because the theory pins down the conditioning set only up to broad categories, some specification search was unavoidable. The protocol was fixed in advance: candidates were limited to the variables named by the institutional literature (Section 4.3), retained or excluded on joint statistical and theoretical grounds, and every candidate tested is reported (Section 6.1), including the failures. The headline coefficient's robustness across every specification tried, not the significance of any single regression, is the basis of the paper's claim.

# 6. Results

## 6.1 Specification development

A brief account of the path to the final specification guards against the impression of a curated result; full output for every step is available on request.

The starting point for the projection horizon was a five-year window, matching the electoral cycle, the Treasury's biennial Spending Reviews, departmental review cycles of three to five years, and the five-year forecast horizon of Budget-time fiscal projections. It was at first implemented as a weighted average of projected OADR changes with weights declining uniformly in distance. The initial level-form specification regressed the inflow rate on this measure, the lagged tax take per working-age person, and lagged unemployment, inactivity, and productivity growth. It produced a strongly significant projection coefficient but failed the Breusch–Godfrey test (p = 0.018), motivating the autoregressive term that the adjustment-cost logic of Section 3 independently implies. An interaction between the projection and the tax burden, prediction P2 taken literally as a modifier rather than a level shifter, generated extreme collinearity (maximum VIF above 100) and was abandoned in favour of the additive form.

The candidate controls resolved as follows. No measure of growth (aggregate real GDP, GDP per capita, output per worker) attained significance in any variant, unsurprising for output per worker given the UK's prolonged productivity stagnation, and consistent with a policy rule oriented to the extensive rather than the intensive margin. Neither gilt yield mattered, suggesting the debt *stock* rather than its price is the operative constraint. The Budget-time growth forecast was significant at the five per cent level in some variants, but only at the cost of destabilising the remaining coefficients, and was excluded. The inactivity rate hovered at the margin of significance, was nearly time-invariant over the sample, and was dropped when degrees of freedom required it. The level-form ARDL passed all diagnostics except RESET, which favoured the log-level form; in logs, the projection horizon that fits best lengthens from five to ten years, consistent with the primacy of the Treasury's planning horizon over the electoral cycle, and an empirical answer to the horizon question posed above. That the statutory minimum horizon for the government's own published long-term projections is likewise ten years (Section 2.4) is a useful coincidence: the specification the data select is the one the fiscal framework mandates. The lagged log change in the debt ratio enters strongly. The trend variant arises because the trend and the tax burden are nearly collinear (r = 0.98) and cannot be jointly included: the tax burden has the theoretical claim (P2) and the trend the cleaner exogeneity. Entered together, with the collinearity caveat that implies (maximum VIF of 55), the tax term holds (0.075, p < 0.001) while the trend turns negative and insignificant (−0.005, p = 0.23) and the projection coefficient is essentially unchanged at 0.0085, so the trend is best read as the tax burden's atheoretical stand-in. Table 2 reports both; every subsequent result is stated in the fiscal specification, and each has been re-estimated with the trend in its place without changing any coefficient's sign or significance class (Online Appendix Table B3).

The projection term is the paper's central result. In the fiscal specification (column 1), a one-point increase in the projected ten-year rise of the OADR, one additional prospective pensioner per thousand working-age residents, is associated with a 0.8 per cent increase in the current non-EU inflow rate (coefficient 0.0082, HAC standard error 0.0015). Scaled by the sample variation, a one-standard-deviation rise in the projected change (8.9 points) raises inflows by about 7.6 per cent in the year and, through the persistence term, by about 15 per cent in the long run before the finite-sample correction of Section 5.2, which raises it further (Table 3). The coefficient in the trend specification is close (0.0073). Both are stable, at similar magnitudes and significance, across projection horizons of 3, 5 and 7 years, weighted averages of horizons, projected levels in place of changes, and sample start dates of 1979, 1983 and 1987 (Section 7).

The controls carry the signs the model predicts. The lagged tax burden (P2): each additional thousand pounds of annual tax per working-age person is associated with 5.2 per cent higher inflows. This is the substitution effect at the heart of the salience mechanism: governments inheriting a heavier tax burden face a higher marginal political price on the loud instrument and lean harder on the quiet one. Its positive sign is worth pausing on, because a naive fiscal-need story might predict the opposite (high taxes indicating adjustment already achieved). The debt trajectory (P3): the coefficient of 0.54 on the lagged log change in debt/GDP implies that a one-percentage-point faster rise in the debt ratio is followed by roughly 0.5 per cent higher inflows, so the years of sharpest fiscal deterioration are those followed by the largest expansions of the quiet margin. Lagged unemployment (P4): a percentage point of unemployment reduces inflows by about 2 per cent, consistent with both the political-cost channel and conventional labour-demand effects on migration; the two cannot be separated here, and no interpretation rests on the distinction. Persistence (ρ ≈ 0.47 in column 1 and 0.69 in column 2, both understated by the finite-sample attenuation of Section 5.2) matches the flow persistence documented across the UK literature.

The fit is tight (adjusted R² of 0.96), though with a lagged dependent variable and trending data the R² overstates explanatory success; the economically meaningful statement is that the projection term remains individually significant at the one per cent level under HAC and Newey–West inference in the presence of the autoregressive term, the trend (or its fiscal twin), and the business-cycle control. Diagnostics pass in both columns at the five per cent level: RESET, Breusch–Godfrey at first and second order, Breusch–Pagan and Jarque–Bera all fail to reject, though in the fiscal specification the second-order Breusch–Godfrey test is close to the margin (p = 0.06) and at third and fourth order it rejects (p = 0.04 and 0.03), which is why inference throughout is HAC and the long-run interval is also computed by moving-block bootstrap (Online Appendix B). The fiscal specification's maximum VIF (13.0) exceeds the conventional threshold of 10, but it belongs to the lagged dependent variable (13.0) and the lagged tax burden (10.4), two trending levels that share the growth of the period; the projection term's VIF is 1.3. Rules of thumb for the VIF are in any case poor guides to whether collinearity matters, which depends on whether the estimates are precise enough for the question at hand (O'Brien, 2007). Finally, the trend specification, with a maximum VIF of 6.0, returns a projection coefficient of 0.0073, so nothing in the headline result depends on the collinearity of the fiscal specification.

The magnitudes sit comfortably within the UK literature where comparison is possible. The unemployment semi-elasticity of around 2 per cent per point is of the order found in studies with and without free-movement flows in the sample, and its presence in *lagged* form accords with Hatton's (2005) finding that migration responds to labour-market changes rather than levels. The persistence parameter brackets the range in Mitchell and Pain (2003). The projection term has no published counterpart, which is the paper's point, but its long-run multiplier can be benchmarked internally: between 1997 and 2002 the projected ten-year OADR change rose by some nineteen points, which through the long-run multiplier of Table 3 implies an eventual expansion of the non-EU inflow rate on the order of three-tenths of a log point, a substantial share of the observed rise over those years, and a measured alternative to the unexplained structural dummies of the Bayesian averaging literature.

## 6.3 Cointegration and the long run

The regressors are a mixture of I(1) and I(0) series (Online Appendix Table B1). The mixture is itself informative, since it rules out the possibility that the level relationship is an artefact of common integration order, and it dictates the testing framework: residual-based cointegration methods presume all-I(1) systems, whereas the bounds procedure of Pesaran, Shin and Smith (2001), implemented with the R package ARDL (Natsiopoulos, Tzeremes and Finnan, 2026), is valid for any combination of I(0) and I(1) regressors, which is why it, together with ADF tests on the residuals, carries the inferential weight here. The level relationship requires justification beyond significance. ADF tests reject a unit root in the residuals of both specifications (p = 0.022 and 0.046). The bounds F-test on the error-correction form rejects the null of no level relationship at the one per cent level (F = 6.16, p < 0.01), and the t-version rejects at five per cent (t = −4.09, p < 0.05), beyond the I(1) bound. The statistics are exact; the p-values come from a stochastic response surface and are reported to two decimals accordingly. This is supportive evidence of cointegration, but with 43 observations the tests have limited power, and the honest statement is that the data are consistent with a long-run equilibrium relationship rather than proof of one.

The long-run response to the projection is approximately 2.0 per cent higher inflows per projected point of OADR deterioration once the finite-sample correction of Section 5.2 is applied, some two and a half times the impact effect, against an uncorrected least-squares multiplier of 1.5 per cent (p = 0.002), with the tax-burden and debt multipliers similarly amplified. Through the lens of the model, the amplification is the cumulative adjustment of a persistent policy instrument to a persistent signal: with an autoregressive coefficient near one half, roughly half of any gap between the current inflow rate and its target closes each year, so a sustained deterioration builds over two to three years to twice its impact size. That adjustment speed is consistent with the administrative propagation lags of entry policy discussed in Section 3, where rule changes work through application pipelines and sponsor behaviour over quarters rather than weeks, and it implies that annual data, while coarse, do not miss the bulk of the dynamics.

## 6.4 Reading the history through the model

The estimated rule acquires content when laid against the sample's political history, because the projection variable moves at times that no party-political account selects. Figure 1 plots the two series.

The projected ten-year OADR change was negative at the start of the sample and first turned positive in the early 1980s, as the leading edge of the post-war birth surge entered the projections' ten-year window; non-EU inflows rose in 1981–85, under a Conservative government committed publicly to restriction, a decade and a half before New Labour. The delta declined through the late 1980s and turned negative around 1990, after which inflows stagnated; it returned to positive territory in 1996, and inflows resumed growth in the final year of the Major administration, before the change of government. In 1997 the delta reached a then-record 6.1 points, and the vintages of the early 2000s repriced the future sharply: in 2002–03 the delta averaged roughly 25 points as the first baby-boom wave arrived a decade from pension age. Through the estimated short-run coefficient, projections of that size call for expansions of inflows on the order of 20 per cent per year from the projection channel alone; gross non-EU inflows cleared 300,000 for the first time in 2004, more than triple their level a decade earlier, the lag between signal and response being what the adjustment costs of Section 3 imply.

Read through the model, then, the celebrated post-1997 policy turn was not the arrival of a new ideology but the arrival of the baby boomer at the projection horizon (women of those cohorts reaching the then-female state pension age of 60): a government of either party, facing the same vintages, is predicted to have expanded the quiet margin, and the pre-1997 record shows governments of the other party doing exactly that whenever the projections instructed it. Accelerations timed to the projections rather than to changes of administration are the pattern a party-specific structural-change account leaves unexplained and the fiscal account predicts; Section 7 runs the comparison formally. The counterfactual is worth stating plainly: had the 1997 election gone the other way, the model expects the subsequent expansion to have occurred in any case, in different rhetoric.

# 7. Robustness and Falsification

Table 4 collects the main exercises, all in the fiscal specification, and Figure 4 at the end of the section summarises every variant; the trend-specification counterparts are in Online Appendix Table B3 and change no conclusion. The two placebo tests carry the greatest evidential weight because they are tests the fiscal hypothesis could have failed.

[[TABLE4]]

*Zero-net-migration projections.* This check addresses the mechanical-endogeneity threat of Section 5.2 directly. From each vintage's published components of change, the projected OADR at every horizon is recomputed under zero net migration from the observation year forward, by removing the vintage's cumulative assumed net migration from the projected working-age denominator (Online Appendix A); the regressor then cannot contain the vintages' migration assumptions. Substituted into the specification it returns 0.0075 (HAC s.e. 0.0013), against 0.0082 with the published projections, with unchanged significance, comparable diagnostics (Breusch–Godfrey at both orders in fact cleaner; RESET and Jarque–Bera far from rejection; residual ADF still rejects a unit root), and an implied long-run response of 1.4 per cent. The response of controlled migration to projected ageing is not an artefact of the projections' own migration assumptions.

*Publication dating.* Dating each vintage from the year after its base rather than by base year (Section 5.2) reassigns the vintage in force in 22 of the 43 sample years, moving one observation by more than fifteen points. The estimate barely moves: 0.0090 (HAC s.e. 0.0015) against 0.0082 under base dating, significant at 0.1 per cent and with diagnostics still passing; the zero-migration variant returns 0.0076 (0.0016). Both falsifications survive: the total dependency ratio falls to 0.0002 (p = 0.91) and the EU placebo stays insignificant (p = 0.28). The timing assumption does not carry the result.

*News versus level.* A slow common trend could in principle produce a relationship between two smoothly rising series. The vintages allow a sharper test. In the twenty-two sample years in which a new vintage came into force, the projection the Treasury saw can be split into the part the previous vintage had already reported for the same years and the revision the new vintage introduced, which is news on publication (standard deviation 5.3 points across those years, range −15.6 to +9.8). Entered separately, the inherited part carries 0.0092 (p < 0.001) and the revision 0.0029 (p = 0.53); the two are not statistically distinguishable (F(1, 36) = 1.64, p = 0.21), and the trend specification gives 0.0078 and 0.0040 (p = 0.41; equality p = 0.44). The response to news has the right sign and about a third of the size of the response to the inherited level, but twenty-two one-off revisions in annual data cannot estimate it precisely: the test is consistent with the projections working through what they newly report, and cannot establish it. Establishing it is what the event-study design of Section 8 is for.

*The projection's marginal contribution.* Because the specification carries a lagged dependent variable, adjusted R² reaches 0.94 before the projection enters, and the increment it adds to R² is correspondingly small; the informative comparison is the nested one. A HAC Wald test of dropping the projection term gives F(1, 37) = 32.23 (p < 0.001), and the information criterion improves by 14.9 points when it is restored — a difference conventionally taken to leave no support for the restricted model. The functional-form evidence agrees: without the projection, RESET falls to p = 0.12, against p = 0.41 with it, and in the trend specification it rejects outright (p = 0.048 against 0.078). The omission does not merely cost fit; it leaves detectable misspecification.

*Old-age versus total dependency.* If the mechanism is fiscal, the projection that matters is the old-age ratio, since pensioners are expensive, not the total ratio, whose child component carries a far smaller fiscal price (Section 2.4). Substituting the projected ten-year change in the total dependency ratio for the OADR change eliminates the result: the coefficient falls to 0.0020 (p = 0.16) and is statistically indistinguishable from zero, while the same substitution in every other log-level variant, at every horizon and in levels as in deltas, likewise yields nothing. Measured against the no-projection baseline above, the contrast is sharper than significance alone conveys: the old-age ratio improves the information criterion by 14.9 points, the total ratio by 0.1 (HAC Wald F(1, 37) = 2.11, p = 0.16). The total ratio does not merely fail to reach significance — it barely earns the degree of freedom it costs, where the old-age ratio transforms the fit. A generic demographic confounder, or a data-mined artefact of trending series, has no reason to select the pensioner-specific ratio so cleanly; a fiscal mechanism must.

*The uncontrolled margin.* Might some unobserved driver, such as rising UK attractiveness, labour demand, or network effects, correlate with projected ageing and move all migration, with policy irrelevant? One version is even behavioural: migrants themselves targeting ageing destinations where labour-market competition is projected to thin. EU inflows provide the test, responding freely to every such driver but not to UK entry policy. Re-estimating the full specification on EU inflows, constructed identically, returns an OADR-projection coefficient of 0.0030 (p = 0.41); only the persistence term survives. Figure 2 shows the contrast directly, as added-variable plots in which the fitted slope is the regression coefficient: a clear conditional relationship on the controlled margin, none on the uncontrolled one. Whatever moved non-EU inflows in step with projected ageing did not move the flows the government could not ration. The confound would have to operate exclusively on the controlled margin, which is to say it would have to *be* policy.

Adding their full indicator set (POL97, POL02 for the 2002 skilled-worker route, and enlargement indicators for 1986, 1995 and 2004) to the fiscal specification leaves the indicators jointly irrelevant under HAC inference (F(5, 32) = 1.09, p = 0.38), while the projection holds at 0.0080 (p = 0.001). *Recognition of the mechanism elsewhere.* The salience model makes a prediction about who advertises the instrument. Westminster held the entry lever throughout the sample and used it without ever campaigning on it; Scotland's devolved government, which does not hold it, campaigns for it explicitly and publishes this paper's regressor as a policy target, calculating that maintaining the Scottish dependency ratio requires the working-age population to grow by some 32,000 a year and proposing a devolved visa to achieve it (Scottish Government, 2018, 2020). Advocacy without the instrument carries no price for the inflows themselves, so visibility of the demand runs inverse to possession of the lever — which is what pricing policy by political exposure predicts, on a case the model was not built from. The demographic arithmetic, meanwhile, is evidently understood well beyond the Treasury.

*The scaling choice.* Expressing inflows per thousand working-age residents is the analyst's decision, not the policymaker's: entry policy rations admissions, and nothing obliges a Chancellor to think in per-capita terms. The result does not depend on it. On gross non-EU inflows, with no population estimate on either side of the equation, the projection coefficient is 0.0082 (HAC s.e. 0.0015), identical to its scaled counterpart, significant beyond the 0.1 per cent level, with clean diagnostics. In levels rather than logs the pattern holds and acquires a directly interpretable magnitude: each further point of projected ten-year OADR deterioration is associated with roughly 1,300 to 1,400 additional gross arrivals per year (1,379, HAC s.e. 318). The choice of tax measure can be tested rather than argued (Online Appendix Tables B4 and B5). Replacing national accounts taxes per working-age person with the same taxes per resident, or with income tax and National Insurance contributions — with or without VAT — on either denominator, leaves the projection coefficient between 0.0081 and 0.0098 and the tax term significant at 1 per cent in every case; measuring the burden as a share of GDP instead removes the tax term (p = 0.70 for all taxes, p = 0.53 for labour taxes, p = 0.43 with VAT added) and weakens the specification (RESET p = 0.026 and 0.007), a share of output being a burden on no one in particular. The average effective tax rate on labour, income tax and contributions over compensation of employees following Mendoza, Razin and Tesar (1994), behaves like the per-capita measures (0.030 per percentage point, p = 0.017; projection 0.0064, p = 0.001). What the burden is divided by matters; what is counted in it does not.

Measurement basis. On the LTIM series (1991 onward), which corrects the IPS's known deficiencies with Home Office and Labour Force Survey data, the projection coefficient is larger and significant at the 0.1 per cent level (0.0159, HAC s.e. 0.0040, on 28 observations), evidence that the headline result is not an artefact of the survey deficiencies documented in Section 4.1.

*Functional form and horizon of the signal.* The projected level of the OADR at the ten-year horizon, in place of the projected change, returns an almost identical coefficient (0.0085 against 0.0082, p < 0.001), and for a reason that supports the change formulation rather than competing with it: the level is the current ratio plus the projected change, so the level model constrains the two to a common coefficient. Entered separately, the projection holds at 0.0083 while the current ratio contributes nothing (0.0002, p = 0.95), and the equality restriction is rejected (F(1, 36) = 8.28, p = 0.007), which is why the level form fits worse (AIC −63.9 against −70.1). The change formulation is retained as the measure of news relative to the inherited position, and the data agree. Figure 3 plots the coefficient at every horizon from one to ten years. The projection term is significant at each horizon from three years on, in changes and levels and in weighted averages across horizons, with the ten-year delta fitting best, while the total dependency ratio registers at no horizon. The exception is instructive: at one and two years the change carries no signal (p = 0.47 and 0.11), consistent with policy responding to medium-term ageing news rather than to next year's arithmetic. The result does not depend on the choice of any single medium-term horizon.

*Dynamics.* The fiscal specification's residuals show serial correlation at the third and fourth orders (Section 6.2). A second lag of the dependent variable does not absorb it: the added lag is small and insignificant (−0.06, p = 0.65), the information criterion worsens, the Breusch–Godfrey tests do not improve, and the projection coefficient is unchanged at 0.0080. The dependence is a feature of the error rather than a missing dynamic, which is what HAC inference and the block bootstrap of Online Appendix B are designed to handle.

*Timing placebo.* With 43 annual observations, a finite-sample check that does not lean on asymptotic inference is worth having. Rotating the projection series through the sample years keeps its own persistence and distribution but breaks its timing relative to inflows; re-estimating under each of the 42 rotations gives a distribution of placebo t-statistics against which the actual one can be ranked. None comes close: the largest absolute t-statistic among the rotations is 3.97, against 5.68 for the actual alignment, so the randomisation p-value is 1/43 = 0.023, the smallest this design can deliver. The result depends on when the projections moved, not merely on how they are distributed.

*Sample and inference.* Results survive beginning the sample in 1979, 1983 or 1987, the substitution of the trend for the tax burden, and OLS, HAC or Newey–West inference, so the result is not carried by any single political era, entry regime, or macroeconomic episode. Figure 4 collects the projection coefficient from every variant in this section: all fifteen intervals exclude zero and overlap the baseline, while both placebo intervals include zero.

*The labour-market state, the tax base, and the macroeconomic environment.* The Bank of England's *Millennium of Macroeconomic Data* (Thomas and Dimsdale, 2017) supplies, as annual averages of monthly series to 2016, the Bank's spliced administrative unemployment rate, notified vacancies spliced to the ONS survey, average weekly earnings and the Treasury bill rate, together with central government receipts by tax; one-year and long-term inflation expectations (entered for the year in which the forecast was made) and total-factor-productivity growth are added. These checks therefore run on 1977–2016. The unemployment measure does not matter (0.0081 with the ILO rate, 0.0082 and 0.0084 with the administrative series). Labour demand does, in the way conventional migration economics expects and without displacing the fiscal channel: vacancies per working-age person substituted for unemployment enter at 0.021 (p = 0.03) and take the projection to 0.0059 (p = 0.003), the bound on how much of the response a pure demand story could claim; the change in the vacancy–unemployment ratio beside unemployment leaves it at 0.0077. Where labour demand shows up is instructive: on EU inflows, the uncontrolled margin, vacancies enter at 0.045 (p = 0.01) while the projection is −0.004 (p = 0.34), and work permits over 1979–2008 respond to the projection (0.53 thousand per point) with or without vacancies. Lagged emigration, wage growth, the bill rate, the real rate, inflation expectations, productivity growth, the benefit replacement ratio and union density are null, with the projection between 0.0078 and 0.0091; wage inflation is negative and marginal. Replacing national accounts taxes with central government receipts by tax on the same per-working-age-person basis gives 0.0082 for income tax, 0.0101 for National Insurance contributions and 0.0079 for all direct taxes, against 0.0065 for taxes on products and production, and within contributions the employee's share does the work (0.59, p = 0.008, against 0.35 for employers'). Contributions are the earmarked receipts of the National Insurance Fund from which the state pension is paid, so a projected rise in the pensioner population is a projected shortfall in exactly this tax, and the burden that best predicts the quiet instrument is the one that would otherwise have had to rise to meet the projection. Because the inflow rate and the tax burden share the working-age denominator, the tax term could in principle be a ratio artefact (Kronmal, 1993); it is unchanged on gross inflows, with taxes per resident, and with the log of the working-age population as its own regressor (0.075, p < 0.001; the population term p = 0.19). Online Appendix Tables B6 and B7 report these columns.

# 8. Out-of-Sample Events, Limitations, and Agenda

*The post-2019 episode.* The estimation sample ends at Brexit, after which the policy regime changed discretely. The subsequent history is directionally consistent with the fiscal account: a government elected on a mandate to reduce migration instead liberalised the non-EU regime, abolishing the Resident Labour Market Test, lowering salary thresholds for settlement and shortening the settlement path (Migration Observatory, 2021), amid a pandemic that raised the debt-to-GDP ratio by more than ten percentage points in a single year and against official projections of renewed ageing as the second, larger baby-boom cohort of the 1955–65 wave approached retirement (ONS, 2019). Net migration rose from 184,000 in 2019 to over 900,000 in 2023, driven overwhelmingly by non-EU entry (Migration Observatory, 2026; BBC News, 2025). Part of that rise is mechanical rebalancing across origins under equalised entry conditions, one reason no structural claim is made on five years of post-regime-change data. The episode is noted because the model predicts the *direction* of the response to the fiscal shock, and the leading alternative, the disposition of a governing party that had just campaigned in the opposite direction, does not.

*Limitations.* Three are material. First, identification: the zero-migration reconstruction of Section 7 treats all net migrants as working-age, an approximation that the vintages' age-specific migration assumptions would refine. Second, aggregation: the dependent variable pools work, study, and family entry, and category-level responses cannot be recovered from the IPS. The pooling itself is defensible on the grounds given in Section 2.3: every category was policy-controlled, every category adds working-age residents, and students in particular arrive with work rights and spending power, with recorded departures well below arrivals over the sample. But the model's sharper implication, that the response should concentrate where administrative discretion and youth are greatest, is testable only with category-level administrative data (item (vi) below). Third, mechanism: outside the 2000–2001 episode documented in Section 2.5, the evidence remains institutional and statistical. The model explains why documentary confirmation should be scarce, since the instrument's value lies in its silence, but the drafting record of Section 2.5 shows that the reasoning was written down at least once and can therefore be found; the archival programme below extends that search to the Treasury's own files and to the Conservative-era decades the released record does not cover.

*Agenda.* Each limitation admits a concrete remedy, listed in order of importance. (i) Refine the zero-migration reconstruction of Section 7 using the vintages' age-specific migration assumptions and the official variant projections published alongside later NPPs, replacing the all-working-age approximation. (ii) Exploit the biennial NPP releases as information shocks, sharpening the news test of Section 7: the revision between successive vintages, driven substantially by mortality and fertility surprises such as the repeated upward revisions to longevity across the sample, is plausibly exogenous news about the fiscal future, and Home Office quarterly visa grants by category provide an outcome with the frequency to support an event-study design around release dates, multiplying the effective sample and permitting the timing of policy responses to be estimated rather than assumed. Concretely: decompose each revision into its mortality, fertility, and migration-assumption components using the published assumption sets, and estimate the response of visa grants by category in the quarters after each release to the mortality-and-fertility component alone. The outcome is grants rather than rule changes, consistent with Section 2.3, since a single Statement of Changes can loosen and tighten across origins at once and admits no directional coding. That design uses only news orthogonal to migration by construction, and would be decisive in a way the present annual reduced form cannot be. (iii) Take the mechanism to the archives: text analysis of Budget speeches, Red Books, and Treasury fiscal-sustainability documents for dependency-related language, correlated with subsequent Statements of Changes; and Treasury files at the National Archives, opened through the mid-1990s by the thirty-year rule, so the sample period's first half is already inside the declassification window. Undigitised Treasury forecast material (HM Treasury, 1997) would further relax the annual-data constraint. (iv) Test external validity in a cross-country panel using UN projection vintages and comparative migration-policy databases: several high-income countries saw politically unclaimed expansions as their boom cohorts approached retirement, and the model predicts the channel is strongest where fiscal authority is centralised and migration policy executive-controlled. (v) Model the pension-age and migration instruments jointly; the Pensions Act 1995 is a within-sample realisation of the alternative extensive-margin response, legislated with the low-salience profile the model predicts. (vi) Construct a spending-side measure of the same burden, state pension and health expenditure per working-age person from DWP benefit-expenditure tables and PESA outturns, to test whether the tax term stands in for the bill it finances; since the OADR terms already carry the demographic component of age-related spending, such a variable adds only policy generosity, and its expected role is that of a check rather than a rival. (vii) Estimate category-level responses across work, study and family entry on Home Office administrative data, testing whether the response concentrates where age profiles are youngest and discretion greatest.

# 9. Conclusion

This paper asked whether the sustained and politically ‘homeless’ expansion of controlled immigration to the United Kingdom between 1977 and 2019 can be explained within the outlines of fiscal policy. The answer returned by the data is consistent with this hypothesis. A newly assembled series of the government's own biennial ageing projections predicts non-EU inflows, the margin policy controlled, with a short-run response of 0.8 per cent per projected point of ten-year OADR deterioration and a bias-corrected long-run response of approximately 2.0 per cent, conditional on the Treasury's fiscal state variables and with signs matching the salience model of instrument choice. The relationship is absent where the fiscal account says it should be: for projections of the fiscally cheap total dependency ratio, and for EU inflows, which no British government could ration.

Beyond its substantive claim, the paper contributes a reusable object and a reusable frame: the reconstructed vintage series of official British ageing projections from 1975 onward, extendable through the archival route documented in Online Appendix A to the first projection of 1954, against which any hypothesis about the policy influence of demographic expectations can now be tested; and the treatment of policy instruments as priced by institutional salience, which also predicts the observed preference for legislating pension-age increases decades before they bind.

The interpretation is disciplined by what the design cannot yet deliver. The projections are the government's own and embed migration assumptions; the documentary record of the mechanism, though it now includes the released 2000 drafts in which the instrument-choice reasoning appears and is then removed before publication, covers a single episode; and the sample is one country and forty-three years. The agenda set out above, covering variant projections, forecast-revision shocks at higher frequency, archival text analysis, and cross-country comparison, is the path from a robust and repeatedly falsifiable association to a causal account.

The finding bears on a question larger than migration policy. Democracies facing slow-moving fiscal pressures possess instruments that differ enormously in visibility: taxes announced from a despatch box, pension ages legislated decades before they bind, entry rules amended by statutory instrument on a quiet parliamentary afternoon. The British case suggests that sustained, consequential policy can flow through the least visible instrument precisely because it is least visible, and that the resulting gap between what democracies vote on and what their governments do can persist for decades without any actor needing to conceal anything, because the concealment is supplied by the institutional architecture itself. As the demographic transition arrives across the rich world and the fiscal arithmetic that Britain confronted becomes general, understanding when governments reach for quiet instruments, and what that choice does to democratic accountability, seems among the more consequential questions the political economy of public finance can ask.
