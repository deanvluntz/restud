# 1. Introduction

Across the period studied, the UK experienced a sustained and consistent rise in policy-controlled immigration, with gross non-EU inflows increasing from 73,000 in 1977 to 343,000 by 2019 — across a spectrum of Conservative, Labour, and Coalition governments (Office for National Statistics, 2020). Notably, no major Westminster party ever campaigned on a platform of higher immigration during this period or in living memory; several campaigned explicitly on reducing it. The disconnect between stated policy and realised outcomes became a defining feature of British politics, contributing to the 2016 referendum vote to leave the European Union, in which control of migration was the most commonly cited motivation among Leave voters (Centre for Social Investigation, 2018). Yet existing economic and political models of migration offer no consistent account of why policy-controlled immigration continued to rise across four decades and governments of every stripe.

The existing candidate explanations each fail on timing or on scope. Labour-demand and network accounts explain why migrants wished to come but not why a state with full legal control over the relevant margin repeatedly chose to admit them, against its stated platform, in rising numbers. Explanations based on the ideology of the governing party fail because the rise did not change when the government did: the expansion proceeded under Thatcher and Major before Blair, and accelerated again under governments elected on restrictionist manifestos. The econometric literature's best answer to date is candid about its own limits: the largest and most careful study attributes the bulk of the rise to unexplained "structural change" captured by dummy variables (Mitchell, Pain and Riley, 2011). A satisfactory explanation must identify a pressure that operated continuously across governments, was visible to all of them, and made expanding this particular margin the path of least resistance for each.

This paper proposes and tests a fiscal explanation, motivated by the framework established by the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR). As part of their national economic forecasts, the UK’s fiscal watchdog envisions multiple scenarios based on different projections of future net migration, where larger inflows are associated with greater fiscal sustainability. Their stated assumption is that since inward migrants are traditionally concentrated in working ages, then higher net migration raises the ratio of taxpayers to dependents and materially improves the projected path of public debt. For reference, the 2014 exercise projected that roughly doubling assumed net migration, from 105,000 to 225,000 a year, would leave public sector net debt in 2063–64 at 41 per cent of GDP rather than 83 per cent (OBR, 2014). If past governments have internalised this arithmetic, and if raising migration is politically cheaper than raising taxes or cutting age-related spending, then official demographic forecasts should predict the migration flows that policy controls. This possibility is strengthened with an understanding of the OBR’s history, elaborated upon in Section 2.1. In short, the authority inherited many features from its predecessor in the Treasury, including arguably an institutionalised ideology with far reach in the British state.

The fiscal logic has a second premise, without which arithmetic alone explains nothing: the instrument must be politically cheaper than its alternatives. Raising taxes or cutting age-related spending requires announcement in the Budget, the most scrutinised event in the British political calendar, legislated through a Finance Bill whose defeat is treated as a matter of confidence. Migration rules, by contrast, are amended by statutory instrument, without debate or vote. A government confronting a projected deterioration in the ratio of pensioners to workers therefore faces three margins of adjustment priced very differently by its institutions, and the cheapest is the quietest. The body of the paper develops this instrument-choice logic formally and shows that Britain's specific institutional architecture (a doctrinally continuous Treasury with authority over every department, an executive integrated with the legislature, and a delegated rule-making channel for entry policy) supplied the conditions for the quiet margin to be used.

The hypothesis I test is therefore specific: that projected deteriorations in the OADR, as published in the government's own biennial NPPs, predict subsequent inflows of non-EU migrants, conditional on the fiscal variables that the institutional literature identifies as the Treasury's decision inputs. The choice of non-EU flows as the outcome is central to the design. Under EU free movement, European inflows responded to market conditions and were beyond the reach of the Home Office; non-EU entry, by contrast, was rationed throughout the period by a discretionary work-permit regime and, from 2008, a points-based system. If demographic forecasts shape policy, their imprint should appear on the controlled margin and not on the uncontrolled one. This contrast supplies the paper's principal falsification test.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it assembles a new annual panel of official demographic forecasts: I reconstruct the projected old-age dependency ratio, at horizons of one to ten years, from every National Population Projection vintage between the 1975-based projection and the 2018-based (all of which the estimation sample requires: the 1975-based is in force for publication-dated 1977). Most survive only in un-indexed scans on the National Archives; the access route recovered in the process reaches back to the first, 1954-based, projection, so the full history is available to later work. To my knowledge no previous study has compiled the UK's projection vintages into a usable time series, and no study of any country has tested whether such projections predict policy-controlled migration. Second, the paper provides a political-economy rationale for the reduced form. I set out a stylised model in which a fiscally dominant finance ministry allocates adjustment across three instruments (taxes, borrowing, and controlled migration) in proportion to their political salience. Britain's institutions generate the stark salience asymmetry described above, and the model delivers the estimating equation's signs as ex ante predictions rather than ex post rationalisations. Third, the paper offers a documented alternative to the leading existing explanation of the UK's migration expansion. Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011) attribute around three-quarters of the rise in net immigration in the decade to 2009 to unexplained structural change, proxied by dummy variables and attributed to the policy disposition of the post-1997 Labour government. I show that the timing of non-EU inflows tracks the projected ten-year deterioration in the OADR across the whole period 1977–2019, including under the Conservative administrations of the 1980s and 1990s, which a Blair-specific ideological account cannot explain.

The empirical framework is an autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) regression of log non-EU inflows (scaled by the lagged working-age population) on the projected ten-year change in the OADR and a parsimonious set of fiscal state variables: the lagged tax take per working-age person, the lagged annual log change in the debt-to-GDP ratio, and the lagged unemployment rate. The central estimate implies that a one-point increase in the projected ten-year OADR deterioration is associated with approximately 0.8 per cent higher non-EU inflows on impact and, once the finite-sample attenuation of the autoregressive coefficient is corrected, approximately 2.0 per cent in the long run. The uncorrected least-squares estimate, recorded for comparability, is 1.5 per cent; the correction runs in the conservative direction throughout. The coefficient is stable across horizons, functional forms, sample start dates, and measurement bases. Two falsification exercises support a fiscal-policy interpretation. Projections of the *total* dependency ratio, which include children, whose fiscal cost is a fraction of that of pensioners, have no predictive power. And the same specification estimated on *EU* inflows, which policy could not ration, yields an OADR coefficient indistinguishable from zero.

The paper is related to three literatures. The first is the literature on the determinants of international migration. In the canonical selection framework (Borjas, 1987), migration responds to the income differential between destination and origin net of moving costs; Hatton and Williamson (2002) document the empirical qualifications: emigration from the poorest countries is constrained by the cost of moving, networks of previous migrants lower that cost and propagate flows, and demographic pressure in source countries (a large youth share) raises out-migration. Two findings from this literature frame the present exercise. Hatton and Williamson note that destination-country *policy* is the binding constraint that most quantitative models omit, evidenced by queues and irregular entry wherever pressure exceeds permitted volumes; and Mayda (2007), integrating policy regimes into a bilateral panel, finds that restrictions create an asymmetry in which origin-country push factors lose explanatory power while destination-country conditions retain it. That is exactly the environment in which the determinants of the *policy* become the determinants of the flow. On the destination side, Storesletten (2000) establishes the fiscal arithmetic this paper takes as its premise: in a calibrated overlapping-generations model, immigration policy alone can in principle sustain fiscal policy through population ageing, provided entrants are of working age and sufficiently skilled; the United Nations (2000) bounds the same arithmetic from the other side, finding the inflows required to hold potential support ratios at their existing levels implausibly large. That is a demonstration of feasibility, and of its limit, not a test of use. Strikingly, the source-country demographic result still has no destination-side empirical counterpart: the possibility that *ageing destinations* pull migrants through deliberate policy has not been tested. That inverse is this paper's subject.

Within the UK-specific branch, Mitchell and Pain (2003) advocate the ARDL approach adopted here, document the persistence of flows and the measurement error in stock estimates, and find relative incomes and trade links matter; Hatton (2005) finds a large role for the existing migrant stock and reports that unemployment affects migration in changes rather than levels; Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011), using Bayesian model averaging, conclude that around three-quarters of the rise in net immigration in the decade to 2009 is structural change "attributed to UK and foreign immigration policy", captured by dummy variables and interpreted through the policy disposition of the post-1997 government. This paper supplies the policy determinant those studies proxy with dummies, and offers evidence that it operated well before 1997. Second, the paper draws on the institutional literature on British fiscal governance (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974; Hood, King, McLean and Piotrowska, 2023, separated by half a century yet in substantial agreement) to specify which variables enter the Treasury's information set, and on critiques of the OBR's independence (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021) to argue that the projection-to-policy channel predates the OBR's creation in 2010. Third, the paper connects to the political economy of policy instrument choice and salience: the mechanism proposed here is an application, in a fiscal setting, of the general principle that governments shift adjustment toward instruments with low public visibility.

One clarification of scope. The argument is not a claim of coordinated concealment, still less of intent beyond fiscal management, but a claim about incentives. Each government faced the same constraint set: rising projected age-related spending, a politically expensive tax instrument, a constrained borrowing margin, and a migration instrument adjustable at near-zero parliamentary cost. The hypothesis is that each, independently, found the same margin cheapest. No coordination across governments is required for the policy rule to appear stable, only a stable constraint set. The distinction matters because the observation that migration rose without electoral mandate has been appropriated by conspiratorial narratives; the mechanism proposed here is, by contrast, ordinary public finance conducted through the instrument that ordinary politics made cheapest, and its evidential standing rests on tests it could have failed.

Three limitations should be stated at the outset, and the empirical strategy in Section 5 addresses each as far as the data allow. First, official population projections embed assumptions about future migration, so a projection is not mechanically exogenous to the outcome; I argue, and exploit the demographic arithmetic to show, that the ten-year-ahead old-age population is almost entirely determined by cohorts already resident, which limits the contamination; Section 7 shows the estimates are unchanged when the projections are rebuilt under zero net migration, closing the concern directly. Second, the annual sample is short (43 observations), which constrains the number of regressors, limits the power of unit-root and cointegration tests, and biases the coefficient on the lagged dependent variable downward in small samples. Third, the evidence for the mechanism is institutional and statistical rather than documentary for most of the sample: no Budget speech states that migration policy was set with reference to dependency-ratio projections, and the instrument-choice logic developed below explains precisely why such statements would not be made. The important exception is the turn-of-the-millennium episode, for which released internal drafts document the reasoning directly, and document its removal before publication (Section 2.5). Section 8 sets out the archival and quasi-experimental extensions (projection revisions as information shocks, quarterly visa grants, and text analysis of Budget documents) that would convert the association documented here into a fully identified causal claim.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the institutional setting; Section 3 the model; Section 4 the data, including the reconstruction of the projection series; Section 5 the empirical strategy and identification threats. Section 6 reports the main results and Section 7 the robustness and falsification tests; Section 8 discusses the post-2019 episode and the agenda, and Section 9 concludes this paper.

# 2. Institutional Setting

## 2.1 The Treasury and the fiscal constitution

The plausibility of the mechanism tested in this paper rests on two institutional claims: that a single actor, His Majesty's Treasury, has both the incentive and the authority to treat migration as an instrument of fiscal policy, and that the instrument could be adjusted at low political cost. This subsection documents the first claim; the next documents the second.

The Treasury occupies a position without parallel among the finance ministries of comparable economies. It centralises within a single department powers which other governments distribute across several: budgeting, tax policy, macroeconomic policy, and the financing of the state. It has even been said that the Treasury’s authority over the latter is “perhaps the one aspect of twenty-first-century government that a medieval monarch would recognise” (Hood et al., 2023). The nonpartisan Institute for Government describes the modern department's power as exceeding "that of any other part of government apart from 10 Downing Street," with a "pervasive" veto and a unique capacity "to initiate and force through policies" (Institute for Government, 2024b).

Two features of this authority matter for the argument. The first is continuity of preferences across elected governments. The department maintains a stable institutional doctrine widely known as the "Treasury view" - a phrase coined by Churchill from his time as Chancellor, announcing the opposition of his department to the new policies of Keynesianism adopted by the rival Liberal Party (Hansard, 1929). The label struck a chord for being able to capture a more general characterisation of fiscal conservatism which had already been associated with the department for decades. Such ideas can be traced back to Gladstone, who made a number of reforms with the primary aim of debt reduction, such as embedding Treasury officials as Principal Finance Officers within every department to closely monitor expenditure. The persistence of his imprint was already remarked decades following his departure: "The Treasury, under Gladstonian influence, is imbued with a dreadful spirit of economy" (Higgs, 1924). Heclo and Wildavsky (1974), interviewing Treasury officials in the early 1970s, and Hood et al. (2023), writing fifty years later, describe substantially the same institution; the latter document that the "negotiated discretion" characterising Treasury spending control from the 1970s persisted into the 2010s. This continuity is what permits a single policy rule to be estimated over a forty-three-year period spanning eight Prime Ministers.

Heclo and Wildavsky's officials put the operative principle plainly: the Treasury's business is to “save money, not to spend it”; and above all, its chief interest is "to save themselves the unpopularity of having to put up taxes." That formulation deserves emphasis, because it is the behavioural assumption of the model below stated verbatim by the institution's own members: the department's objective is fiscal adequacy achieved at minimum political cost, and the ranking of instruments by political cost is therefore decision-relevant. On process, the study establishes that annual budgeting took the previous year's estimates as baseline, incorporated internal forecasts of growth, inflation, and employment (many deliberately unpublished), and examined proposals over five-year horizons. Those facts anchor the empirical information set of Section 4: lagged fiscal aggregates, the lagged labour-market position, and official forecasts. The balance of payments, which the 1974 study lists alongside them, is excluded below, because the collapse of Bretton Woods and the float of sterling had removed it from the centre of policy by the start of the sample.

The second feature is the Treasury's demonstrated willingness to pursue its doctrine independently of, and occasionally against, the elected government of the day. This idea has gained notoriety in recent years following the short-lived Truss government, after the Conservative PM blamed such Treasury orthodoxy for the failure of her administration. However, the clearest case study can be found in the circumstances leading up to the International Monetary Fund’s bailout of the UK in 1976. A crisis in sterling had left the Labour Cabinet split and unable to decide on a unanimous response. PM Callaghan asked the Treasury to explore the conditions of a possible deal with the IMF, which later materialised with the agreement of the largest loan in the IMF’s history. The deal imposed strict measures of austerity due to Britain’s large debts – which were later found to have been grossly exaggerated, in false documents submitted to the IMF by Treasury officials. Edmund Dell, the authority responsible in his capacity as the Treasury’s Paymaster General, would later go on to admit and defend his actions in print; arguing that the exaggerated figures were necessary for the IMF to impose harsher cuts which he and others in the Treasury believed were necessary – despite contradicting the platform upon which the Callaghan government had been elected (Dell, 1991; López, 2014). The episode is offered not as typical but as an existence proof: the department could and did steer major policy outcomes through its control of forecasts and figures, without public announcement, across the heads of elected ministers. A milder version of that disposition, policy shaped by the fiscal centre's doctrine through instruments below the threshold of public debate, is all the mechanism of this paper requires.

The Office for Budget Responsibility, created in 2010 as part of the post-crisis fiscal reforms, inherited the Treasury's forecasting functions and is frequently described as independent. The description warrants qualification. At creation the OBR had a professional staff of fewer than twenty, a scale at which its statutory duties are dischargeable only in close cooperation with the Treasury from which it was staffed (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021); and it operates under the Charter for Budget Responsibility, a document the Treasury writes and updates, which defines the very targets against which the OBR assesses the government, an arrangement under which, as critics note, the examined party sets the marking scheme. None of this is offered to disparage the OBR's forecasting; it is offered to establish continuity. The analytical machinery linking demography to fiscal sustainability predates the OBR: it existed within the Treasury and the Government Actuary's Department throughout the sample period. The OBR's published statements about migration and debt, including the projection variants that motivate this paper, should therefore be read not as a post-2010 discovery but as the publication of arithmetic that the fiscal centre had long performed internally, and the empirical analysis accordingly treats the whole period 1977–2019, not merely its final decade, as governed by one informed fiscal centre.

## 2.2 The salience asymmetry: Budget Day and the Immigration Rules

The Treasury's annual fiscal programme is announced in the Budget, an event whose political salience is difficult to overstate. The Chancellor's headline announcements are the objects of weeks of gossip before being delivered to a full House, and then scrutinised line-by-line by the opposition, the press and, since 2010, the OBR. Those measures must be enacted through a Finance Bill, and the defeat of a Finance Bill is by convention treated as a matter of confidence capable of bringing down the government. Heclo and Wildavsky (1974) describe Budget Days as "great political occasions" on which "the pressures on a Chancellor are intense." The fiscal cost of an ageing population must, if met through taxation or spending retrenchment, be announced on this stage and voted upon.

The Budget's salience is not incidental but constitutive: the event exists to concentrate accountability for fiscal choices at a single, maximally visible moment, and a century and a half of accumulated theatre has made it the one fiscal procedure the general public reliably pays attention to (UK Parliament, 2026). Its measures are trailed and leaked for weeks beforehand, the speech is a fixture of the broadcast calendar, and the accompanying documentation is dissected by the opposition, the Institute for Fiscal Studies and the press within hours. The consequences of mispricing the politics of a Budget are demonstrated at intervals throughout modern British history, most recently in 2022, when a fiscal statement delivered without the customary OBR assessment repriced government debt within days and ended the Truss premiership within weeks. A Chancellor contemplating the fiscal arithmetic of ageing therefore faces a specific and personal exposure: the tax and spending responses to a deteriorating dependency ratio must be announced by him, on that stage, and voted on by his colleagues.

Migration policy faces no comparable exposure. The Immigration Act 1971 delegated the detail of entry policy to the Immigration Rules, which the Home Secretary amends by laying a Statement of Changes before Parliament. These statements are a species of secondary legislation: they require no debate, no vote, and no Commons majority, and in practice are laid multiple times per year (Home Office, 2026). The shift of British law-making from primary to secondary legislation accelerated over precisely the period studied here. The annual count of UK statutory instruments held near two thousand through the 1970s and 1980s, stepped up sharply from around 1990 and remained above three thousand for the following two decades, while the number of Acts of Parliament roughly halved over the same span (House of Commons Library, 2019; Hansard Society, 2014). The timing matters. The escalation belongs to no single administration. It begins at the close of the Thatcher government, continues under Major and is sustained through the Blair and Brown years, exhibiting the same cross-party persistence as the migration series itself, and indicating a shift in the machinery of government rather than a partisan programme. It also runs alongside rather than after the migration expansion: the quiet instrument became progressively cheaper to use over exactly the decades in which the fiscal pressure modelled here was building. A government that wished to expand the inflow of taxpayers could therefore do so through instruments whose visibility, relative to a Budget measure of equivalent fiscal value, was close to zero.

Within the executive, coordination between the fiscal centre and the Home Office requires no public act, because the Westminster system's explicit rejection of the separation of powers integrates the two. The Cabinet is drawn entirely from the legislature; its members are bound by collective responsibility, under which a minister who publicly dissents from an agreed position resigns or is dismissed, so that whatever position the centre carries in Cabinet is defended unanimously outside it. The Treasury sets every department's budget, including the Home Office's, the power of the purse operating inside the executive rather than between branches; and the Chancellor is by convention the second figure of the government, reflected in its alternative designation as Second Lord of the Treasury following the Prime Minister – a moniker which also serves to reflect the primacy of the Treasury in government. A departmental minister weighing a Treasury preference on entry policy therefore faces the department that funds her, a Cabinet doctrine that forecloses public disagreement, and a rule-making instrument that requires no parliamentary test of the outcome. The claim advanced here is not that migration targets were written down in Budget documents (they were not, and Section 3 explains why the logic of instrument choice predicts their absence) but that the institutional machinery for using migration as a fiscal instrument existed, was centralised, and operated below the threshold of parliamentary and public scrutiny throughout the sample period.

## 2.3 Migration policy instruments, 1977–2019

The modern architecture of control has an imperial prehistory. Work permits regulating entry from outside the Empire date from the aftermath of the First World War (Bauer and Salt, 2020); imperial subjects held a nominal right of entry, but movement from the non-white colonies was policed at the point of departure, so the right was exercised principally by the settler dominions (Hatton and Wheatley Price, 1999). Decolonisation converted the nominal right into a real one, and the response was legislative: successive Commonwealth Immigrants Acts in the 1960s restricted entry, and the Immigration Act 1971 ended Commonwealth free movement two years before EEC accession created its European replacement. That Act is the hinge of the institutional story told here: the same statute that closed the imperial regime created the delegated rule-making machinery (Section 2.2) through which all subsequent non-European entry policy has been made.

Throughout the sample period, then, the United Kingdom, as an EEC and later EU member, could not restrict inflows from other member states. Control was exercised exclusively over the non-EU margin, through two successive regimes.

Under the work-permit system, in force until 2008, a non-EU national required an offer of employment from a British employer, who was in turn required to demonstrate, under the Resident Labour Market Test, that the vacancy could not be filled domestically; a separate entry visa could be refused even after a permit was granted. The regime was intricate: by the mid-2000s more than eighty distinct routes existed by which a non-EEA national could enter for work, study, or training (Home Office, 2006, p. 7), and the Rules have since grown to some 375,000 words (Guardian, 2018). The complexity is analytically useful. Absent published quotas, the state's dual power of approval, over the permit and over the visa, gave it continuous discretionary control over volumes, exercised through administrative practice rather than legislation. It also dictates the paper's measurement strategy, because policy in this regime cannot be quantified as a single index: a given Statement of Changes may loosen conditions for some origins and categories while tightening others; historical work-permit data are, in the assessment of the specialists who have studied them, too fragmentary for statistical use (Bauer and Salt, 2020); and no stringency series spans the period. The empirical work therefore measures policy by its outcomes, realised inflows on the controlled margin, under the maintained assumption that the instrument was effective. That assumption is defensible precisely because the discretion was dual and continuous, and the post-Brexit episode, in which rule changes moved flows by hundreds of thousands within two years, illustrates the instrument's potency.

The permit lever's demographic sensitivity, and the preference for operating it quietly, are both visible at the administrative level. Commenting on a draft careers booklet in 1990, the Employment Department's Overseas Labour Section recorded that "because of demographic changes the number of work permits being granted is rising sharply", with approval rates near 75 per cent under the main scheme and 90 per cent for trainees, while asking that "actual figures" be "omitted as they can be misleading" (The National Archives, LAB 109/66). The demography in question was the late-1980s shortfall of young labour-market entrants rather than old-age dependency; what the file documents is the permit lever moving with demographic pressure, and an official preference against publicising the numbers, a decade before the drafting record of Section 2.5.

The points-based system introduced from 2008 consolidated the work-permit routes into five tiers: highly skilled entrants without a job offer (Tier 1, later replaced by talent visas), sponsored skilled workers (Tier 2, the successor to the core work-permit route), shortage-occupation workers (Tier 3, later folded into Tier 2 with reduced salary thresholds), students (Tier 4) and temporary and youth-mobility entrants (Tier 5). Entry was allocated by points scored on salary, qualifications, language and, notably for this paper, age: applicants aged 27 or under received 20 points toward the threshold, with the credit declining thereafter to 5 points and cutting off at 31 (Home Office, 2006). The explicit pricing of youth into the entry rule is the closest the policy regime comes to a published statement that the demographic structure of inflows was a policy objective. The initial system imposed no aggregate cap, delegating volumes to labour demand subject to government-chosen thresholds that remained adjustable by statutory instrument. The delegation had a political economy of its own: by removing the state's case-by-case approval role, the reform shifted apparent responsibility for volumes from the government to the labour market, a visibility-reducing design choice with a familial resemblance to Labour’s own delegation of monetary policy to an independent central bank a decade earlier, and one that sits naturally within the salience framework of the next section.

Two further features of the regime bear on interpretation. First, the state's influence over non-EU inflows extended beyond rule-setting: the National Health Service, the country's largest employer, recruits internationally at scale, and non-British nationals, three-quarters of them from outside the EU, constitute roughly a fifth of its workforce (House of Commons Library, 2025). Second, the dependent variable in this paper aggregates all long-term non-EU inflows, including students and family migration, rather than work-visa entries alone. This is deliberate: from a fiscal standpoint what matters is the addition of working-age residents, whatever their entry category, and the entry criteria for every category were policy-controlled. Students, the category most often queried, fit the fiscal logic rather than strain it: they enter with work rights, tuition payments, and consumption spending, they are the youngest entrants, and the official estimates of the period recorded student departures well below arrivals. What aggregation forecloses is only the estimation of category-level responses, taken up in Section 8.

The post-2019 regime change, in which the points-based system was extended to EU nationals and several of its parameters were liberalised (Migration Observatory, 2021), falls outside the estimation sample and is used in Section 8 as an out-of-sample check.

## 2.4 The information set: National Population Projections

The forecasts at the centre of the analysis are the National Population Projections, produced biennially since 1954, by the Government Actuary's Department (GAD) until 2006 and by the Office for National Statistics (ONS) thereafter. Each vintage projects the population by single year of age and sex for decades ahead, on stated assumptions about fertility, mortality, and net migration, and reports the population split into young dependents, the working-age population, and those of pension age, together with the implied dependency ratios for every projected year. The measure this paper uses is the old-age dependency ratio: the number of persons of state pension age per thousand persons of working age. Before the equalisation legislated in the Pensions Act 1995 and phased in over 2010–2019 the state pension age was 60 for women and 65 for men, so the UK measure differs by construction from the international 65-plus convention; all projections used here apply the UK definition consistently within each vintage. The OADR, rather than the total dependency ratio, is the headline ageing measure of the bulletins the ONS publishes alongside each vintage. That editorial choice by the projections' own producers anticipates this paper's weighting, and for fiscal purposes the emphasis is well placed. Those aged 65 and over were projected to absorb some 42 per cent of UK health spending by 2021/22, health being the largest single component of public spending, and they draw the state pension besides; education, the principal fiscal cost of children, takes roughly nine per cent of public spending (Full Fact, 2018; Institute for Fiscal Studies, 2024). An anticipated rise in the OADR is, to a close approximation, an anticipated rise in mandatory spending per worker.

The demographic history the projections track gives them their forecasting power. Britain's post-war baby boom arrived in two waves, an immediate post-war surge and a second, larger wave over roughly 1955–65. At the beginning of the period studied, it was already the consensus among demographers that fertility rates would not return to those levels, and therefore future generations would be outnumbered by the baby boomers (Jones, 1980). Each wave traverses the age distribution and reaches pension age on a schedule known decades ahead, the first from around 2010 and the second through the 2020s. This is why old-age dependency is, uniquely among fiscal pressures, forecastable: the people who will be pensioners in ten years are alive, resident, and counted today, so at that horizon the projection error reduces essentially to mortality surprises. A low dependency ratio is a loan rather than a gift. The demographic dividend (Farid and Mostari, 2022) is repaid, with interest, when the large cohorts retire, and the projections priced that repayment for any government that cared to look.

For the government of the day, the NPP is not an external publication but an internal product: for the programme's first five decades it was produced by GAD, a department of government, on assumptions agreed with the Treasury. GAD itself grew out of the actuarial machinery of national insurance after the First World War: established as a separate department in 1919, it assumed the actuarial oversight of the contributory state pension scheme from 1925. Its own account to the Treasury Committee leaves no doubt where it stood: created "as a Chancellor's department", its objectives were set under a Public Service Agreement approved by the Economic Secretary to the Treasury, its spending plans by the Chief Secretary, its performance reported to the Treasury quarterly, and the biennial population projections were the one service exempt from its charging regime, funded from its own Vote as a service to all departments (House of Commons Treasury Committee, 2001, paras 1.1, 4.8, 8.1–8.6). When the advice mattered most the Treasury drew it closer still: the Deputy Government Actuary of the mid-1980s recalls the Chancellor insisting that GAD advise from inside the Treasury rather than the social security department during the 1985–86 pension reform, and spending most of the run-up to the Bill there (Pemberton, 2018). Nor was its arithmetic beyond contest: Hemming and Kay (1982) showed that the Government Actuary's memorandum on the 1978 earnings-related pension scheme, which ran only to 2008–09, masked the scheme's cost behind demographic trends favourable within that horizon and reversed beyond it, an early instance of official projections being read for what their horizon left out. The NPP is a direct measurement of the fiscal centre's own demographic expectations, precisely the information set that the model of the next section requires. Toward the end of the sample the incorporation of these projections into Budget-time analysis ceased to be inferential and became statutory. The Code for Fiscal Stability (HM Treasury, 1998) required the government to publish illustrative long-term fiscal projections within the Budget documents, over a horizon of "no less than 10 years into the future" (HM Treasury, 1999, p. 87). The first such set appeared as Annex A of the 1999 Economic and Fiscal Strategy Report, which opens by observing that around one in five people in the UK would be aged over 65 by 2026 against one in seven in 1998 (p. 87), quantifies the additional spending demanded by demography (p. 89), and notes that the average annual cost of providing health care to a person over 65 is "over 3½ times the average for the remainder of the population" (p. 90). The statutory minimum horizon is worth noting for what follows: it is the horizon at which the projection variable of this paper is measured, and Section 6.1 reports that it is also the horizon that fits best. From 2002 a dedicated Treasury long-term unit produced the stand-alone Long-term Public Finance Report, work that, by the account of one of its own economists, led the Treasury to work more closely with the Home Office on the economics of migration (Eich, 2008).

That annex also illustrates the instrument problem at the centre of this paper. Its section on long-term policy names the responses the government was prepared to advertise: raising productivity, raising labour-market participation, welfare reform, private retirement saving, and, first among them, the legislated increase in the female state pension age from 60 to 65, which "will play a key role in reducing long-term spending pressures" (p. 90). Migration appears nowhere among the remedies. It is mentioned once, among the uncertainties that might produce "a different population profile from that currently expected" (p. 90). The published record thus confirms that the fiscal centre was analysing ageing at Budget time, on a ten-year-plus horizon, and confirms the salience ranking of the model: the pension-age instrument, legislated fifteen years before it began to bind, is named and claimed, while the instrument adjustable by statutory instrument is not.

## 2.5 Documentary evidence: the 2000 Cabinet Office analysis

For one episode, the turn-of-the-millennium expansion at the centre of the structural-change debate, the mechanism can be documented directly, in a drafting record released under the Freedom of Information Act (Cabinet Office, 2010). In 2000 the Cabinet Office Performance and Innovation Unit and the Home Office jointly prepared the government's first public, comprehensive economic analysis of migration, published in January 2001 (Glover et al., 2001). The released drafts permit a comparison of what the government's analysts wrote internally with what the government published.

The preliminary draft of July 2000, classified RESTRICTED–POLICY, reasons precisely in the terms of Section 3. It quantifies the demographic constraint through the zero-migration counterfactual: without net migration, the working-age population would fall by two million over twenty-five years while the over-65 population rose by three million, taking the support ratio from above four to below three. It quantifies the instrument: net migration of roughly 120,000 per year to hold the working-age population constant, and of nearly a million per year to stabilise the support ratio. It states the instrument menu: ageing "will have to be addressed: presumably by some combination of net migration, an increase in the average working life, changes to entitlements, increases in contributions (either tax-based or private), and increases in productivity," adding that "the alternative to migration may often be tax increases, which would also have an adverse economic effect." And it draws the political-economy conclusion: these pressures would recast immigrants "less like a threat and more as a way to avoid the more unpleasant alternatives of tax increases, benefit cuts, and economic stagnation."

None of this survived to publication. The published paper retains the demographic description, the support ratio falling from more than four to less than three, but the quantified migration requirements, the instrument menu, the comparison with tax increases, and the "unpleasant alternatives" passage are all absent; the released November 2000 draft shows the excision had already occurred by that point, dating it to the autumn in which ministers first announced the "managed migration" reframing of policy. The episode thus documents both halves of the model: the reasoning, migration as the fiscally equivalent and politically cheaper substitute for taxation, articulated inside government at the moment the expansion began; and the salience management, observable as the difference between draft and publication. Two limits on the inference should be noted at once: the record covers a single episode under one government; and although the project was commissioned through the Chancellor's cabinet committee and drew on unpublished Treasury research, the drafting hands are the Cabinet Office's and the Home Office's, so the reasoning survives in their voice rather than the Treasury's own, whose parallel paper trail remains to be recovered from the archives (Section 8).

The 2000 record is the clearest instance of the mechanism's paper trail, but not the earliest evidence that the fiscal centre read the projection machinery. The Treasury's 1984 Green Paper on long-term expenditure tabulated the OPCS projections and costed the pensioner numbers they implied, naming demography first among the pressures on future spending (HM Treasury, 1984); the Cabinet Office brief on its drafting warned of the 'political risks' of illustrating that arithmetic in public; and by November 1996 the Treasury's own board had been briefed that the old-age dependency ratio would nearly double over fifty years (The National Archives, PREM 19/1190; T 733). An OECD ministerial note circulated to Downing Street in 1984 had already remarked on governments' 'expanding use of off-budget measures' whose true costs, 'though less apparent, are not necessarily less' -- the selection principle, stated by contemporaries, that Section 3 formalises. None of these documents names migration as the response; what they establish is receipt, fiscal reading, and an explicit official sensitivity to the salience of the visible alternatives.

## 2.6 The institutional argument in summary

The sections above establish three premises on which the model and the empirical design rest. First, a single, doctrinally continuous fiscal centre, the Treasury, possessed throughout 1977–2019 both the information (internally produced demographic projections and fiscal forecasts) and the authority (budgetary control over every department, seniority within an integrated executive) to treat migration as an instrument of fiscal policy. Second, the political price of the alternative instruments was institutionally fixed and high: taxes and spending must be adjusted in the Budget, under maximal scrutiny and a confidence-vote convention, while migration rules were adjustable by statutory instrument at negligible parliamentary cost, a salience asymmetry built into the machinery of government rather than chosen by any administration. Third, the instrument was effective and continuously available: through dual work-permit approvals and adjustable points thresholds the state exercised standing discretion over non-EU entry, while EU entry remained beyond its control, supplying both the treated and the placebo margins the empirical work requires.

Evidence framing His Majesty's Treasury as the principal agent is indirect, but well-founded. Its authority over both fiscal policy and departmental funding gives it the means and motive to influence other areas of government, including immigration policy (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974; Hood, King, McLean and Piotrowska, 2023). Documentary evidence links the Treasury's interest in migration policy to the dependency ratio (Cabinet Office, 2010). The fall of the Truss premiership demonstrated that Treasury institutions can outlast any individual government (Wamsley, 2026) – and are therefore likely capable of producing the consistency of results within a single framework across 40+ years of government that my model results suggest. A century of scholarship documents the department's independence and an ideological inclination towards fiscal conservatism and austerity (Higgs, 1924; Institute for Government, 2024a). Furthermore, the Dell affair provides a historical example of the Treasury acting independently to determine policy when those principles were in conflict with a democratic mandate (Dell, 1991; López, 2014). An out-of-sample case study follows this pattern – a 1964 Labour mandate to divide Treasury authority with the creation of a new Department of Economic Affairs was seen as a "power struggle" fought and won by the Treasury, which re-absorbed the DEA within five years (Clifford, 1997). Seen in this light, the continued independence of the Office for Budget Responsibility (which split off from the department as promised by the 2010 Conservative manifesto) can be understood alongside further evidence to characterise the OBR as a continuation of Treasury principles. Indeed, the Charter governing the OBR is dictated by the Treasury and establishes the benchmarks on which their fiscal policy can be judged. The OBR's methodology in this regard suggests an institutional assumption of positive correlation between higher net immigration and fiscal sustainability (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021; OBR, 2014).


# 3. A Stylised Model of Fiscal Instrument Choice

This section sets out a deliberately minimal model whose purpose is to discipline the empirical specification: it delivers the sign and the conditioning set of the estimating equation as ex ante predictions.

Consider an incumbent government that must finance age-related spending each period. Let dₜ denote the old-age dependency ratio and let Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] be the government's own projection of the ratio h years ahead, taken from the current NPP vintage. Mandatory spending per working-age person is g(dₜ), with g′ > 0. The government disposes of three instruments: the tax burden per working-age person τₜ, borrowing bₜ, and controlled (non-EU) migration mₜ, which augments the working-age tax base. The per-period budget identity is

[[EQ]]
τₜ + bₜ = g(dₜ),
[[/EQ]]

and anticipated ageing, Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ > 0, raises the spending requirement the government expects to have to finance within its planning horizon.

Each instrument carries a political cost proportional to its salience. Following the institutional evidence of Section 2, tax changes are maximally salient (they must be announced in the Budget and legislated), borrowing is constrained by bond markets and the Treasury's doctrinal aversion to debt, whereas migration adjustments are minimally salient (proceeding by statutory instrument). Let the period political cost be

[[EQ]]
Cₜ = ½ φ_τ sτ (τₜ − τ̄)² + ½ φₘ sₘ mₜ² + φᵤ uₜ₋₁ mₜ + ½ ψ (mₜ − mₜ₋₁)²,
[[/EQ]]

where sτ ≫ sₘ > 0 are salience weights, τ̄ is the inherited tax burden regarded as politically neutral, uₜ₋₁ is the unemployment rate observed when policy is set (higher unemployment raises the political price of visible foreign inflows), and the final term is an adjustment cost reflecting the administrative and diplomatic frictions of changing entry rules quickly. The government minimises expected discounted political cost subject to the budget identity, a debt limit bₜ ≤ b̄(Bₜ₋₁) that tightens in the inherited debt stock Bₜ₋₁, and the demographic transition law under which migration today lowers the dependency ratio at the projection horizon.

Each element of the cost function has an institutional referent. The salience ranking sτ ≫ sₘ is Section 2.2 in symbols: a pound of fiscal adjustment obtained through the Budget is priced at the full attention of the political system, the same pound obtained through a Statement of Changes at the attention of specialists. The borrowing margin is constrained doctrinally by the Treasury view and externally by market discipline, tightening in the inherited stock, which is why lagged debt, not the flow of borrowing, enters the policy rule. The unemployment term reflects the one circumstance in which the migration instrument's salience rises sharply: visible inflows during visible joblessness. That inflows carry an electoral price at all, so that sₘ is positive rather than zero and the instrument is quiet rather than free, is well established: exploiting the quasi-random allocation of refugees across Danish municipalities, Dustmann, Vasiljeva and Piil Damm (2019) find that local exposure shifts votes toward anti-immigration parties outside the largest cities. The adjustment cost stands in for the administrative machinery of entry policy, through which rule changes propagate over months and years, not instantly.

The first-order condition for migration takes the form

[[EQ]]
φₘ sₘ mₜ + φᵤ uₜ₋₁ + ψ (mₜ − mₜ₋₁) = λₜ · MB(Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ),
[[/EQ]]

equating the marginal political cost of admissions (left side) to their marginal fiscal benefit (right side), where λₜ, the shadow value of fiscal room, rises with the inherited tax burden, through sτ, and with the tightness of the borrowing constraint, through b̄(Bₜ₋₁), and the marginal benefit MB(·) rises with the projected demographic deterioration the inflow offsets. Relief is more valuable the worse the projected position and the more expensive the alternative instruments. Linearising around the inherited position yields a policy rule of the form

[[EQ]]
mₜ = ρ mₜ₋₁ + β₁ (Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ) + β₂ τₜ₋₁ + β₃ Bₜ₋₁ − β₄ uₜ₋₁,
[[/EQ]]

with all coefficients positive. The model thus delivers four predictions. (P1) Controlled migration rises with the projected deterioration of the OADR, because migration is the low-salience margin of fiscal adjustment: β₁ > 0, and β₁ is larger the greater the salience gap sτ/sₘ. (P2) Migration rises with the inherited tax burden, because a high τₜ₋₁ raises the marginal political cost of further tax increases and induces substitution toward the quiet instrument: β₂ > 0. (P3) Migration rises when the borrowing position has been deteriorating, because a tightening constraint closes the intertemporal escape route: β₃ > 0. Empirically the constraint is measured by the recent change in the debt ratio rather than its level (Section 4.3). (P4) Migration falls with lagged unemployment, which raises the political price of inflows: β₄ > 0. The adjustment cost delivers persistence, ρ ∈ (0,1), and motivates the autoregressive term in the empirical specification.

The framework can be read as organising fiscal capacity along two margins. On the *intensive* margin the government extracts more from the existing working-age population, through the tax rate directly, or through policies raising employment and participation, which is why the unemployment and inactivity rates belong in the information set. On the *extensive* margin it enlarges the base itself. Migration is one extensive-margin instrument; the other is redefinition of the base by raising the state pension age, which converts prospective dependents into prospective taxpayers at the stroke of a statute. The model treats the two as substitutes priced by salience, and the sample contains one large realisation of the second instrument: the Pensions Act 1995, which legislated the equalisation of the female pension age from 60 to 65, phased over 2010–2019, enacted fifteen years before it began to bind, a timing profile itself consistent with salience management, and named in the 1999 Budget as a key means of reducing long-term spending pressures (Section 2.4). Because the projections used here incorporate legislated pension-age changes, the estimated migration response is identified net of this alternative channel; a joint model of the two instruments is beyond the present data but is noted in Section 8 as a natural extension.

The model abstracts from much: migrants are homogeneous fiscal contributors rather than heterogeneous workers; wages and prices do not respond to inflows; salience weights are exogenous rather than shaped by media and opposition strategy; and the projection is taken as the government's belief rather than modelled as a signal-extraction problem. Each abstraction isolates the mechanism under test, and each marks a boundary the conclusions must respect: the estimates below identify a policy response to projected ageing, not the welfare consequences or general-equilibrium incidence of that response.

Two remarks connect the model to the institutions. First, the model predicts silence: because the value of the migration instrument derives from its low salience, no optimising government publicises its use, which is why the mechanism must be detected in outcomes rather than announcements, and why the absence of documentary statements is consistent with, rather than evidence against, the hypothesis. This feature is uncomfortable, since it renders the mechanism difficult to falsify from the documentary record alone, and it is precisely why the falsification tests of Section 7, which the reduced form *can* fail, carry the evidential weight. The prediction is also, in one documented instance, borne out literally: the instrument-choice reasoning appears in the internal draft of the government's 2000 migration analysis and is excised from the published version (Section 2.5). Second, the model predicts which projection should matter: the old-age ratio, whose fiscal price is high, and not the total dependency ratio, whose child component is fiscally cheap. This yields the placebo test implemented in Section 7.

## 4.1 Dependent variable: non-EU inflows

Annual gross inflows of long-term migrants (intended stay of one year or more) by origin are taken from the International Passenger Survey (IPS), conducted by the ONS at ports of entry since 1961. Origin is classified by country of last residence, the conventional choice in the UK literature; country of birth and citizenship are also recorded, and the three differ negligibly at the EU/non-EU partition used here. The dependent variable is the log of non-EU inflows per thousand working-age residents, with the working-age denominator lagged one year to prevent current-year inflows from entering both sides of the equation.

The IPS is a voluntary interview survey: ONS surveyors, stationed at UK entry/exit ports, approach travellers at fixed sampling intervals, achieving coverage of roughly 90 per cent of passenger traffic, with a 68 per cent response rate among those approached (ONS, 2023). Its limitations are well documented. Migration status is self-reported intention, not realised duration: a respondent intending a year's stay but leaving after ten months is a long-term migrant in the data, a student who remains after graduation on a work visa is recorded only under the status held at entry, and respondents with reasons to misreport, prospective asylum applicants among them, face no verification. Sampling variance is material for thinly travelled routes, which is why origins are published as regional groupings rather than individual countries.

The decennial Census provides the accuracy benchmark. A stock count cannot supply annual flows, so its role is indirect: the ONS uses each Census to validate and, where necessary, adjust the intervening decade of IPS estimates, and the record of those reconciliations (below) is the best available evidence on the survey's reliability.

Three considerations govern the use of the IPS despite these deficiencies. First, and decisively for this paper's question, the IPS was the basis of the official migration estimates used by the government itself throughout the period (Portes and Forte, 2017): it measures the flows as policymakers perceived them, which is the relevant object when the outcome of interest is a policy response. For the same reason the series is used as published contemporaneously, not as retrospectively revised: the government of 1995 responded to the 1995 figures, not to their 2012 corrections. Second, the alternative series is Long-Term International Migration (LTIM), which adjusts the IPS for visa switchers, asylum flows, and the Irish land border, and which the ONS cautions cannot simply be compared with the raw IPS (ONS, 2015). It begins only in 1991; it is used as a robustness check on the overlapping sample. Third, the decennial Census, against which the ONS benchmarks the IPS, has contradicted it materially only once, when the 2011 Census revealed an undercount of post-2004 EU8 arrivals through regional airports and prompted a methodological expansion, and that discrepancy concerned EU rather than non-EU flows. The maintained assumption is that non-EU measurement error, whatever its level, is not systematically correlated with the regressors; classical error in the dependent variable inflates standard errors without biasing coefficients, which works against finding the results reported below.

The gross-inflow basis (rather than net) is preferred because policy rations entry, not exit: the instruments of Section 2.3 operate on admissions, and emigration, of Britons and of previous migrants alike, responds to decisions outside the policy rule. Scaling by the lagged working-age population expresses the inflow in the units the fiscal mechanism cares about (additions per thousand existing contributors); results for gross inflows unscaled by population are reported as a robustness check, so that no population estimate appears on either side of the equation. All long-term entry categories are aggregated (work, study, family) because every category's entry criteria were policy-controlled and every category adds working-age residents; the category-level responses that aggregation forecloses are taken up in Section 8.

## 4.2 The projection series

The variable of interest is the projected change in the OADR: Δ̂dₜ(h) = Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ, the difference between the OADR projected h years ahead in the most recent NPP vintage and its current value. The baseline horizon is h = 10; horizons of 1, 3, 5 and 7 years, weighted averages across horizons, and projected levels rather than changes are all examined below. A worked example fixes the construction: for 1997, the operative vintage projected an OADR roughly six points higher in 2007 than its 1997 value, so Δ̂d₁₉₉₇(10) ≈ 6.1, at the time the largest projected ten-year deterioration on record, though the vintages of the early 2000s would soon quadruple it. The variable is best understood as the ageing news confronting the Budget's author: how much worse, in the government's own published expectation, the ratio of pensioners to taxpayers will be a decade hence.

Assembling this series required reconstructing the full history of NPP vintages, and the reconstruction is described in Online Appendix A in the detail required for replication. In brief: the ONS website publishes complete projections only back to 2014; earlier vintages exist as scans on a National Archives site that is neither searchable by its own tools nor indexed by search engines, and direct navigation recovers vintages only to 1998. A machine-readable annex to the 2008-based NPP was found to contain direct links to every predecessor publication back to the first 1954-based projection (ONS, 2016b); through those links the projected old-age dependency ratio at each horizon was extracted for every vintage from the 1975-based projection through the 2018-based (GAD and ONS, 1975–2018), the 1977-based being the earliest the estimation sample requires. In years between biennial vintages, the most recent vintage's projections are carried forward, mirroring the information actually available to policymakers; the same convention supplies the annual working-age population denominators. Multi-vintage records of these projections have been assembled before, but from within the statistical service that produced them (Shaw, 2007) or over the recent, publicly navigable vintages; outside government, the pre-1998 record was effectively unobtainable until the retrieval route documented here. To my knowledge this is the first horizon-complete, machine-readable panel of projected dependency ratios by vintage assembled from public sources, and the first use of the projection vintages as an information-set regressor in a model of policy. It may be of independent use to researchers studying the formation and revision of official demographic expectations.

Two timing conventions deserve note. First, each NPP is labelled by its base year but published with a lag of roughly a year; the baseline assigns vintages by base year, on the grounds that GAD was an arm of government and the fiscal centre had access to the projections in advance of publication, an assumption that is defensible for the internal-information argument of Section 2.4 but is confronted directly, as an identification matter, in Section 5.2. Second, the UK definition of the OADR uses the state pension age, which for women rose from 60 toward 65 over 2010–2019 under legislation enacted in 1995; because the reform was legislated fifteen years in advance, it is embedded in the relevant projection vintages, and the projections therefore already incorporate the principal policy change to the ratio's definition within the sample.

## 4.3 Fiscal and macroeconomic controls

The institutional literature reviewed in Section 2 specifies the Treasury's decision inputs: the fiscal aggregates and the labour market position, observed with a lag at Budget time (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974). Accordingly the controls are the previous year's values of the following.

Tax per working-age person, in thousands of constant (2024–25) pounds, constructed by dividing national accounts taxes from the OBR's Historical Public Finances Database (OBR, 2026), deflated with HM Treasury's March 2026 GDP deflators (HM Treasury, 2026), by the working-age population as per the most recent NPP. The per-working-age-head basis, rather than the tax-to-GDP ratio, is the theoretically appropriate measure: the political cost in the model attaches to the burden borne per taxpayer, and taxes on the working-age population, where income tax and National Insurance alone account for around 45 per cent of National Accounts taxes across the sample and, with VAT, around two-thirds, are where the incidence of an ageing population falls. In the model's terms this variable measures how far the intensive margin has already been used, and therefore the marginal political price of using it further.

*The annual log change in public sector net debt as a share of GDP*, from the OBR databank: the trajectory of the debt ratio rather than its level, measuring the rate at which the borrowing margin is closing. The level appears in the stationarity tests but not in the estimating equation.

*The ILO unemployment rate* (ONS, 2026, series LF2Q), the Budget-time reading of the labour market, which enters the model both as a political cost of visible inflows and, in conventional migration economics, as a demand-side determinant.

Candidate variables tested and excluded on statistical grounds during specification development (the economic inactivity rate, real GDP and productivity growth, ten-year and three-month gilt yields (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 2025), and the Budget-time official growth forecast for the year, digitised from HM Treasury Budget reports via the historical fiscal database of the Economic Statistics Centre of Excellence, 2026) are documented in Section 6.1, with the selection protocol stated in Section 5.3. The growth forecasts deserve a note: they are the only official forecasts published consistently alongside Budgets over the sample, but their published timelines shift across eras, and the unemployment forecasts of the earlier decades were verbal rather than numerical, a data limitation that the archival agenda of Section 8 could relax.

## 4.4 The absence of an instrument

No credible instrumental variable for UK migration inflows exists in the literature. The only proposed proxy, National Insurance registrations (Portes and Forte, 2017), fails at the first requirement of relevance, on the ONS's own assessment: registrations do not distinguish short-term from long-term stays and cannot net out emigration (ONS, 2016a). The institutional setting compounds the problem: enforcement of the right-to-work regime attached to National Insurance has long been weak by European standards, so the mapping from registrations to resident inflows is loose in both directions. The identification strategy therefore rests on timing, predetermined regressors, and falsification, as set out next, rather than on exclusion restrictions.
