# 1. Introduction

Across the period studied, the UK experienced a sustained and consistent rise in policy-controlled immigration, with gross non-EU inflows increasing from 73,000 in 1977 to 343,000 by 2019 — across a spectrum of Conservative, Labour, and Coalition governments (Office for National Statistics, 2020). Notably, no major Westminster party ever campaigned on a platform of higher immigration during this period or in living memory; several campaigned explicitly on reducing it. The disconnect between stated policy and realised outcomes became a defining feature of British politics, contributing to the 2016 referendum vote to leave the European Union, in which control of migration was the most commonly cited motivation among Leave voters (Centre for Social Investigation, 2018). Yet existing economic and political models of migration offer no consistent account of why policy-controlled immigration continued to rise across four decades and governments of every stripe.

This paper proposes and tests a fiscal explanation: that successive governments used the least visible instrument available to them, controlled immigration, to offset the fiscal cost of projected population ageing. The empirical framework is an autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) regression of log non-EU inflows, scaled by the lagged working-age population, on the projected ten-year change in the OADR and the lagged tax take per working-age person, change in the debt-to-GDP ratio, and unemployment rate. A one-point increase in the projected deterioration is associated with approximately 0.8 per cent higher inflows on impact and, once the finite-sample attenuation of the autoregressive coefficient is corrected, approximately 2.0 per cent in the long run (1.5 per cent uncorrected), stable across horizons, functional forms, start dates, and measurement bases. Projections of the *total* dependency ratio, whose child component is fiscally cheap, have no predictive power, and the same specification on *EU* inflows, which policy could not ration, yields a coefficient indistinguishable from zero. Figure 1 shows the pattern the estimates formalise: non-EU inflows rise when the official projections foresee faster ageing, under governments of both parties and well before 1997.

The existing candidate explanations each fail on timing or on scope. Labour-demand and network accounts explain why migrants wished to come but not why a state with full legal control over the margin repeatedly chose to admit them, against its stated platform, in rising numbers; explanations based on the ideology of the governing party fail because the rise did not change when the government did, proceeding under Thatcher and Major before Blair and accelerating again under governments elected on restrictionist manifestos. The econometric literature's best answer attributes the bulk of the rise to unexplained “structural change” captured by dummy variables (Mitchell, Pain and Riley, 2011). A satisfactory explanation must identify a pressure that operated continuously across governments, was visible to all of them, and made expanding this margin the path of least resistance for each.

The explanation is motivated by the Office for Budget Responsibility's practice of projecting the public finances under alternative migration assumptions. Because migrants are concentrated in working ages, larger inflows raise the ratio of taxpayers to dependents: the 2014 exercise projected that roughly doubling assumed net migration, from 105,000 to 225,000 a year, would leave public sector net debt in 2063–64 at 41 per cent of GDP rather than 83 (OBR, 2014). If past governments internalised this arithmetic, and if raising migration was politically cheaper than raising taxes or cutting age-related spending, official demographic forecasts should predict the flows policy controls. Section 2.1 shows the OBR inherited the arithmetic from the Treasury.

The fiscal logic has a second premise: the instrument must be politically cheaper than its alternatives. Raising taxes or cutting age-related spending requires announcement in the Budget and legislation through a Finance Bill whose defeat is treated as a matter of confidence; migration rules are amended by statutory instrument, without debate or vote. A government confronting a projected deterioration in the ratio of pensioners to workers faces three margins of adjustment priced very differently by its institutions, and the cheapest is the quietest.

The hypothesis is therefore specific: that projected deteriorations in the OADR, as published in the government's own biennial National Population Projections, predict subsequent inflows of non-EU migrants, conditional on the fiscal variables the institutional literature identifies as the Treasury's decision inputs. The choice of non-EU flows is central to the design. Under free movement, EU inflows responded to market conditions and were beyond the Home Office's reach; non-EU entry was rationed throughout by a discretionary work-permit regime and, from 2008, a points-based system. If demographic forecasts shape policy, their imprint should appear on the controlled margin and not the uncontrolled one, and that contrast supplies the paper's principal falsification test.

The paper makes three contributions. It assembles a new annual series of official demographic forecasts, the projected old-age dependency ratio at horizons of one to ten years reconstructed from every National Population Projection vintage from the 1977-based to the 2018-based, most of which survive only as un-indexed scans; no study has compiled the UK vintages into a usable series, or tested for any country whether such projections predict policy-controlled migration. It provides a political-economy rationale for the reduced form, a model in which a fiscally dominant finance ministry allocates adjustment across taxes, borrowing, and controlled migration in proportion to their salience, delivering the estimating equation's signs as ex ante predictions. And it offers a documented alternative to the leading account of the expansion: Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011) attribute around three-quarters of the rise in net immigration in the decade to 2009 to structural change proxied by dummy variables and attributed to the post-1997 government, whereas non-EU inflows track the projected OADR deterioration across the whole of 1977–2019, including under the Conservative administrations of the 1980s and 1990s, which a Blair-specific account cannot explain.

The paper is related to three literatures. In the selection framework migration responds to income differentials net of moving costs (Borjas, 1987); Hatton and Williamson (2002) add networks, source-country youth shares, and the observation that destination *policy* is the binding constraint most models omit, while Mayda (2007) finds that restrictions make destination conditions dominate origin push factors, precisely the environment in which the determinants of the policy become the determinants of the flow. Storesletten (2000) establishes the fiscal arithmetic taken here as premise, that working-age, skilled immigration can in principle sustain fiscal policy through ageing, and the United Nations (2000) bounds it from the other side, finding the inflows needed to hold support ratios constant implausibly large; whether ageing destinations pull migrants through deliberate policy has not been tested. Within the UK branch, Mitchell and Pain (2003) advocate the ARDL approach adopted here, Hatton (2005) finds unemployment matters in changes rather than levels, and Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011) supply the structural-change account raced in Section 6.6. The paper also draws on the institutional literature on British fiscal governance (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974; Hood, King, McLean and Piotrowska, 2023), on critiques of the OBR's independence (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021), and on the political economy of instrument choice, of which the mechanism is a fiscal application.

The argument is a claim about incentives, not concealment: each government faced the same constraint set and, on the hypothesis, independently found the same margin cheapest, so no coordination is needed for the rule to appear stable, and its evidential standing rests on tests it could have failed. Three limitations are addressed as far as the data allow: the projections embed migration assumptions, so Section 6.3 re-estimates on projections rebuilt under zero net migration; the sample is short (43 observations); and outside one documented episode (Section 2.4) the evidence for the mechanism is institutional and statistical, for reasons the instrument-choice logic itself explains.

Section 2 sets out the institutional background, Section 3 the model, Section 4 the data, and Section 5 the empirical strategy. Section 6 presents the estimates, identification checks, long-run relationship, falsification tests, and the horse race against the structural-change account; Section 7 discusses external events and limitations, and Section 8 concludes.

# 2. Institutional Background

## 2.1 The Treasury as fiscal centre

The mechanism rests on two institutional claims: that His Majesty's Treasury has both the incentive and the authority to treat migration as an instrument of fiscal policy, and that the instrument could be adjusted at low political cost. The Treasury centralises powers other governments distribute across several ministries, budgeting, tax, macroeconomic policy, and the financing of the state, the last “perhaps the one aspect of twenty-first-century government that a medieval monarch would recognise” (Hood et al., 2023); the Institute for Government describes a “pervasive” veto and a unique capacity “to initiate and force through policies” (Institute for Government, 2024b). The full institutional history is in Online Appendix E.

Two features matter. The first is continuity of preferences across governments. The department's doctrine, the “Treasury view”, a phrase Churchill coined as Chancellor (Hansard, 1929) for a fiscal conservatism traceable to Gladstone and still, decades later, “imbued with a dreadful spirit of economy” (Higgs, 1924; Institute for Government, 2024a), is described in substantially the same terms by Heclo and Wildavsky (1974) and by Hood et al. (2023) fifty years apart: its business is to “save money, not to spend it” and its chief interest “to save themselves the unpopularity of having to put up taxes.” That is the behavioural assumption of the model, stated by the institution's own members. The same study establishes that budgeting took the previous year's estimates as baseline, incorporated internal forecasts of growth, inflation, and employment, and examined proposals over five-year horizons, facts that anchor the information set of Section 4; the continuity is what permits one policy rule to be estimated over forty-three years and eight Prime Ministers.

The second is the department's willingness to pursue its doctrine independently of the elected government. In 1976 the Treasury negotiated the largest loan in the IMF's history, while the Labour Cabinet was split, on austerity terms justified by debt figures later shown to have been grossly overstated in the documents submitted to the Fund; Edmund Dell, then Paymaster General, defended the exaggeration in print as necessary to secure cuts the department believed essential (Dell, 1991; López, 2014). The Department of Economic Affairs, created in 1964 to divide the Treasury's authority, was re-absorbed within five years (Clifford, 1997). These are existence proofs; a milder disposition is all the mechanism requires. The Office for Budget Responsibility, created in 2010 with a staff of fewer than twenty under a Charter the Treasury writes (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021), inherited machinery linking demography to fiscal sustainability that had existed within the Treasury and the Government Actuary's Department throughout the sample; its migration variants (OBR, 2014) publish arithmetic the fiscal centre had long performed internally.

## 2.2 The salience asymmetry: Budget Day and the Immigration Rules

The Treasury's annual programme is announced in the Budget, an event that exists to concentrate accountability for fiscal choices at one maximally visible moment (UK Parliament, 2026): trailed for weeks, delivered to a full House, dissected within hours by the opposition, the Institute for Fiscal Studies, the press and, since 2010, the OBR, and enacted through a Finance Bill whose defeat is by convention a matter of confidence. Heclo and Wildavsky (1974) describe Budget Days as “great political occasions” on which “the pressures on a Chancellor are intense”; the 2022 statement delivered without an OBR assessment repriced government debt within days and ended the Truss premiership within weeks (Wamsley, 2026). The fiscal responses to ageing must be announced by the Chancellor, on that stage, and voted on by his colleagues.

Migration policy faces no comparable exposure. The Immigration Act 1971 delegated the detail of entry policy to the Immigration Rules, which the Home Secretary amends by laying a Statement of Changes before Parliament: secondary legislation requiring no debate, no vote, and no Commons majority, laid several times a year (Home Office, 2026). The shift from primary to secondary legislation accelerated over exactly this period: annual statutory instruments held near two thousand through the 1980s, stepped up sharply from around 1990 and stayed above three thousand for two decades, while Acts of Parliament roughly halved (House of Commons Library, 2019; Hansard Society, 2014), an escalation belonging to no single administration and running alongside the migration expansion.

Within the executive, coordination requires no public act: collective responsibility forecloses public dissent, and the Treasury sets every department's budget. The claim is not that migration targets were written into Budget documents, which Section 3 explains they would not be, but that the machinery existed, was centralised, and operated below the threshold of scrutiny throughout.

## 2.3 Control of the non-EU margin, 1977–2019

The Immigration Act 1971 ended Commonwealth free movement two years before EEC accession created its European replacement, and created the delegated machinery through which all later non-European entry policy has been made (Hatton and Wheatley Price, 1999; Bauer and Salt, 2020). Throughout the sample control was exercised exclusively over the non-EU margin, through two regimes.

Under the work-permit system, in force until 2008, a non-EU national required a job offer from an employer who had shown under the Resident Labour Market Test that the vacancy could not be filled domestically, and a visa could be refused even after a permit was granted; by the mid-2000s more than eighty routes existed (Home Office, 2006, p. 7), and the Rules have since grown to some 375,000 words (Guardian, 2018). Absent published quotas, the dual approval gave the state continuous discretionary control over volumes, and it dictates the measurement strategy: a Statement of Changes may loosen some routes while tightening others, permit data are too fragmentary for statistical use (Bauer and Salt, 2020), and no stringency series spans the period, so policy is measured by its outcomes on the controlled margin. That the lever moved with demography, quietly, is visible administratively: in 1990 the Overseas Labour Section recorded that “because of demographic changes the number of work permits being granted is rising sharply” while asking that “actual figures” be “omitted as they can be misleading” (The National Archives, LAB 109/66).

The points-based system introduced from 2008 consolidated the routes into five tiers and allocated entry by points for salary, qualifications, language and age: applicants aged 27 or under received 20 points, the credit declining to 5 and cutting off at 31 (Home Office, 2006), the closest the regime comes to stating that the age structure of inflows was an objective. It imposed no cap, and by removing case-by-case approval shifted apparent responsibility for volumes to the labour market, a visibility-reducing design. Two features bear on interpretation. The National Health Service recruits internationally at scale, and non-British nationals, three-quarters from outside the EU, form roughly a fifth of its workforce (House of Commons Library, 2025). And the dependent variable aggregates all long-term non-EU inflows deliberately: every category's criteria were policy-controlled and every category adds working-age residents; students enter with work rights and spending power, are the youngest entrants, and recorded departures well below arrivals. The post-2019 extension of the system to EU nationals (Migration Observatory, 2021) is used in Section 7 as an out-of-sample check.

## 2.4 What the fiscal centre wrote down

The fiscal centre's reading of the projection machinery can be tested against the written record at three points. The Treasury's 1984 Green Paper tabulated the official projections and costed the pensioner numbers they implied, naming demography first among the pressures on future spending (HM Treasury, 1984); the Cabinet Office brief on its drafting warned of the 'political risks' of illustrating that arithmetic in public; and by November 1996 the Treasury's board had been briefed that the old-age dependency ratio would nearly double over fifty years (The National Archives, PREM 19/1190; T 733). An OECD note circulated to Downing Street in 1984 had already remarked on governments' 'expanding use of off-budget measures' whose true costs, 'though less apparent, are not necessarily less', the selection principle Section 3 formalises.

The first statutory long-term projections, Annex A of the 1999 Economic and Fiscal Strategy Report (HM Treasury, 1999; Section 4.2), name the responses the government was prepared to advertise, first among them the legislated rise in the female state pension age, which “will play a key role in reducing long-term spending pressures” (p. 90); migration appears nowhere among the remedies. The record thus confirms that the fiscal centre was analysing ageing at Budget time on a ten-year-plus horizon, and the salience ranking of the model: the pension-age instrument, legislated fifteen years before it bound, is named and claimed; the instrument adjustable by statutory instrument is not.

For the turn-of-the-millennium expansion the mechanism is documented directly, in drafts released under the Freedom of Information Act (Cabinet Office, 2010) of the government's first comprehensive economic analysis of migration, prepared by the Cabinet Office Performance and Innovation Unit and the Home Office and published in January 2001 (Glover et al., 2001). The July 2000 draft, classified RESTRICTED–POLICY, reasons in the terms of Section 3: without net migration the working-age population would fall by two million over twenty-five years while the over-65 population rose by three million; ageing “will have to be addressed: presumably by some combination of net migration, an increase in the average working life, changes to entitlements, increases in contributions (either tax-based or private), and increases in productivity,” since “the alternative to migration may often be tax increases”; and these pressures would recast immigrants “less like a threat and more as a way to avoid the more unpleasant alternatives of tax increases, benefit cuts, and economic stagnation.” None of this survived to publication: the quantified requirements, the instrument menu, the comparison with taxation, and the “unpleasant alternatives” passage are all absent by the November draft, the autumn in which ministers announced “managed migration”. The episode documents the reasoning and its removal; it covers one episode under one government, in the Cabinet Office's and Home Office's voice rather than the Treasury's (Section 7). The premise was later stated on the record: the Home Office's 2008 evidence to the House of Lords reported its own estimate that migrants paid more in taxes, relative to what they received, than the UK-born, and cited GAD analysis in which higher migration lowered the National Insurance rate needed in 2060–61 from 27.9 to 26.3 per cent (Home Office, 2008). The argument requires only that the government assumed this, not that it was true.

# 3. A Stylised Model of Fiscal Instrument Choice

This section sets out a minimal model whose purpose is to discipline the specification: it delivers the sign and the conditioning set of the estimating equation as ex ante predictions.

Consider an incumbent government that must finance age-related spending each period. Let dₜ denote the old-age dependency ratio and let Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] be the government's own projection of the ratio h years ahead, taken from the current NPP vintage. Mandatory spending per working-age person is g(dₜ), with g′ > 0. The government disposes of three instruments: the tax burden per working-age person τₜ, borrowing bₜ, and controlled (non-EU) migration mₜ, which augments the working-age tax base. The per-period budget identity is

[[EQ]]
τₜ + bₜ = g(dₜ),
[[/EQ]]

and anticipated ageing, Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ > 0, raises the spending requirement the government expects to have to finance within its planning horizon.

Each instrument carries a political cost proportional to its salience: tax changes are maximally salient, borrowing is constrained by bond markets and the Treasury's doctrinal aversion to debt, and migration adjustments are minimally salient, proceeding by statutory instrument. Let the period political cost be

[[EQ]]
Cₜ = ½ φ_τ sτ (τₜ − τ̄)² + ½ φₘ sₘ mₜ² + φᵤ uₜ₋₁ mₜ + ½ ψ (mₜ − mₜ₋₁)²,
[[/EQ]]

where sτ ≫ sₘ > 0 are salience weights, τ̄ the inherited tax burden regarded as politically neutral, uₜ₋₁ the unemployment rate when policy is set, since visible inflows during visible joblessness are the one circumstance in which the instrument's salience rises sharply, and the final term an adjustment cost for the frictions of changing entry rules quickly. That inflows carry an electoral price at all, so that sₘ is positive and the instrument quiet rather than free, is well established: Dustmann, Vasiljeva and Piil Damm (2019), exploiting quasi-random refugee allocation across Danish municipalities, find that local exposure shifts votes toward anti-immigration parties outside the largest cities. The government minimises expected discounted political cost subject to the budget identity, a debt limit bₜ ≤ b̄(Bₜ₋₁) tightening in the inherited stock, which is why lagged debt rather than the flow of borrowing enters the rule, and the transition law under which migration today lowers the ratio at the horizon. The salience ranking is Section 2.2 in symbols.

The first-order condition for migration takes the form

[[EQ]]
φₘ sₘ mₜ + φᵤ uₜ₋₁ + ψ (mₜ − mₜ₋₁) = λₜ · MB(Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ),
[[/EQ]]

equating the marginal political cost of admissions to their marginal fiscal benefit, where λₜ, the shadow value of fiscal room, rises with the inherited tax burden and with the tightness of the borrowing constraint, and MB(·) rises with the projected deterioration the inflow offsets. Linearising around the inherited position yields a policy rule of the form

[[EQ]]
mₜ = ρ mₜ₋₁ + β₁ (Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ) + β₂ τₜ₋₁ + β₃ Bₜ₋₁ − β₄ uₜ₋₁,
[[/EQ]]

with all coefficients positive. Online Appendix D proves the following. *Proposition 1.* If taxation is more salient than migration (sτ > sₘ > 0), adjustment is costly (ψ > 0), the debt limit tightens in the inherited stock, and the marginal fiscal benefit of migration rises with projected ageing, then: (P1) β₁ > 0, larger the greater the salience gap sτ/sₘ; (P2) β₂ > 0, provided the salience of a further tax increase rises steeply enough with the burden already borne; (P3) β₃ > 0, the debt constraint being measured by the recent change in the debt ratio; (P4) β₄ > 0; (P5) ρ ∈ (0, 1); (P6) inflows the government cannot ration do not respond to the projection; and (P7) the response to the total dependency ratio, whose child component is fiscally cheap, is attenuated towards zero. P6 and P7 are the falsification tests of Section 6.5, which a labour-demand or network account of the same inflows would fail.

The framework organises fiscal capacity along two margins: the *intensive*, extracting more from the existing working-age population, which is why unemployment and inactivity belong in the information set; and the *extensive*, enlarging the base by migration or by raising the state pension age. The sample contains one large realisation of the latter, the Pensions Act 1995, which equalised the female pension age over 2010–2019, enacted fifteen years before it bound and named in the 1999 Budget as a key means of reducing long-term pressures (Section 2.4); because the projections incorporate legislated pension-age changes, the migration response is identified net of this channel.

The model abstracts from migrant heterogeneity, price responses, endogenous salience, and signal extraction; each abstraction isolates the mechanism and bounds the conclusions to a policy response, not its welfare consequences. Two predictions connect it to the institutions. It predicts silence: because the instrument's value lies in its low salience, no optimising government publicises its use, so the mechanism must be detected in outcomes rather than announcements, which is why the falsification tests of Section 6.5, which the reduced form *can* fail, carry the weight, and which the excised 2000 draft bears out literally. And it predicts which projection matters: the old-age ratio, not the total ratio whose child component is cheap, the placebo of Section 6.5.

## 4.1 Dependent variable: non-EU inflows

Annual gross inflows of long-term migrants (intended stay of a year or more) by country of last residence are taken from the International Passenger Survey (IPS), conducted at ports since 1961. The dependent variable is the log of non-EU inflows per thousand working-age residents, with the denominator lagged one year so that current-year inflows cannot enter both sides of the equation.

The IPS is a voluntary port survey covering roughly 90 per cent of passenger traffic with a 68 per cent response rate (ONS, 2023); status is self-reported intention, entrants are recorded under the status held at entry, and sampling variance is material on thin routes. It is used for three reasons. It was the basis of the official estimates the government itself used (Portes and Forte, 2017), so it measures flows as policymakers perceived them, and the series is used as published contemporaneously. The adjusted Long-Term International Migration series, not directly comparable with the raw IPS (ONS, 2015), begins in 1991 and serves as a robustness check. And the decennial Census has contradicted the IPS materially only once, on post-2004 EU8 arrivals, a discrepancy on EU rather than non-EU flows. Classical error in the dependent variable inflates standard errors without biasing coefficients, which works against the results below.

Gross rather than net inflows are used because policy rations entry, not exit; scaling by the working-age population expresses the inflow in the units the mechanism cares about, and unscaled results are reported so that no population estimate need appear on either side. All entry categories are aggregated because every category's criteria were policy-controlled and every category adds working-age residents.

## 4.2 The projection series

The National Population Projections have been produced biennially since 1954, by the Government Actuary's Department (GAD) until 2006 and the ONS thereafter, projecting the population by single year of age on stated fertility, mortality, and migration assumptions. The measure used is the old-age dependency ratio, persons of state pension age per thousand of working age, on the UK definition consistent within each vintage. It is the headline measure of the ONS's own bulletins, and for fiscal purposes rightly so: those aged 65 and over were projected to absorb some 42 per cent of health spending by 2021/22 and draw the state pension besides, while education takes roughly nine per cent of public spending (Full Fact, 2018; Institute for Fiscal Studies, 2024). An anticipated rise in the OADR is, to a close approximation, an anticipated rise in mandatory spending per worker.

Old-age dependency is uniquely forecastable: Britain's baby boom arrived in two waves, the second and larger over roughly 1955–65, each reaching pension age on a schedule known decades ahead (Jones, 1980), so the people who will be pensioners in ten years are alive, resident, and counted today.

For the government the NPP is an internal product: for five decades GAD, a department of government, produced it on assumptions agreed with the Treasury. Toward the end of the sample its use at Budget time became statutory: the Code for Fiscal Stability (HM Treasury, 1998) required illustrative long-term fiscal projections over a horizon of “no less than 10 years into the future” (HM Treasury, 1999, p. 87), the horizon at which the projection variable is measured and, Section 6.1 reports, the one that fits best.

The variable of interest is the projected change in the OADR, Δ̂dₜ(h) = Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ, the ratio projected h years ahead in the most recent vintage less its current value, at baseline horizon h = 10; other horizons, weighted averages, and projected levels are examined below. For 1997 the operative vintage projected an OADR roughly six points higher in 2007, so Δ̂d₁₉₉₇(10) ≈ 6.1, then the largest projected ten-year deterioration on record, though the vintages of the early 2000s would soon quadruple it. The variable is the ageing news confronting the Budget's author.

Assembling the series required reconstructing the full history of vintages (Online Appendix A). The ONS publishes complete projections only back to 2014, and earlier vintages exist as scans on an unindexed National Archives site navigable only to 1998; a machine-readable annex to the 2008-based NPP was found to link every predecessor back to 1954 (ONS, 2016b), and through it the projected OADR at each horizon was extracted for every vintage from the 1977-based to the 2018-based, the most recent vintage carried forward between releases. Multi-vintage records had been assembled only from within the statistical service (Shaw, 2007); this is, to my knowledge, the first horizon-complete panel from public sources and the first use of the vintages as an information-set regressor. Vintages are assigned by base year, an assumption confronted in Section 5.2, and the legislated female pension-age rise is embedded in the relevant vintages.

## 4.3 Fiscal and macroeconomic controls

The institutional literature specifies the Treasury's inputs as the fiscal aggregates and the labour market, observed with a lag (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974); the controls are the previous year's values of three series. *Tax per working-age person*, in thousands of constant 2024–25 pounds, is national accounts taxes (OBR, 2026; HM Treasury, 2026) divided by the working-age population; the per-taxpayer basis rather than tax-to-GDP is the appropriate measure because the political cost attaches to the burden per taxpayer, and the variable measures how far the intensive margin has already been used. *The annual log change in net debt as a share of GDP* measures the rate at which the borrowing margin is closing. *The ILO unemployment rate* (ONS, 2026) is the Budget-time reading of the labour market. Candidates tested and excluded, the inactivity rate, growth and productivity, gilt yields (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 2025), and the Budget-time growth forecast digitised via the Economic Statistics Centre of Excellence (2026), are documented in Section 6.1.

## 4.4 The absence of an instrument

No credible instrument for UK inflows exists: the only proposed proxy, National Insurance registrations (Portes and Forte, 2017), fails on relevance by the ONS's own assessment, since registrations neither distinguish short-term from long-term stays nor net out emigration (ONS, 2016a). Identification therefore rests on timing, predetermined regressors, and falsification, as set out next.
