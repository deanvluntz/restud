                                     SUMMARY OF THE REVIEW
                                     1. The major purpose of the five-yearly reviews by the Government Actuary of
                                     the operation of the Social Security Acts is to establish the rates of contribution
                                     likely to be required in future years to meet the cost of the benefits provided for
                                     under the National Insurance Scheme. The main estimates in Section 4 of this
                                     report are based on the Acts in force at the end of 1994. Section 6 includes
                                     estimates which take account of the proposals in the Pensions Bill 1994 to raise
                                     female pension age and to amend the arrangements for contracting out as well as
                                     the proposals in the Jobseekers Bill 1994 to change unemployment benefits. My
                                     report on the financial effect on the National Insurance Fund of the Pensions Bill
                                     1994 contained estimates of the effect of the proposals in that Bill on the
                                     projections of future benefit expenditure and contribution rates. Section 6 of this
                                     report provides some further estimates, in particular on the sensitivity of the
                                     projections to different assumptions for the main factors affecting the future
                                     finances of the Fund.

                                     2. The National Insurance scheme is financed on the pay-as-you-go principle.
                                     The contribution rates are set from year to year to produce the income needed to
                                     meet current expenditure on benefits and the costs of administration. The rates
                                     of contribution needed are determined by the average level of benefits and by the
                                     relative numbers of contributors and beneficiaries. As these numbers are very
                                     much affected by demographic factors, projections are made for a period of 60
                                     years into the future to illustrate the impact of demographic changes and the
                                     gradual maturing of the provisions of the Scheme.

                                     3. The demographic trends projected in this report point to higher levels of
                                     social security expenditure on retirement pensions supported by a slowly
                                     declining population of working age. This would suggest a continuing rise in the
                                     level of contributions needed to sustain the National Insurance Fund if benefit
                                     levels maintain their values relative to the earnings on which contributions are
                                     based. However, assuming that flat-rate benefits continue to be increased in line
                                     with prices and taking account of the changes proposed in the Pension Bill 1994,
                                     the principal projections show a slight decrease in contribution rates by 2030 and
                                     more significant reductions in the longer term.

                                     4. The main benefit in terms of cost is the retirement pension, which currently
                                     accounts for about 75 per cent of total expenditure. The population in Great
                                     Britain at present includes about 10.4 million people over pension age and this
                                     number is expected to rise slowly by an average of about 40,000 each year over
                                     the next 10 years. Thereafter, with the changing age structure of the population
                                     and assumed steady reductions in mortality rates, the number over the current
                                     State pension ages of 60 for females and 6.5 for males will increase gradually to
                                     reach a projected peak of over 16 million in the 20.30s. The numbers in the
                                     population at working ages are expected to remain relatively stable for the next
                                     30 years. On the main assumption of births averaging 1.9 per woman, the
                                     numbers at working ages will fall slowly in the long-term future. As a result, there
                                     will be a significant deterioration in the ratio of the number of contributors to the
                                     number of pensioners which, other things being equal, would require steady
                                     increases in contribution rates over the early decades of the next century.

                                     5. Another significant factor which tends to increase contribution rates in
                                     future is the continuing build-up of the cost of the State earnings-related pension
                                     scheme (SERFS). Employees started to earn entitlement to SERFS pensions in
                                     April 1978 but no-one retiring will have earned a full entitlement until 1998.
                                     Thereafter it will be a further 20 years or so before all pensions in payment reflect
                                     the full additional earnings-related element. Expenditure on SERFS therefore
                                     increases steadily throughout the first quarter of the next century in spite of the
                                     changes introduced in the Social Security Act 1986 and proposed in the Fensions
                                     Bill 1994.

                                   SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION AND SCOPE OF THE
                                   REVIEW
                                   1.1 The National Insurance Scheme is financed on the pay-as-you-go principle
                                   with rates of contribution set to produce the income needed to meet current
                                   expenditure on benefits and the costs of administration. With this system of
                                   finance, the rates of contribution required may alter significantly over the years,
                                   as a result of the maturing of the benefit rights under the scheme or on account of
                                   demographic or other factors leading to changes in the relative numbers of
                                   pensioners and contributors. For this reason the legislation governing the
                                   National Insurance Scheme in Great Britain has always made provision for
                                   5-yearly reviews by the Government Actuary on the operation of the scheme,
                                   including long term projections of the expenditure and of the corresponding
                                   rates of contribution likely to be required over the years. The present review is
                                   the third under Section 137 of the Social Security 1975 Act.


                                   1.2 The report on the second Quinquennial Review was published as H.C.
                                   Paper No 582 of 1989-90. Since that date, regular annual reports have been made
                                   by the Government Actuary on the estimated short-term financial effect on the
                                   National Insurance Fund of amendments to the contribution and benefit rates. In
                                   addition, a report by the Government Actuary on the financial provisions of the
                                   Pensions Bill 1994 was published in December 1994 (Cm 2714).


                                   1.3 The main legislative changes which were enacted in the 1985-90
                                   quinquennium or subsequently and which have affected the entitlement to
                                   benefits and the structure of contributions in the National Insurance Fund, are
                                   summarized in Appendix B. The main projections of benefit expenditure and
                                   contribution income in Section 4 are based on the legislation in force as at the end
                                   of 1994. They therefore take account of changes since 1990 but do not allow for
                                   the proposed changes to female state pension age and to the terms for
                                   contracting-out which have not yet been enacted but which will have a material
                                   effect on future contribution rates. Some estimates of the financial effect of these
                                   proposals are contained in my report on the Pensions Bill 1994 and further details
                                   and analysis of the sensitivity of the estimates in that report are given in Section 6.


                                   1.4 'Fhe object of this Review, as stated in Section 137(3) of the Social Security
                                   Act 1975, is to determine “the extent to which the level at which the National
                                   Insurance Fund stands from year to year may be expected in the longer term to
                                   bear a proper relation to demands in respect of payments of benefit’’. For this
                                   purpose the Government Actuary is required to take into account current rates
                                   of contribution, the yield to be expected from contributions in the longer term
                                   and such other matters as he considers to be relevant as affecting the present and
                                   future level of the Fund.

                                    1.7 A factor which in isolation would lead to a steady increase in the rates of
                                    contribution required for many years ahead is the gradual maturing of rights to
                                    earnings-related additional retirement and other pensions. Rights to such
                                    additional pensions have been accruing only since April 1978. It will be 1998
                                    before those retiring will have been able to secure an additional pension based on
                                    a full period of accrual and over 20 years thereafter before the pensions in
                                    payment for nearly all pensioners reflect this level of pension. Furthermore, the
                                    reductions in the rate of accrual and the other modifications introduced by the
                                    Social Security Act 1986 are being phased in gradually and will not be fully
                                    reflected in the cost of pensions until the middle of the next century. The
                                    estimates in this report must therefore cover a long period into the future in order
                                    to show the effect of the developing cost of additional pensions.

                                    1.8 Apart from the increase in the cost of additional pensions, the main factors
                                    liable to produce a significant change in the contribution rates needed in future to
                                    keep income and expenditure in balance are:

                                         (i) any divergence between the future rates of increase in earnings and the
                                             rates of increase in the flat-rate benefits and in the earnings limits used in
                                             the assessment of earnings-related contributioiis and benefits;

                                        (ii) factors resulting in a change in the relative numbers of contributors and
                                             pensioners in future. These factors are mainly demographic but include
                                             also social and economic factors such as changes in the proportion of
                                             women in employment or in the rate of unemployment.

                                    1.9 The relationship between average earnings levels and benefit rates is
                                    fundamental in determining the contribution rates required under the scheme.
                                    The Fund’s income is largely linked to the movement of earnings. If the rate of
                                    increase in the flat-rate benefits is below the rate of growth of earnings then,
                                    other things being equal, the rate of contribution required to meet the cost of
                                    flat-rate benefits would steadily decline. 'Fhe implications of alternative
                                    strategies in regard to the uprating of benefits and of the earnings limits and the
                                    assumptions made for the review are considered in Section 2.

                                    1.10 The projected demographic developments are discussed in Section 3. Very
                                    significant changes in the age structure of the population are expected to occur in
                                    the second and third decades of the next century. The numbers over state
                                    pension age will be increasing steeply at a time when the numbers at working
                                    ages are declining, leading to a marked fall in the ratio of the number of
                                    contributors to the number of pensioners. In order to bring out the effects of
                                    these demographic changes on the finances of the scheme, as well as the effects of
                                    the maturing of the entitlements to additional pension, estimates in this review
                                    are shown for the period up to 20.50.

                                    1.11 Projections for such long periods ahead are inevitably subject to
                                    considerable uncertainty and this must be taken into account in considering the
                                    implications of the figures. In particular, regard should be had to the potential
                                    variation in future contribution rates as a result of changes to the more important
                                    variables. .Sections 5 and 6 of the report gives estimates of the effect on the rates
                                    of contribution required for specimen changes in the main demographic and
                                    other assumptions determining future income and outgo, in order to bring out
                                    the sensitivity of the estimates to variation from the underlying assumptions.

                                      Balance in the Fund
                                      1.12 The adequacy of the Fund to meet the demands in respect of payment of
                                      benefits depends on rates of contribution being set from year to year at levels to
                                      produce the income required to meet the expenditure under the Scheme. It is
                                      also necessary to ensure that the balance in the Fund remains sufficient to ensure
                                      the smooth day to day operation of the Scheme. As the National Insurance Fund
                                      has no borrowing powers, the balance, including the Treasury grant, must be
                                      sufficient to cover the consequences of an unexpected excess of expenditure or
                                      short-fall in income in the short term, given the inevitable time lag before such
                                      developments mav become apparent and appropriate measures approved and
                                      implemented to restore the position. In addition, a working balance has to be
                                      maintained in order to be able to advance money to the Post Office and other
                                      agencies for the purpose of paying pensions and other benefits.

                                      1.13 The minimum balance of the Fund on this criterion increases with the
                                      growth in benefit expenditure as a result of the annual reviews of benefit rates
                                      and the other factors referred to in this report. It is thus necessary to budget for a
                                      small excess of income over expenditure for this purpose. However the Fund is
                                      invested and generates investment income which over recent years has been at a
                                      level which, other things being equal, would have broadly maintained the real
                                      value of the Fund relative to expenditure. Although there are fluctuations from
                                      year to year in the rate of return on the Fund’s investments relative to the rate of
                                      growth in expenditure, it is reasonable to assume that, when considered over a
                                      period of time, the investment income will provide broadly the amounts required
                                      to ensure the growth of the Fund balance keeps in step with expenditure.
                                      Investment income has therefore been left out of account in estimating the rates
                                      of contribution likely to be required in future years, which have been calculated
                                      as the rates required to generate income equal to the expected expenditure on
                                      benefits and administration.

                                      1.14 Having regard to the possible extent of errors in the estimates of income
                                      and expenditure on the basis of which contribution rates are decided, and the
                                      need for a degree of liquidity to provide cash balances for the paying of benefits,
                                      it is considered advisable to plan to maintain a minimum balance of about
                                      one-sixth of a year’s benefit expenditure.

                                      SECTION 2: THE EFFECT OF FUTURE INCREASES

                                      2.1 As the income of the National Insurance Fund arises mainly from earnings-
                                      related contributions, an important factor in determining the contribution rates
                                      required in future years will be the relationship between the level of flat-rate
                                      benefits, especially the basic pension, and the level of earnings. Section 150 of the
                                      Social Security Administration Act 1992 requires that the Secretary of State shall
                                      in each tax year review the change in the general level of prices over the previous
                                      year. If there has been an increase in prices, the Act requires benefit rates to be
                                      increased by at least that percentage. A requirement for an annual review of
                                      benefit rates and increases at least in line with the movement of prices has formed
                                      part of the legislation since the Social Security Act 1973, except that between
                                      May 1974 and May 1980 the long-term pension benefits were based on the
                                      increase in line with the growth in earnings if this exceeded the growth in prices.
                                      Prior to 1973 increases in benefits were on an ad hoc basis on the initiative of the
                                      government of the day and in practice, over the years between 1948 and 1980,
                                      benefits were increased broadly in line with average earnings. Since 1980 the
                                      increases have been in line with the movement of prices.

                                     Adjustment of upper and lower earnings limits
                                     2.5 Section 5 of the Social Security Contributions and Benefits Act 1992
                                     provides that the lower earnings limit for Class 1 contribution liability for any tax
                                     year should be set at the level of the basic pension rate for that year and that the
                                     upper earnings limit should be between 6’/2 and TA times that rate. Since 1985 the
                                     limit has been consistently at the upper end of this range. The effect is that the
                                     limits are now adjusted each year in proportion to the increase in the basic
                                     pension so that the basis of uprating the flat-rate benefit also applies to the
                                     earnings limits.
                                     2.6 In April 1994 the upper earnings limit represented about 119 per cent and
                                     the lower earnings limit about 16 per cent of the average earnings of male
                                     full-time employees. If the basic pension is increased by less than the increase in
                                     average earnings, then these limits will also fall steadily in relation to earnings.
                                     For example, if the increase in flat-rate benefits were to average l'/2 per cent a
                                     year below earnings, the upper earnings limit would fall to male average earnings
                                     in about the year 2005 and to two-thirds of male average earnings in about 2030.
                                     By the latter date the lower earnings limit would also have fallen to about half its
                                     present level in relation to earnings. The extent of this decline in the limits in the
                                     longer term is very sensitive to any variation from the differential of 1A per cent a
                                     year assumed.
                                     2.7 A fall in the upper earnings limit to below, and in the longer term
                                     significantly below, male average earnings would greatly diminish the range of
                                     earnings on which employees’ National Insurance contributions were levied and
                                     would gradually alter the contribution structure of the scheme. In time, most
                                     contributors would have earnings above the upper limit and, because employees
                                     pay contributions on earnings up to that limit, would effectively pay
                                     contributions at a flat-rate. The reduction in the lower earnings limit in relation
                                     to earnings would bring within the scope of National Insurance contributions, for
                                     both employees and employers, a large number of persons with relatively small
                                     earnings, mainly from part-time work, who are at present not covered by the
                                     scheme although they may pay contributions on a voluntary basis.

                                     3.1 In order lo project the future income and expenditure of the National
                                     Insurance Fund, it is necessary first to estimate the future numbers in the

                                     Projections of the future population of Great Britain are prepared regularly by
                                     the Government Actuary in consultation with the Registrars General of England
                                     and Wales and of Scotland. The projections based on the estimated population at
                                     mid-1992 have been used for the present review.

                                     assumptions will be published by the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys
                                     in a booklet (Series PP2 No 19). Only the assumptions of most significance for
                                     the future finances of the National Insurance Scheme are discussed in the
                                     following paragraphs.

                                     3.3 Estimates of the future population many years ahead are inevitably subject
                                     to a considerable margin of uncertainty. This is recognised in the official
                                     projections which in addition to a principal projection include variant projections
                                     on alternative assumptions in regard to the main factors. The principal projection
                                     has been used for the main estimates in Section 4 of this report but the variant
                                     projections form the basis of the estimates of the effect of changes in the
                                     mortality and fertility assumptions on the future rates of contribution shown in
                                     Sections 5 and 6.

                                     3.4 Those persons who wili be over the current pension ages for men (65) and
                                     women (60) up to the middle of the next century are already living and so the
                                     projected numbers of pensioners will be very largely determined by the
                                     assumption about future mortality. The rates assumed have been based on the
                                     experience over recent years, with an allowance made for continuing
                                     improvement in mortality for the period of the projection. For the main
                                     estimates in the report, reductions in mortality rates over the next 50 years of the
                                     order of 20 per cent to 40 per cent have been assumed at most ages for both men
                                     and women.

                                     3.5 Mortality is of much less significance in determining the future numbers at
                                     working ages, but after about 20 years the numbers at these ages will depend to
                                     an increasing extent on the numbers of future births. The fertility rates for
                                     women at each age in recent years have totalled about 1.8 children per woman,
                                     broadly the average level which has applied since the late 1970s, though well
                                     below the fertility experienced during the post-war years prior to that time. It was
                                     assumed for the principal projection that this ‘total period fertility rate’ would
                                     rise gradually to 1.9 by the end of the century and remain at that level thereafter.
                                     This is about 10 per cent below the rate of 2.1 required for each generation
                                     exactly to replace itself. This represents a change from the assumption
                                     underlying the estimates in the previous review when a gradual increase in
                                     fertility to full replacement level was assumed.

                                    5.2 The estimates in Section 4 are based on the principal mid-1992 based

                                    5.3 The lower mortality variant assumes that the rate of mortality improvement
                                    will be twice the level than in the principal projection after 40 years. As a result,
                                    the numbers in the population are higher at all ages than in the principal
                                    projection. However, the effect is greater at older than at younger ages and, in
                                    particular, there is a significant increase in the number of pensioners relative to
                                    the number of contributors, leading to increases in the required contribution
                                    rates.

                                    5.4 The lower fertility variant assumes that the average family size will
                                    ultimately reach 1.7 for women born in 1978 and later, rather than 1.9 as in the
                                    principal projection. This change in the annual number of births has a delayed
                                    effect on the estimates for future contribution rates. A change in the assumption
                                    for the number of births hardly affects the contribution rate until that generation
                                    has passed school leaving age and joined the working population. As time passes,
                                    more and more of the working population is made up of the generations that are
                                    yet to be born. On the lower fertility assumption, these generations are smaller,
                                    so that there will be fewer contributors and the contribution rate will thus have to
                                    be higher; for many years there will be little effect on the cost of benefits as
                                    retirement pensions will be unaffected and most of the other benefit costs are
                                    concentrated at the older ages. As these smaller generations reach pension age,
                                    the smaller number of pensioners would reduce pension costs although the main
                                    effect of this will be beyond the period covered by the projections. However, if
                                    the lower fertility persisted, the fall in the numbers of pensioners in the longer
                                    term would be accompanied by a continuing fall in the number of contributors so
                                    that the contribution rates would have to be higher in spite of the lower number
                                    of pensioners.

                                     employees. Under the current personal pension arrangements, a sum from the National
                                     Insurance Fund equivalent to the joint flat-rate contracted-out rebate for members of
                                     occupational schemes on the earnings of the individual concerned, together with a sum

                                     D.13 The flat-rate contracted-oul rebate is intended to represent the average cost to a
                                     funded occupational scheme of providing the Guaranteed Minimum Pension (GMP)
                                     forgone under the State Scheme pension entitlement. Because of the longer period to
                                     pension age over which contributions accumulate through investment and because of the
                                     higher rate of accrual for older age cohorts (for those over age 16 in 1978), the cost of
                                     providing the GMT is significantly lower at younger than at older ages. However, the
                                     contracted-out rebate is independent of age and represents an average calculated on the
                                     basis of the distribution by age and sex of members of contracted-out defined benefit
                                     occupational schemes. For this reason personal pensions have been taken out almost
                                     entirely by people at younger ages, where it can be shown that contributions to a personal
                                     pension arrangement of the minimum amount represented by the contracted-out rebate
                                     are likely to produce a higher pension than the GMP forgone under the State Scheme.
                                     The reverse applies at older ages and the statistics show that relatively few appropriate
                                     personal pensions have been set up for persons over age 45.

                                     D.14 The 2 per cent incentive payments ceased from April 1993. Together with the

                                      Unemployment benefit
                                      D.71 Unemployment benefit (UB) is payable under the conditions summarised in
                                      paragraph A.34. Expenditure in 1994-95 is estimated to be about £1.3 billion, i.e. about 3
                                      per cent of outgo, if unemployment averages at around 2,45 million (a rate of about 9 per
                                      cent).

                                      D.72 For the central estimates in Section 4 of the report, a long-term rate of
                                      unemployment of 5 per cent has been assumed for all future years. The effect of different
                                      assumptions for the level of unemployment is shown in Section 5.

                                      D.73 For a stated assumption for the level of employment, in order to estimate
                                      expenditure on unemployment benefit it remains to consider the proportion who will be
                                      entitled to receive benefit from the National Insurance Fund. Those newly unemployed
                                      who were formerly self-employed or non-employed (e.g. married women who had been
                                      at home) or who had previously exhausted their entitlement to benefit but not requalified
                                      or young persons who had not been employed for a sufficient period since leaving school
                                      will not be entitled to receive benefit. Those who meet the contribution and other
                                      conditions will receive benefit from the National Insurance Fund for a maximum of 52
                                      weeks before benefit ceases on current legislation. They will not requalify until they have
                                      been back at work for at least 16 hours in each of at least 13 weeks in the 26 week period
                                      before they claim benefit again. (Income support to the claimant unemployed may be
                                      paid from general revenue if UB is not in payment, National Insurance Fund ceases, or,
                                      under certain circumstances, to top-up the unemployment benefit in payment).

                                      D.74 It is impossible to predict with any confidence just what proportion of the
                                      unemployed will receive benefit from the National Insurance Fund at any time. This will
                                      depend upon several factors and particularly on whether the unemployment rate has
                                      recently been increasing or decreasing, whether the newly unemployed include large
                                      numbers of young persons or others without entitlement or adults who have been in
                                      regular employment, and whether the same individuals remain unemployed for long
                                      periods and exhaust their entitlement or are replaced by others with entitlement to 52
                                      weeks’ benefit.

                                      D.75 The probability of being entitled to unemployment benefit depends upon the
                                      duration of the spell of unemployment; for instance only a very small number of those
                                      who have been unemployed for 12 months or more are still entitled. Costs have therefore
                                      been derived by examining the trends in both the probability of ceasing to claim
                                      unemployment benefit between durations of unemployment and the proportions of those
                                      unemployed who are entitled to benefit at successive durations. It is estimated that on
                                      average about 21 per cent of the unemployed will be entitled to unemployment benefit in
                                      1994-95 with the current level of unemployment. In general it has been the case that for
                                      lower levels of unemployment, the average duration of unemployment declines and the
                                      overall proportion entitled to unemployment benefit rises. The recent experience implied
                                      that it would be reasonable to assume that 25 per cent of those counted as unemployed
                                      would on average be entitled to benefit if the unemployment rate were 5 per cent.

                                       D.76 For the estimates based on assuming an unemployment rate of 7 per cent,
                                       although the number unemployed is higher, a higher proportion of the unemployed will
                                       be over 52 weeks duration and, as a result, an estimated 22% are assumed to be entitled to
                                       benefit.

                                       Statutory sick pay
                                       D.77 Statutory Sick Pay (SSP) is payable to employees for up to 28 weeks during a
                                       period of entitlement due to sickness. The benefit is paid by employers, but qualifying
                                       small employers may recover some of the SSP they pay by reducing the amount of
                                       National Insurance contributions they remit.

                                       D.78 The finances of the National Insurance Fund are not affected by SSP as the Fund
                                       recovers from the Consolidated Fund the amount of the deductions made by small
                                       employers. The estimates for SSP are thus included only for completeness. It is estimated
                                       that these will amount to £25 million in 1994-95, the first full financial year under the
                                       current arrangements. In view of the small amount, the future estimates were derived by
                                       simply adjusting this figure in line with the estimates of the numbers of future
                                       contributors.

                                     D.79 Statutory Maternity Pay (SMP) is payable by employers to women satisfying the
