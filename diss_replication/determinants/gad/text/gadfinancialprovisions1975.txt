 REPORT BY THE GOVERNMENT ACTUARY ON THE FINANCIAL
                             PROVISIONS OF THE BILL

 To the Right Hon. BARBARA CASTLE, M.P.,
 Secretary of State for Social Services

 Madam,
   I submit the following report on the financial provisions of the Social Security
 Pensions Bill 1975.

    1. The Bill gives effect to the proposals for earnings related pensions which
 were outlined in the White Paper entitled “Better Pensions” (Cmnd. 5713)
 published in September 1974, and on which I reported in the Appendix to that
 White Paper. The proposals embodied in the Bill are described in detail in an
 explanatory memorandum (Cmnd. 5929) and the main provisions affecting the
 finances of the scheme are summarised below. The changes are to take effect
 from dates to be appointed but I understand that the new scheme is intended
 to start not later than April 1978 and this date has been assumed for the purpose
 of the estimates.

         Summary of main provisions affecting the National Insurance Fund
   2. The Bill amends the Social Security Act 1975 to provide for retirement,
 widows’ and invalidity pensions for employees to be related to the earnings on
 which contributions have been paid, which for this purpose will be revalued
 with changes in the general level of earnings. The pension will be calculated in
 two parts, a basic component corresponding to the fiat rate pension under the
 present scheme, equal to 100% of earnings up to this level, and an additional
 component which, when the scheme is fully mature, will be 25% of the employee’s
 average earnings above the level of the basic component.

    3. Members of occupational pension schemes which fulfil certain require-
 ments may be contracted out and they and their employers will pay Class 1
 contributions at reduced rates. In return the occupational scheme will be
 required to provide a minimum level of pension calculated on a basis similar
 to that for the additional component of retirement pension under the state
 scheme, with a widow’s pension at half this rate; the benefits payable from the
 state scheme will be correspondingly reduced.

   4. The Bill abolishes the contribution option for married women and widows,
 but provides for special arrangements to be made for women who have already
 acquired the right to exercise this option when the new scheme starts.

                                 The basis of the estimates

  Level of earnings and benefits
     24. In an earnings-related scheme, the actual amounts of income and outgo
  in future years will naturally depend on the level of earnings from time to time.
  However, estimates of income and outgo which made explicit allowance for
  future rises in earnings would show steadily increasing amounts which would
  be meaningful only if set against corresponding estimates of total wages and
  salaries, gross national product, etc., in future years. For this reason the trends
  over the years are best illustrated by expressing all future amounts in equivalent
  present-day terms. Estimates of benefit expenditure have thus been shown in
  terms of a basic component of pension of £11-60 a week. In estimating rates of
  contributions the yields have been calculated on earnings assumptions
  consistent with this basis.

     25. The estimates should thus be regarded as making explicit allowance for
  future increases in earnings and the consequential effects on contributions and
  benefits with the resulting amounts of income and outgo in future years in
  money terms being discounted by reference to the assumed level of the basic
  component of pension in the year in question and the level at the base date,
  so as to produce meaningful figures expressed in present day terms. Apart from
  the adjustments for the factors described in paragraph 26, the figures expressed
  in present-day terms will be the same whatever the projected rate of inflation.

    26. As the effect of inflation on contributions and benefits is broadly self-
 balancing, the rate of contributions required to support the benefits, expressed
 as a percentage of earnings, is not sensitive to the rate of inflation. Indeed, if
 benefits and contributions were to rise exactly in step with earnings, both the
 estimates expressed in present-day terms and the rate of contribution would
 be wholly independent of this factor. In practice, however, the time lag before
 any changes are reflected in the flow of receipts or payments varies considerably.
 Thus for employees with earnings below the upper limit contributions will
 increase continuously with rising earnings but benefits will remain fixed between
 upratings and both the earnings limits and flat rate Class 2 and Class 3 con-
 tributions will be adjusted only at annual intervals. Furthermore, although the
 basic component of pension will be increased in line with earnings, the addi-
 tional component and the graduated pensions under the present scheme will be
 increased in line with prices. Thus to the extent that earnings increase faster
 than prices the cost of the latter benefits will rise more slowly than the income
 from contributions.

     31. The number of employees who will be contracted out cannot be forecast.
   The Fourth Survey of Occupational Pension Schemes showed that in 1971
   about 8 million employees were members of final salary pension schemes and

    48. Future births. The rates of contributions required at any time for a scheme
  financed on the pay-as-you-go principle will depend on the relative numbers
  of beneficiaries and contributors which in turn will be largely determined by the
  age structure of the population. The population projection which has been
  used for the estimates presented in this report is considered to be suitable for
  the purpose of illustrating the finances of the scheme and the contribution
  rates likely to be required, but it must be recognised that there can be no
  certainty in projections of the population. In particular, it is difficult to predict
  the number of future births with confidence, and calculations have therefore
  been made to show the effect of making an alternative assumption as to future
  fertility rates. Table 4 shows the effect of a 10% variation in fertility rates
  compared with the assumption on which the estimates are based. It should be

          to be increased more frequently than yearly there would be a corresponding
          increase in expenditure and in the rates of contribution required.

     Comparison with estimates in '‘^Better Pensions^'
       52. The rates of contribution shown in Table 2 above, on the assumption
     of 8 % per annum earnings increase, are on a comparable basis with the estimates
    given in Table 2 of the Appendix to “Better Pensions” but show increases on
    the earlier estimates of about f % for the first few years of the scheme falling to
    nil after 30 years. The main reason for these increases is the change in the
    method of determining the benefit increases at successive upratings fOr which
    allowance has been made.. For the estimates in “Better Pensions” it was
    assumed implicitly that benefits at each uprating would be increased from the
    £10 rate which took effect in July 1974 in proportion to the rise in the general
    level of earnings from that date. As explained in paragraph 28, however, the
    present report allows for the method which was subsequently adopted in
    practice, which may be stated as follows: that benefits will rise from the £10
    pension level in line with the movement of earnings from November 1973 up
    to a date 8 months before the effective date of the uprating in question. Between
    November 1973 and July 1974 earnings rose at a rate of about 20% per annum
    so that substituting this period for the 8 months preceding the date of the
    uprating in the period over which the increase in earnings is measured will
    result in a higher rate of benefit than on the basis previously assumed once the
    rate of increase in earnings falls below 20%. If the rate of increase in earnings
    falls ultimately to 8% per annum the basic component of pension brought
    out would be some 7|% higher in relation to earnings than the pension in
    July 1974, with a corresponding increase of nearly | % in the rate of contribution
    required.

              54.               A further important factor accounting for ch
           tribution is the assumption in regard to the contribution option for married
           women and widows. As stated in “Better Pensions”, the estimates were based
           on the assumption that the option for long term benefits would be abolished
           from the start of the new scheme but that an option to forgo short term sickness
           and unemployment benefits in return for a reduced rate of contribution would
           remain as a permanent feature of the scheme. The Bill, however, provides for
           the contribution option to be wholly abolished for women who had not married
           before the starting date of the new scheme. When such women marry, they will
           be required to pay the normal rates of contribution and to be fully insured for
           all benefits, but it has been assumed for the purpose of the estimates in this
           report that the option will remain, as under the present scheme, for existing
           married women and widows. The effect of this change as compared with “Better
           Pensions” estimates is to add about 0*2% to the rates of contribution required
           initially, when the loss of contribution income will exceed the saving in benefit
           expenditure, but in the longer term the retention of the option for the present
           generation of married women will result in a slower build up in the expenditure
           on pensions and lower rates of contribution will be required. This is the main
           reason why the increases in contributions over the earlier estimates due to the
           other factors mentioned above are wholly offset by the end of 30 years.


           Further changes to be expected after 2008-09
              55. Detailed estimates have been shown for the first 30 years of the new
           scheme but the improvements in pensions provided for under the Bill will not
           be fully reflected in the expenditure at the end of this period. Although after
           20 years the additional components of pension will be awarded at the full 25%
           rate, the pensioners after 30 years will still include many whose benefit is based
           on lower rates because they retired during the first 20 years. Also the provision
           whereby the additional component is calculated on the basis of earnings in
           the best 20 years means that the average level of awards will continue to increase
           gradually for many years. Furthermore, the assumption that married women
           and widows at the start of the new scheme will retain the right to exercise a
           contribution option and that the great majority will choose to rely on benefits
           payable on their husbands’ insurance, means that it will be well beyond 30 years
           before the majority of married women and widow retirement pensioners come
           to consist of persons who have paid full contributions throughout their periods
           of employment.
