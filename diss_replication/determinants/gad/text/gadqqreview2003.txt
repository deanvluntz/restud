1.          SUMMARY OF THE REVIEW


1.1        The main purpose of the review is to estimate the contribution rates required
           to be paid to the National Insurance Fund of Great Britain in future years in
           order to meet expenditure on a pay-as-you-go basis under the current benefit
           and contribution structure.

1.2        The review considers projections of future income and expenditure up to the
           year 2060/61. Projections over such long time periods are subject to
           considerable uncertainty. It is necessary to consider the potential effects on
           the results of the projections of future experience differing from the
           assumptions used. This is considered in Section 8 of the report. This
           summary is based on the principal projections carried out for the review.

           Demographic background

           population of Great Britain subdivided by age and sex. This review is based
           on the 2001-based projections which were produced by the Government
           Actuary’s Department in 2002, and which take interim account of the results
           of the 2001 census. The projected population by age band is shown in Table

1.13   The price uprating scenario of the principal results allows for flat-rate benefits
       and earnings limits to be increased in line with prices. However, current
       Government policy is to increase the basic retirement pension each year by
       the greater of the rise in prices, or 2.5%, although this has not been
       incorporated into legislation. If this 2.5% underpin is applied in every future
       year, long-term expenditure on the basic retirement pension will be greater
       than that if the rate of basic retirement pension is increased each year solely
       in line with price increases. The effect of the underpin on long-term
       expenditure will depend on the level and variability of price inflation in the
       future. For illustrative purposes, with price uprating and assuming 2% per
       annum real earnings growth, if the underpin were to apply in every future
       year, the projected joint Class 1 contribution rate required to balance income
       and expenditure in 2060/61 may increase from 14.9% (as shown in Table 1.3)
       to around 16% or 17%.

1.15      Projections of the balance of the Fund are subject to considerable

          only be considered as broad indications as to possible future experience, and
          the uncertainties inherent in the projections must be borne in mind. Subject

          expected to continue to increase in the future, both in absolute terms and as a

          contrast, with earnings uprating, the balance in the Fund would be likely to fall
          to zero during the next 10 to 20 years, at which point the contribution rate
          would need to rise to the full pay-as-you-go rate.

2.          INTRODUCTION AND SCOPE OF THE REVIEW


2.1        The National Insurance Scheme of Great Britain is financed on the pay-as-
           you-go principle. This means that current rates of National Insurance
           contributions are set to produce the income needed to meet current
           expenditure on benefits and the costs of administration, as well as to maintain
           a reasonable working balance in the Fund. Legislation provides for a limited
           Treasury Grant to be paid into the Fund if the working balance falls below a
           certain level.

2.2        The legislation governing the National Insurance Scheme of Great Britain3
           has always made provision for 5-yearly reviews by the Government Actuary
           on the operation of the scheme, including long-term projections of the
           expenditure and of the corresponding rates of contribution likely to be
           required in future years. The present review is based on the quinquennium
           from 6 April 1995 to 5 April 2000 and contains projections from 2001/02 to
           2060/61. The figures for 2001/02 have been based on those shown in the
           Fund accounts for that year.

2.3        The Social Security Contributions (Transfer of Functions, Etc.) Act 1999
           transferred responsibility for National Insurance contributions policy to
           Treasury Ministers and the Inland Revenue with effect from 1 April 1999.
           Operational policy and functions were also transferred. The Department for
           Work and Pensions (previously the Department for Social Security) continues
           to be responsible for policy on benefits.

2.4        The object of this review is to report on the progress of the National Insurance
           Fund over the five year period and to consider the likely future impact of the
           current legislation, including estimating the contribution rates likely to be
           required up to the year 2060/61 in order to finance the benefits. With the pay-
           as-you-go method of financing, the Fund will remain adequate to meet the
           demands upon it over future years provided contribution rates are set at
           levels sufficient to generate the income each year to meet the expenditure in
           that year.

2.5        In addition, the review shows how the balance in the Fund is likely to develop
           on the assumption that the existing rates of contribution are maintained.

2.6        As well as reviewing the financial progress of the National Insurance Fund, it
           is appropriate to consider the likely impact of the benefit provisions from the
           beneficiaries’ point of view and the impact of the contribution structure on the
           balance of financing between employees and employers.

2.7        The previous review, based on the period 6 April 1990 to 5 April 1995, was
           published as Cm 4406, dated July 1999. That report allowed for the changes
           affecting the National Insurance Fund that were contained in the Pensions Act
           1995, in particular:

3.     DEMOGRAPHIC BACKGROUND


3.1    A key factor in the financial development of the National Insurance Fund is
       the structure of the population and how this changes over time. This means
       that a fundamental starting point of the review is a projection of the future
       population of Great Britain subdivided by age and sex. Projections of the
       future population of Great Britain are prepared regularly by the Government
       Actuary’s Department in consultation with the Registrars General of England
       and Wales and of Scotland.

3.2    The results of the 2001 census that were published in September 2002
       indicated population numbers about 1 million lower than previous population
       estimates. In the light of the census results, interim revised population
       projections were prepared. These projections were released on 1 November
       2002 and are available from the website of the Government Actuary’s
       Department at www.gad.gov.uk. These projections have been used as the
       basis for this review.

3.3    In September 2003, the Office for National Statistics published revised mid-
       2001 population estimates, together with the mid-2002 population estimates.
       These revised estimates take into account recently concluded research
       studies into the population estimates, and will be reflected in the next set of
       population projections prepared by the Government Actuary’s Department.

3.4    Estimates of the future population many years ahead are inevitably subject to
       a considerable margin of uncertainty. This is recognised in the official
       projections, which, in addition to a “principal” projection, include variant
       projections on alternative assumptions regarding the main factors. The
       principal projection has been used for the main results in Section 5 of this
       report. The variant projections are however used to show how changes in the
       projected population structure would affect the results of this review; this is
       shown in Section 8.

3.5    The future development of the population is strongly influenced by the current
       population structure by age and sex. In addition to this, there are three main
       factors that govern how population develops: mortality, fertility and migration.

       Mortality

3.6    Those persons who will be over the pension ages for men (65) and women
       (60 up to the year 2010 increasing to 65 by the year 2020) over the period of
       the review are already living. Thus, the projected numbers of pensioners will
       be very largely determined by the assumption about future mortality (and, to a
       lesser extent, by migration). The mortality rates assumed have been based
       on the experience over recent years, with an allowance made for continuing
       improvement in mortality for the period of the projection. Table 3.1 shows the
       expectation of life based on actual past mortality rates and assumed future
       rates for individuals born in 1940, 1970 and 2000.

3.8       Mortality is of much less significance in determining the future numbers at

          Fertility

3.9       The fertility rate (broadly the average number of children born to each
          woman) has been projected on a cohort basis (i.e. according to the mother’s
          year of birth). Cohort based fertility rates tend to be rather more stable than
          those for individual calendar years (which can be subject to distortion from
          variations in the average age of childbearing). Over recent years, fertility
          rates have been falling and at the same time there has been a shift towards
          later childbearing. Women born in the 1930s each had on average nearly 2½
          children but for those born in 1950 this had fallen to a little over 2. Women
          born in later years will not have come to the end of their potential period of
          childbearing and therefore it is more difficult to reach firm conclusions about
          their fertility. Nevertheless, from the available information, it is apparent that
          the decline in fertility is continuing.

3.10      It was assumed for the principal projections that the average number of
          children for subsequent cohorts will gradually fall to reach 1.74 for those
          women born in or after 1985. The average age at childbirth for those born in
          1950 was 26.4 years and for those born in 1985 and later is assumed to be
          just over 29 years.

3.11      The long-term fertility rate of 1.74 may be compared with the rate of about 2.1
          required for each generation to replace itself.

       Great Britain over pension age will rise slowly from just over 10.5 million in
       2001 to about 11 million by 2006 and then faster to reach nearly 11.7 million
       in the year 2010. Between 2010 and 2020, during which time pension age for
       women is to be raised to 65 on a phased basis, the numbers over pension
       age remain almost constant. After this, the numbers over pension age will
       rise more rapidly to a peak of nearly 15 million, over 40% above the current
       numbers, in the year 2039, before slowly declining again to 14.8 million by the
       year 2060. If female pension age had not been increased to 65, the numbers
       over pension age would have been higher, for example, by a little over 2
       million in 2030.

3.16   By contrast, the expected changes in the numbers at working ages are much
       less dramatic. The number rises slowly from 35.1 million in 2001 to a peak of
       nearly 38 million, about 8% above the starting number, in the year 2020,

       the year 2060. This reflects the assumption that fertility rates will be lower
       than the level required to replace the population, which largely offsets the
       effect of the increase in female pension age and the assumption of significant
       net inward migration.

3.20   The pensioner support ratio is important when considering the financial
       position of the National Insurance Fund because, in broad terms, the benefits
       for those over pension age need to be financed by contributions from those of
       working age, unless a significant fund of assets is built up to help pay for the
       benefits. The fall in the ratio from 3.3 in 2000 to about 2.4 in 2060 would
       lead, other things being equal, to an increase of over a third in the required
       rate of National Insurance contributions. Nevertheless, as already mentioned,
       this is a continuation of a well established process. Between 1950 and 2000
       the pensioner support ratio fell from 4.6 to 3.3 and this implied, other things
       being equal, an increase of nearly 40% in the required rate of contributions.

4.     METHOD AND ASSUMPTIONS


4.1    In addition to the population projection, it is necessary to make a large
       number of other assumptions about future experience in order to carry out the
       review. A summary of the methods and assumptions used for estimating
       future income and expenditure for the review is given in Appendix E. The
       assumptions shown in Appendix E are those underlying the results given in
       Sections 5, 6 and 7 of this report. The effect of varying some of these
       assumptions is considered in Section 8.

4.2    The rest of this Section comments on some of the key points of the
       assumptions.

       Uprating of benefit rates and earnings limits

4.3    The projections for the review have been carried out on two bases: price
       uprating and earnings uprating.

       Price            Under this approach all benefit rates are assumed to increase
       uprating         each year in line with the increase in the Retail Prices Index
                        (RPI). Earnings limits applied in determining contributions are
                        also assumed to increase in line with the RPI, except the Low
                        Earnings Threshold (LET), which in accordance with
                        legislation, is assumed to rise in line with the growth in
                        average earnings.

                        However, since earnings are assumed to rise more quickly
                        than prices, the LET will in due course reach the Upper
                        Earnings Limit (UEL). This is projected to happen in 2055/56
                        allowing for 2% per annum real earnings growth and in
                        2072/73 (i.e. beyond the end of the projection period in
                        2060/61) with 1.5% per annum real earnings growth. From
                        that point onwards it is assumed that the UEL will rise in line
                        with earnings, but that other earnings limits will remain linked
                        to prices.

       Earnings         Under this approach all benefit rates are assumed to increase
       uprating         in line with the rise in average earnings, apart from any
                        SERPS/State Second Pension that is in payment, which is
                        assumed to rise only in line with prices (which has always
                        been the practice since SERPS was introduced in April 1978).

                        Earnings limits for contributions purposes are also assumed
                        to rise in line with average earnings. The earnings threshold
                        for paying Class 1 contributions is currently aligned with the
                        single person’s tax allowance (SPTA), which under current
                        Government policy is increased in line with prices. Since
                        under the earnings uprating assumption the earnings
                        threshold rises in line with earnings, this therefore implies a
                        breaking of the link between the threshold and the SPTA.

       In each case, the review has allowed for the actual rates of benefit and
       earnings limits up to and including 2003/04. For subsequent years the



                                           19
 Government Actuary’s Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund as at April 2000



       benefits and earnings limits have been increased in line with prices or
       earnings as appropriate.

       State Second Pension

4.4    When originally introduced it was envisaged that, in due course, the State
       Second Pension would become a flat-rate scheme such that the pension
       would be independent of earnings. The calculation of the pension would then
       always be calculated as if earnings were equal to the LET, in the same way
       as already applies to employees who have earnings under the LET.
       However, for those contracted-out of the State Second Pension, contribution
       rebates and the benefit “top-ups” after retirement (see paragraph 10.56)
       would continue to be calculated based on the current earnings–related
       pension.

4.5    In the Green Paper “Simplicity, security and choice: Working and saving for
       retirement” the Government explained that the timing of any possible move to
       a flat-rate scheme will be kept under review, taking into account wider
       developments in the pensions and savings environment. For the purpose of
       the main results set out in Section 5, it has been assumed that the State
       Second Pension does not become flat-rate, but the financial consequences of
       such a move are considered in Section 7.

       Underpin on annual increase in basic pension

4.6    In 2001, the current Government announced its policy whereby the basic
       retirement pension would be increased each year in line with the rise in
       prices, subject to a minimum increase of 2.5% each year. In addition, a
       minimum increase of £100 per annum was applied in 2003/04 (thereafter an
       increase of 2.5% should always be more than £100 per annum).

4.7    Provision for this minimum increase has not been incorporated into legislation
       and therefore the main results of this review have not taken it into account.
       However, the application of the minimum increase will impose additional costs
       on the National Insurance Fund over and above the cost of providing
       increases in line with prices only. This is because, even if prices increase on
       average by 2.5% a year, as assumed in this review, in some years inflation
       will be more than, and in some years less than, 2.5%. In those years where
       inflation is less than 2.5% the minimum increase would be activated and the
       effect of this will be that on average the basic pension will increase by more
       than prices. Section 6 illustrates the potential additional cost of providing this
       minimum increase.

       Rate of earnings increases in excess of prices

4.8    When considering the price uprating scenario, the assumption regarding the
       rate of earnings growth in excess of prices (real earnings growth) is critical.
       This is because contribution income grows broadly in line with earnings
       (although the link to earnings will become weaker as increasing numbers of
       contributors have earnings above the UEL). In contrast, benefit expenditure
       would only grow in line with prices (other things being equal). The higher the
       assumed rate of real earnings growth assumed in the review, the lower will be
       the contribution rates shown by the review. A relatively small change in this
       assumption can have a large impact on the projected future contribution rates
       to the National Insurance Fund.

4.9    The historical rate of real earnings growth over the last 50 years has been
       quite variable: there are some periods of high growth (in excess of 2% per
       annum) and others of much lower growth (less than 1.5% per annum). In
       view of this past variation and the importance of the assumption, the principal
       results in Section 5 include two sets of figures: one assuming real earnings
       growth will average 1.5% per annum and another assuming 2% per annum.

4.10   However, in some cases results have only been given on the basis of 2% per
       annum real earnings growth.          This was done in order to avoid
       overcomplicating the report and should not be regarded as implying that 2%
       per annum growth is any more likely than 1.5% per annum growth.

       Rate of real investment return

4.11   An assumption about the investment return is needed in order to make
       projections of the balance of the National Insurance Fund. However this
       assumption does not affect either the projected expenditure or the projected
       contribution rates required to balance income and expenditure in each year.
       For the purpose of this review, the rate of investment return in excess of price
       inflation was assumed to be 3.5% per annum. This assumption is a
       reasonable assumption for the long-term yield on index-linked gilts having
       regard to experience in other countries (although current yields are rather
       lower than this because of particular features of the current market).

       Numbers contracted-out

4.12   Another important assumption relates to the assumed numbers of employees
       who are contracted-out of the State Second Pension. The previous
       Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund included assumptions
       about contracting out which were based on data for 1995/96 published in the
       DSS publication “Second Tier Pension Provision”. These assumptions were
       subsequently adjusted to reflect the potential increase in the level of
       contracting out as a result of the introduction of stakeholder pension schemes
       (see the “Report by the Government Actuary on the Financial Effects on the
       National Insurance Fund of the Child Support, Pensions and Social Security
       Bill 1999”, Cm 4573).

       contracted-out was based on the original NIRS1 administration system and
       covered the year 1996/97. However, importantly, this was before the
       introduction of age-related contracting out rebates from April 1997, which
       could have had a significant effect on those contracting out through personal
       pensions and money purchase occupational pension schemes. The NIRS2
       system continues to hold details of the numbers contracted-out but its
       introduction resulted in the data being structured differently from that which

       about the numbers of people contracting out after 1996/97. Such information
       is expected to be published soon, but even then there will remain
       considerable doubt over how the numbers are likely to change in future.

          Terms in which results are shown

4.16      The projected income and expenditure shown in Sections 5 to 8 have been
          shown in constant 2003/04 price terms, except where stated otherwise.

          and are consistent with the figures given in the Fund accounts for that year.

          Sensitivity of the results to the assumptions made

4.17      The results of the review are sensitive to the assumptions made. The
          assumptions adopted form a reasonable basis for the review. However, in
          practice, it is not possible to predict the future with any certainty and therefore
          the Fund’s experience will differ from the assumptions made. This, in turn,
          will mean that the Fund’s financial progress will differ from that projected by
          the review. For this reason, it is important to consider the effect on the results
          of the review of making changes to the assumptions (see Section 8).

4.18      The Fund’s finances, and the assumptions adopted, should also be kept
          under regular review. At future reviews, differences between experience and
          assumptions made for the previous review will be analysed and taken into
          account in revising the assumptions.

          Financing approach

4.19      In common with many social security schemes, the National Insurance Fund
          is financed on a pay-as-you-go basis. This means that contributions in one
          year are set in order to meet expenditure in that year and there is therefore no
          need to build up a significant fund of assets out of which to pay future
          benefits. Nevertheless, differences between the income and expenditure of
          the National Insurance Fund have allowed a balance to build up, and as at 31
          March 2002 it represented approximately 47%7 of the annual total
          expenditure in 2001/02. This fund is invested in a range of Government
          securities.

4.20      It will be seen in Section 5 of this report that the contribution rates required
          under an earnings uprating approach are projected to increase quite
          significantly over the next 30 years. Some countries, for example Canada,
          are attempting to build up a significant fund of assets within their social
          security systems in order to help limit the future increases in the rate of
          required contributions. However, in accordance with current Government
          policy, the results shown in this report have been prepared on the pay-as-
          you-go basis, without any allowance for advance funding. A projection of how
          the existing fund is likely to develop if the existing contribution rates are
          maintained has been included in Section 5.

5.3    The above tables demonstrate that, considered in constant price terms,
       expenditure from the National Insurance Fund is projected to increase
       significantly over the next 60 years, whether a price or an earnings uprating
       policy is adopted for increasing benefits and earnings limits. This reflects the
       fact that, over the period, the numbers in the population over pension age are
       expected to increase substantially, rising from 10.6 million in 2001 to 14.8
       million in 2060. The build up of entitlements to additional pension (SERPS
       and State Second Pension), the falling proportions that are contracted-out
       and the growing pension rights of women also lead to an increase in
       expenditure.

5.4    Although expenditure increases under both uprating scenarios, the increase
       is much greater in the case of earnings uprating, reflecting the higher rate of
       benefits that would be payable in that case. For example, under earnings
       uprating, the level of the basic retirement pension in 2060 would be over three
       times as much as the equivalent pension under price uprating, based on 2%
       per annum real earnings growth.

5.5    However, the difference in expenditure on additional (earnings-related)
       retirement pension under price and earnings uprating is relatively small.
       There are two reasons for this. Firstly, under both scenarios, additional
       pension once in payment is assumed to increase in line with prices. This has
       been the policy since the introduction of SERPS in 1978 and is consistent
       with standard practice when occupational pension schemes provide salary-
       related pensions. Secondly, although increasing the Upper Earnings Limit
       (UEL) by more than prices will lead to higher State Second Pension accruals
       (for those with earnings above that limit), the increase in the Lower Earnings
       Limit (LEL) by more than prices will offset this by reducing the earnings on
       which everyone earns additional pension.

5.6    The expenditure is higher under the assumption of 2% per annum real
       earnings growth than it is with 1.5% per annum growth. In the case of price

       (earnings-related) retirement pension, since the expenditure on other benefits
       is largely unaffected by the assumption about the increase in real earnings.

       pension (both SERPS and State Second Pension) and the larger pensions

          earned by women. The position is very different with earnings uprating:
          expenditure increases as a percentage of GDP because there is no reduction
          in benefit levels relative to earnings.

5.9       The pattern of contracted-out rebates as a percentage of GDP is different,
          since, in contrast to retirement pension spending, the rebates are paid while
          the individual is employed rather than a pensioner. Table 5.5 shows that, with
          price uprating, the rebates as a percentage of GDP fall from 0.9% to 0.4%
          over the period of the projection. The main reason for this is the assumption
          of a lower incidence of contracting out in future. There is assumed to be a
          reduction of 25% in membership of contracted-out salary-related (COSR)
          pension schemes and of 50% in the case of contracted-out money purchase
          (COMP) schemes. In addition, it is assumed that contracting out through
          appropriate personal pensions (APPs) and stakeholder plans will reduce by
          around half. A further cause of the reduced expenditure on rebates (as a
          percentage of GDP) is that, under price uprating, the band of earnings
          (between the LEL and UEL) on which rebates are calculated, reduces relative
          to earnings (and GDP). With earnings uprating, the fall in rebates as a
          percentage of GDP is less pronounced since the LEL and UEL are assumed
          to rise in line with earnings rather than prices.

5.10      Overall, the Fund expenditure and the contracted-out rebates fall as a
          percentage of GDP under price uprating but rise under earnings uprating.

5.11      A similar picture is shown in Table 5.6 which gives the equivalent figures with

          growth means that benefit levels decline more quickly relative to earnings
          (and GDP). Under earnings uprating the expenditure figures are very similar
          whether the earnings growth assumption is 1.5% or 2% per annum. The
          rebates as a percentage of GDP are also similar whether 1.5% or 2% real
          earnings growth is used; this is because both rebate expenditure and GDP

           which contribution income is largely linked). The impact of this is however
           partially offset by factors including the increasing additional pension
           entitlements (both SERPS and State Second Pension) and greater pension
           entitlements for women. Up to 2030, there is a rise in the number of
           pensioners per contributor and this also tends to increase the required
           contribution rate.

5.29       The same factors influence the progression of the contribution rate under
           earnings uprating in Table 5.11, except that in this case benefit levels do not
           decline relative to earnings.      As a result the contribution rate rises
           continuously over the projection period.

           per annum growth in real earnings are lower than those based on 1.5% per
           annum growth under price uprating. This is because the higher earnings
           growth assumption leads to a higher contribution base but has a much
           smaller effect on the expenditure. With earnings uprating, the contribution
           rate is relatively insensitive to the assumed rate of real earnings growth.

5.31       The projected future increase in the required contribution rate under earnings
           uprating can be seen in the context of contribution rates that have already
           increased substantially over the past 50 years. In the early 1950s, a man on
           average earnings and his employer were together paying about 5% of his
           earnings in National Insurance contributions (excluding contributions to the
           industrial injuries fund and those allocated to the NHS)16. By the 1980s this
           had grown to nearly 18%, although it has since fallen back to stand at about
           16% in 2003/04.17

6          UNDERPIN ON UPRATING OF BASIC PENSION


6.1        As mentioned in Section 4, current Government policy is to increase the basic
           retirement pension each year in line with the rise in prices, subject to a
           minimum increase of 2.5% each year.

6.2        Provision for this minimum increase has not been incorporated into legislation
           and therefore the results of the review given in Section 5 do not take it into
           account. However, the application of the minimum increase will impose
           potential additional costs on the National Insurance Fund over and above the
           cost of providing increases in line with prices only. Over the long-term these
           extra costs could be substantial, if price inflation remains modest.

6.3        The reason for the additional costs is that, even if prices increase on average
           by 2.5% a year, in some years the increase will be more than, and in some
           years less than, 2.5%. In those years where inflation is less than 2.5% the
           minimum increase would be activated and the effect of this will be that, on
           average, the basic pension will increase by more than prices. The amount by
           which the increase exceeds price inflation will depend on how often annual
           inflation falls below 2.5%.

           relative to its average level (see the final column of the table). This
           demonstrates that the past variation in price inflation is very dependent on the
           period under consideration, although over recent periods inflation has been
           much more stable than previously.

7         STATE SECOND PENSION AND CONTRACTING OUT


7.1       The results in Section 5 show that a substantial part of expenditure from the
          National Insurance Fund relates to the additional earnings-related pension.
          Up to March 2002, the additional pension was earned under SERPS (the
          State Earnings-Related Pension Scheme). From April 2002, the pension has
          become known as the State Second Pension, which is a modified version of
          SERPS. Only employees (i.e. not the self-employed) earn the additional
          earnings-related pension.

7.2       In broad terms, SERPS provides a pension related to the employee’s
          earnings between the Lower Earnings Limit (LEL) and Upper Earnings Limit
          (UEL) (“band earnings”). When first introduced in 1978, the aim was to
          provide a pension equal to 25% of the employee’s average band earnings.
          For this purpose earnings in years before retirement are indexed to average
          earnings in the economy in order to provide protection against inflation. The
          target pension was reduced to 20% of average band earnings from April
          1988, but there were special transitional provisions to protect entitlements
          built up before 1988 and of those employees who would reach pension age
          before 2010. Further details of how SERPS entitlements are calculated are
          given in Appendix A.

7.3       The State Second Pension that is earned from April 2002 is based on SERPS
          but incorporates some major changes. In particular, it allows those on low
          earnings to build up larger additional pension entitlements by:

          x   Doubling the rate of pension accrual (compared with SERPS) for earnings
              between the LEL and the Low Earnings Threshold (LET) (respectively
              £4,004 a year and £11,200 a year in 2003/04)

          x   Treating all those who earn more than the LEL but less than the LET as if
              they earned at the LET for the purpose of calculating their State Second
              Pension entitlement

7.4       In addition, the rate of pension accrual on earnings between the LET and the
          Upper Earnings Threshold (UET) (respectively £11,200 a year and £25,600 a
          year in 2003/04) is half that of SERPS. On earnings between the UET and
          the UEL (£30,940 a year in 2003/04) the pension accrues at the same rate as
          under SERPS. In broad terms, the net effect of this is that for those earning
          below the UET, the rate of pension accrual is higher than under SERPS,
          while for higher earners pension accrual is the same as that under SERPS.

7.5       The State Second Pension allows qualifying carers and long-term sick and
          disabled who meet certain requirements to build up an entitlement to
          additional pension. Further details of the State Second Pension are given in
          Appendix A.

          Contracting out

7.6       Employees who are members of contracted-out occupational pension
          schemes or who have taken out an appropriate (i.e. contracted-out) personal
          pension (APP) or stakeholder pension, do not earn the full State Second
          Pension (or SERPS before April 2002). The rules covering contracting out



                                              47
 Government Actuary’s Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund as at April 2000



       are complex and what follows is only a broad overview of the position
       applying from April 2002. Further details are given in Appendix A.

7.7    In the case of a member of a contracted-out salary-related (COSR)
       occupational pension scheme, the scheme must provide a minimum level of
       benefits which is deemed to replace part of the State Second Pension. In
       return for this, the employee and employer receive a reduction (“the
       contracted-out rebate”) in the amount of the National Insurance contributions
       they must pay. This rebate is designed to reflect the value of the SERPS the
       employee gives up as a result of being a member of a contracted-out pension
       scheme. On reaching pension age the employee’s State Second Pension is
       calculated as the pension he or she would have been entitled to if they had
       never been contracted-out less a contracted-out deduction (COD). The COD
       is broadly equal to the amount of SERPS (which for many employees will be
       less than the State Second Pension) they would have earned during the
       period for which they were contracted-out, had SERPS remained in force
       after March 2002.

7.8    The rules are slightly different for an employee with an APP or stakeholder
       plan. In this case, both the employer and the employee continue to pay the
       full rate of National Insurance contributions, but after the end of the tax-year,
       the State pays the contracted-out rebates as a contribution to the personal
       pension. This contribution is calculated on an age-related basis, such that it
       is broadly equivalent in value to the State Second Pension the employee
       would have earned in the year (together with an allowance for tax-relief). For
       this purpose no allowance is made for the uplift of earnings to the LET for
       those with earnings less than this level (see paragraph 7.3). There is also a
       limit placed on the rebate (expressed as a percentage of earnings) which
       means that, based on the assumptions adopted in the calculation, the
       contribution made for older employees is less than the value of the State
       Second Pension they would have earned.

7.9    In the same way as for members of COSR schemes, when the employee
       reaches pension age, the State Second Pension is calculated as the State
       Second Pension to which they would have been entitled had they never been
       contracted-out less the contracted-out deduction (COD). However, in this
       case, the COD is calculated as the State Second Pension they would have
       earned had they been contracted-in instead, ignoring the uplift of earnings to
       the LET.

7.10   The rules for members of contracted-out money purchase (COMP)
       occupational pension schemes incorporate elements of both the COSR
       scheme and personal pension rules. The employee and the employer receive
       an immediate rebate in their National Insurance contributions but the rebate
       for the employer is much smaller then it is for a COSR scheme member.
       However, after the end of the tax-year the State makes an age-related
       contribution into the occupational scheme. The method of calculating the
       State Second Pension at pension age is the same as that for a COSR
       scheme member.

7.11   The scale of contracted-out rebates is set by the Government on the advice of
       the Government Actuary, usually every five years. The current scale applies
       for the years from 2002/03 to 2006/07.

       Move to a flat-rate State Second Pension

7.29   The legislation that introduced the State Second Pension made provision for
       the move to a “flat-rate” structure for those reaching pension age after a
       certain date. This provision has not yet been implemented. Under a flat-rate
       structure, everyone would be treated as if they had earnings equal to the LET
       for the purpose of calculating their State Second Pension entitlement.
       However, for those people contracted-out, rebates and benefit “top-ups” after
       retirement would continue to be calculated in the same way as currently (i.e.
       based on the earnings-related pension). This would mean that, for those
       earning over the LET, the rebate would, on the assumptions underlying its
       calculation, be more valuable than the State Second Pension forgone. As a
       result, most employees earning more than the LET are likely to wish to
       contract out.

7.30   In the Green Paper “Simplicity, security and choice: Working and saving for
       retirement” the Government indicated that it would keep under review the
       possibility of moving to a flat-rate structure, taking into account wider
       developments in pension provision. The figures in Table 7.7 show the
       financial impact of a move to flat-rate structure which for this purpose is

       45 in 2006/07 with earnings over the LET will be contracted-out through an

       contracted-out through a COMP or COSR scheme).

8         EFFECT OF VARYING THE ASSUMPTIONS


8.1       Any estimates of future expenditure and contribution income for the National
          Insurance Fund, especially those made far into the future, are inevitably
          subject to wide margins of error. The projections which have been carried out
          for the review depend on a large number of assumptions that have been
          made. It is certain that future experience will differ, to some degree, from
          these assumptions. This is the reason for the legislative requirement to keep
          the long-term financial position of the National Insurance Fund under regular
          review.

8.2       When considering the results of the review, it is vital that not too much
          emphasis should be placed on a single set of estimates. Instead, it is
          important to consider the potential effects on the future financial position of
          the Fund if actual experience differs from the assumptions which underlie the
          main projections. This Section illustrates the effect on the principal results of
          the review of varying certain assumptions. The assumptions considered in
          this section are those which are the major sources of uncertainty in terms of
          their impact on the required future contribution rates.

8.3       The main results in Section 5 of this report show the effect of assuming either
          price or earnings uprating of flat-rate benefit rates and earnings limits, and the
          effect of assuming either 1.5% or 2% per annum real earnings growth.
          Section 7 illustrates the effect of varying the assumed level of contracting out
          of the State Second Pension. These assumptions are therefore not
          considered further in this Section. For simplicity of presentation, the results in
          this section are based on an assumption of 2% per annum real earnings
          growth.

       Employment and unemployment factors

8.11   The principal estimates in Section 5 are based on the economic activity rates
       described in Appendix E. The changes in activity rates over time reflect many
       demographic, economic and social factors. In general, the trends over the
       past 20 years have been towards lower activity rates for men, particularly at
       ages over 55, and higher activity rates for women. It is possible that some of
       these trends may be reversed in the future, particularly for economic activity
       rates at older ages.

8.12   In order to consider the possible implications of this, results have been
       recalculated approximately on the basis that the activity rates are 10
       percentage points higher for those over age 55 and 20 percentage points for
       those over age 60, than the rates assumed for the principal estimates. The
       effect of the increase in economic activity would be to increase contribution
       income to the Fund. However, this would be offset by increased expenditure,
       particularly on contracted-out rebates, Incapacity Benefit and the additional
       component of retirement pension. As a result, the overall effect on the



                                              61
 Government Actuary’s Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund as at April 2000



       required joint Class 1 pay-as-you-go contribution rate under price uprating in
       2060/61 would be small.

8.13   The principal results are based on the assumption that the rate of
       unemployment (based on the ILO definition) remains at about 5% throughout
       the period of the projections. If unemployment were higher, there would be
       lower contribution income due to the lower number of people in employment,
       increased expenditure on contributory Jobseeker’s Allowance and reduced
       expenditure on some other benefits (and rebates). If unemployment were
       assumed to be 50% higher than for the principal estimates (i.e. a rate of about
       7.5%), the required joint Class 1 pay-as-you-go contribution rate under price
       uprating in 2060/61 would increase by approximately 0.2%.

       Investment return and Fund projections

8.14   The projected balance in the Fund which was shown in Section 5 was based
       on an assumed future rate of investment return of 3.5% per annum in excess
       of price increases. The projected balance in the Fund in the short- to
       medium-term is not very sensitive to this assumption, as investment income
       comprises a relatively small proportion of the Fund’s total income. For
       example, in the principal projections, the Fund is projected to be extinguished
       in 2019/20 with earnings uprating, assuming 2% per annum real earnings
       growth. If the assumed rate of investment return from 2003/04 onwards were
       2% per annum higher or lower than in the principal projection, the Fund would
       be expected to be extinguished two years later or earlier respectively.

8.15   Although the projected balance in the Fund is not very sensitive to the
       assumed rate of investment return, it is very sensitive to other assumptions.
       The balance in the Fund depends on the excess income or expenditure in
       each year, which in turn depends on the difference between two large
       amounts: annual income and annual expenditure. A small percentage
       difference in either income or expenditure can result in a large percentage
       difference in the excess income or expenditure in the year, leading to a large
       change in the balance in the Fund.

8.16   For example, if contribution income were 5% lower in each year from 2003/04
       onwards than in the principal projections, with earnings uprating and
       assuming 2% per annum real earnings growth, the Fund would be projected
       to be extinguished in 2011/12, 8 years earlier than in the principal projections.

8.17   This section of the report has considered the effects on the principal results of
       the review of varying certain assumptions in isolation. A combination of such
       effects may be likely to occur in the future. Additionally, the variations in
       assumptions considered in this section do not represent the boundaries of
       likely experience. For example, it is not unreasonable to suggest that future
       migration may be either higher than that assumed in the high migration
       variant, or lower than that assumed in the low migration variant. For these
       two reasons, the results in this section should not be considered to illustrate
       minimum or maximum limits for the future financial position of the Fund.

       pension from the National Insurance Fund will be less dependent on the

9.11       These figures show the narrowing range of pension outcomes relative to
           earnings under State Second Pension compared with SERPS. The reason
           for this is that the amount of the Second State Pension earned in each year is
           much less dependent on earnings than SERPS (even ignoring the explicit

           earnings (see paragraph 10.44). Furthermore, under current policy, earnings

           band of earnings. From this point onwards, the State Second Pension will
           accrue on an entirely flat-rate basis with a rate of accrual double that of the
           SERPS scheme.

10     APPENDIX A: SUMMARY OF CONTRIBUTIONS AND BENEFITS


10.1   This appendix summarises the principal provisions regarding National
       Insurance contributions and benefits in the Social Security Acts and
       Regulations on which the estimates for future years in this review are based.
       It concentrates on those aspects of contribution liability and benefit
       entitlement that are most significant in financial terms and does not attempt to
       give a definitive description of the rules.

       Classes of contributions

10.2   For the purpose of assessing contribution liability, the population of Great
       Britain over school-leaving age and under pension age is divided into three
       main categories:

       A.      employed persons, i.e. all persons gainfully occupied under a contract of
               service or as an office-holder, and certain other persons;

       B.      self-employed persons;

       C.      other persons, not employed or with low earnings.

10.3   Employed and self-employed persons whose earnings are above specified
       minima are required to contribute to the National Insurance Fund. Those with
       earnings below these minima and non-employed persons may do so if they
       wish.

10.4   Employed persons who have earnings above the Primary Threshold pay
       earnings-related Class 1 primary contributions; their employers pay earnings-
       related Class 1 secondary contributions if the employee earns above the
       Secondary Threshold. Employees pay the main rate of contributions up to an
       Upper Earnings Limit and at a lower rate on earnings above this. Employers
       pay contributions on all their employees' earnings above the Secondary
       Threshold. No Class 1 primary contributions are payable by those at work
       after pensionable age (currently 65 for men and 60 for women) but the
       employer is still required to pay Class 1 secondary contributions. Class 1A
       contributions are payable by employers in respect of certain benefits in kind
       received by people liable to Class 1 contributions. Class 1B contributions are
       payable by employers in respect of PAYE settlement agreements.

10.5   Certain employed married women and widows, who exercised an option
       before 11 May 1977, have a reserved right to pay a reduced rate of contri-
       bution on earnings up to the Upper Earnings Limit (UEL); their employers pay
       full Class 1 secondary contributions in respect of them. They may be entitled
       to receive Statutory Sick Pay and Statutory Maternity Pay but are not entitled
       to other benefits, except where title arises through their husband’s insurance.

10.6   Self-employed persons who have not been granted exemption on account of
       low earnings, pay flat-rate Class 2 contributions and, if they have profits
       above the Lower Profits Limit (LPL), earnings-related Class 4 contributions.
       The self-employed pay the main rate of Class 4 contributions on profits up to
       the Upper Profits Limit (UPL) and at a lower rate on profits above this level.

       Treasury grant

10.21 The Treasury Grant was introduced by the Social Security Act 1993. It
      enables money provided by Parliament to be paid into the National Insurance
      Fund if the Treasury (formerly the Secretary of State for the Department of
      Social Security, subject to Treasury consent) considers it expedient to do so
      to maintain the level of the Fund. It is subject to a maximum of 17% of benefit
      expenditure in the year. The amount paid in any financial year is based on
      estimates, made by the Government Actuary prior to the start of the year, of
      the expected financial position of the Fund at the end of the year.

       Contribution conditions and eligibility

10.22 Full rate Class 1 contributors are eligible for all benefits provided by the
      scheme. Eligibility is generally determined by reference to whether a person
      received (or was treated as having received) qualifying earnings for the year
      that were at least 52 times the weekly Lower Earnings Limit (LEL). This is
      called a qualifying year. In certain circumstances, an individual may be
      credited with a qualifying year even though they did not have earnings over
      the LEL (see paragraph 10.26).

10.23 As already noted, since April 1999 for employers and April 2000 for
      employees, liability to pay National Insurance contributions only starts when
      earnings reach the earnings threshold, rather than the LEL. Despite this
      change, eligibility for benefits, SERPS/State Second Pension entitlement and
      contracted-out rebates are still assessed with reference to earnings between
      the LEL and UEL.

       Basic retirement pension

10.31 The standard rate of Category A basic pension is currently payable to male
      contributors from age 65 and to female contributors from age 60 (rising in
      stages to age 65 between the years 2010 and 2020), subject to the
      contribution conditions being satisfied. For the pension to be paid at the full

       Additional earnings-related pension (State Second Pension and SERPS)

10.37 Since April 1978, employees have been able to earn an additional earnings-
      related pension. The pension earned up to April 2002 is calculated under the
      State Earnings-Related Pensions Scheme (SERPS). From 6 April 2002, the
      pension is known as the State Second Pension, which, although based on the
      SERPS structure, incorporates some important revisions.           The main
      provisions of SERPS are described, followed by a description of how these
      provisions are modified for the State Second Pension.

10.38 Employees who have earnings over the annual Lower Earnings Limit (LEL) in
      any year in the period from April 1978 to April 2002 are entitled to an
      earnings-related additional pension (SERPS). The earnings in any tax-year
      between the LEL and Upper Earnings Limit (UEL) for that year are revalued in
      line with the increases in the general level of earnings, up to the year before
      that in which pension age is attained. A slightly different approach applied for
      new awards of pension before April 2000, whereby the earnings were
      revalued before deducting the Lower Earnings Limit, but then the limit for the
      final year was deducted from the revalued earnings.

10.39 Under SERPS as originally enacted in the Social Security Pensions Act 1975,
      the aim of the system was to provide a pension equal to 25% of revalued
      average earnings, considering the highest 20 years’ earnings from the
      working life when calculating this average. Under the Social Security Act
      1986, this was changed, so that SERPS would ultimately provide a pension
      equal to 20% of revalued average earnings, and only after a full working life.
      Therefore the rate of accrual each year would eventually be 20/N%, where N
      is the number of years in the working life, but with transitional provisions to
      protect the entitlements of certain groups. For the purpose of calculating
      SERPS, working life is the number of tax years from the year of attaining age
      16 (or 1978/79 if later) up to the year before attaining pension age, subject to
      a minimum of 20.

10.40 Under these transitional rules, the accrual rate remains 25/N% in respect of
      earnings up to April 1988. The accrual rate in respect of earnings from April
      1988 depends on the year in which pension age is attained. Those retiring
      before April 2000 will receive a pension based on an accrual rate of 25/N%
      for post-1988 earnings. For those retiring from April 2000 up to 2009 there is
      a phased reduction in the accrual rate from 25/N% to 20/N%. For later
      retirements, the accrual rate for post-1988 earnings will be the ultimate rate of

       x   Treating qualifying carers and people with long-term disabilities who have
           no earnings above the Lower Earnings Limit, as if they had earnings at
           the level of the LET.

10.42 The State Second Pension accrues on earnings (actual or deemed) between
      the LEL and UEL (as for SERPS). However, unlike SERPS, earnings in this
      range are divided into three bands. Band 1 runs from the LEL to the Low
      Earnings Threshold (LET). Band 2 runs from the LET to the amount equal to
      (3 x LET less 2 x LEL), often known as the Upper Earnings Threshold (or
      UET). Band 3 covers the remaining earnings up to the Upper Earnings Limit.
      The LET and UET are £11,200 and £25,600 a year respectively in 2003/04.

10.43 These bands determine the rate of pension accrual under the State Second
      Pension: the rate of accrual is double, half and equal to the SERPS accrual
      rate on earnings in Bands 1, 2 and 3 respectively. This means that for all
      those who have earnings above the top of Band 2, the pension they earn is
      broadly the same as that which they would have earned under SERPS. For
      those with earnings below the top of Band 2, their State Second Pension will
      be greater than the SERPS they would have earned.

10.44 People who earn in Band 1 will be treated as though they had earnings at the
      LET. This creates a significant breakpoint: those earning just below the LEL
      accrue no State Second Pension, whereas those who earn at the LEL have
      their pension calculated based on earnings equal to the LET.

       Contracting out

10.49 Members of occupational pension schemes meeting required conditions may
      be contracted-out of SERPS/State Second Pension. In that case, both the
      employees and their employers are allowed a reduction (“rebate”) in the rate
      of National Insurance contributions payable on the band of earnings between
      the LEL and UEL, as described in paragraph 10.17. The system of
      contracting out changed from April 1997 based on the provisions of the
      Pensions Act 1995, and again in April 2002 in order to take into account the
      introduction of the State Second Pension.

10.50 In respect of accruals of pension for periods prior to April 1997, contracted-
      out defined benefit pension schemes are required to provide Guaranteed
      Minimum Pensions (GMPs) at age 60 for women and age 65 for men, and to
      a spouse on the death of a member. Occupational schemes operating on a
      defined contribution basis could also be used for contracting out provided the
      contributions made to the scheme in respect of employees were not less than
      the amount of the contracted-out National Insurance rebate and provided that
      the pensions from the scheme satisfied certain requirements.

10.51 From April 1988, employees not contracted-out under an occupational
      pension scheme could choose to effect a personal pension arrangement that
      is approved for the purposes of contracting out. In this case, the individual
      and their employers will pay the full National Insurance contributions
      applicable to those who are not contracted-out.          A contribution will
      subsequently be paid by the State into the personal pension arrangement
      chosen by the individual, equal to the contribution rebate for contracted-out
      members of occupational pension schemes (together with an allowance for
      tax-relief on the employee’s share and in some cases an additional incentive).

10.52 The GMPs which schemes have to provide in respect of periods up to April
      1997 are broadly equivalent to the SERPS pension that the individual would
      have earned had they not been contracted-out. SERPS pension payable
      from the National Insurance Fund is reduced by the amount of any
      contracted-out deductions (COD) (which is of a similar amount to the GMP) in
      respect of any period of contracted-out employment. The occupational
      pension scheme is required to uprate GMPs accrued after April 1988, when in
      payment, in line with the increase in prices, up to a limit of 3% each year. No
      increases are required for GMPs that accrued before April 1988.

10.53 For members of defined contribution occupational pension schemes and
      those with approved personal pensions, the SERPS pension payable from the
      National Insurance Fund is reduced by a COD in respect of the period up to
      April 1997. This is calculated in the same way as the COD for a member of a
      contracted-out defined benefit occupational pension scheme (even though
      there is no GMP in a defined contribution scheme).

10.54 A GMP at half of the rate payable to the contributor must be provided by
      contracted-out defined benefit schemes to surviving widows in respect of
      accruals from April 1978 and to widowers in respect of accruals from April
      1988 onwards. Where appropriate, CODs will be deducted from benefits
      payable from the National Insurance Fund to widows and widowers. In the
      case of surviving spouses of members of defined contribution schemes or
      holders of approved personal pensions, a COD (which may in certain



                                           87
 Government Actuary’s Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund as at April 2000



       circumstances be equivalent to 100% of the COD for the contributor) will also
       be deducted from the National Insurance Fund benefit.

10.55 A slightly amended system of contracting out operated in the period between
      April 1997 and April 2002. Someone who was contracted-out in this period
      (whether as a member of an occupational pension scheme or an approved
      personal pension) will not receive any SERPS in respect of these years of
      accrual. The earnings-related element of their State retirement pension is
      deemed to be provided in full from the occupational pension scheme or
      personal pension and these arrangements will be responsible for providing
      increases in pensions in payment in line with movements in prices, up to a
      limit of 5% per annum. In order to be contracted-out, a defined benefit
      occupational pension scheme did not have to provide a GMP, but rather had
      to satisfy a minimum quality of benefits test (broadly a pension of one
      eightieth of earnings for each year of service payable from age 65). The rules
      for contracting out via a personal pension remained broadly as they had been
      prior to April 1997, but the amount of the contributions paid by the State to the
      individual’s pension arrangement became age-related. Similar rules were
      introduced for contracted-out defined contribution occupational schemes.

10.56 The rules changed again from April 2002 when the State Second Pension
      was introduced.     The rebates in National Insurance contributions for
      contracted-out members of occupational pension schemes are calculated in
      the same way as before, reflecting the rates of pension accrual under
      SERPS. However, such members may also receive a State Second Pension
      top-up. The top-up is calculated, for each year of accrual for which they are
      contracted-out, as the difference between what their State Second Pension
      would be (based on the LET if their earnings are less than this) and their
      SERPS entitlement, assuming in each case that they were not contracted-out.

10.57 Employees who are contracted-out through an appropriate personal pension
      continue to pay National Insurance contributions at the full, not contracted-out
      rate. However, the contributions subsequently paid by the State to the
      personal pension are calculated based on the revised rate of pension accrual
      under the State Second Pension, but do not allow for the boost of earnings to
      the LET for those with earnings less than this. Therefore, the State’s
      contributions to the personal pension will be twice, half or equal to those
      applicable under SERPS for earnings in Bands 1, 2 and 3 respectively.
      Members who earn less than the LET will also receive a State Second
      Pension top-up calculated as the difference between the State Second
      Pension based on the LET and that based on actual earnings.

       Bereavement benefits

10.58 The bereavement benefits comprise Bereavement Payment, Widowed
      Parent’s Allowance and Bereavement Allowance. The entitlement is based
      on the contributions paid by or credited to the deceased spouse and is
      broadly subject to similar conditions as for retirement pension, adjusted for
      the actual age of the deceased at death. For awards from April 2001,
      bereavement benefits are not awarded after pension age and any benefit
      already in payment is usually converted to retirement pension at pension age.
      Prior to April 2001, the rules were different; in particular there were no
      benefits for widowers. Cases in force at April 2001 were transitionally
      protected and may continue to receive widows’ benefits up to age 64. The
      following paragraphs describe the main rules governing the benefits payable


                                           88
 Government Actuary’s Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund as at April 2000



       to widows and widowers who were widowed on or after 9 April 2001, and to
       widowers widowed before April 2001 and who had a child.

10.59 A Bereavement Payment of £2,000 (this amount is not uprated each year) is
      payable immediately on bereavement if the surviving spouse is under pension
      age or the deceased spouse was under pension age or not entitled to a
      Category A retirement pension. This is followed by Widowed Parent's
      Allowance of up to £77.45 per week from April 2003 where the widow or
      widower has a qualifying child eligible for, or with underlying entitlement to,
      Child Benefit. An addition of £9.55 is paid for the first dependent child and
      £11.35 per week is paid in respect of each subsequent dependent child of
      someone in receipt of Widowed Parent's Allowance. Child dependency
      increases have not been awarded since April 2003, although those in
      payment at that date were transitionally protected. Reduced rates of benefit
      are paid where the contribution record is deficient.

10.60 Where no Widowed Parent’s Allowance is payable, a Bereavement Allowance
      of up to £77.45 per week from April 2003 is paid, for 12 months only, to those
      aged between 45 and pension age at bereavement. The Bereavement
      Allowance is payable on a sliding scale of between 30% and 93% of the full
      rate for those aged between 45 and 54 at bereavement, and at the full rate for
      age 55 and above. Reduced rates of benefit are paid where the contribution
      record is deficient.

10.61 An earnings-related additional spouse’s pension is payable, along with
      Widowed Parent’s Allowance, in respect of earnings on which contributions
      were paid by the deceased spouse since April 1978. This additional pension
      is calculated in broadly the same way as SERPS or the State Second
      Pension, but assuming the person reached pension age in the year of death.
      The proportion of the pension inherited by the spouse is the same as for
      retirement pension (see paragraph 10.47), which in practice means that most
      awards of earnings-related additional pension with Widowed Parent’s
      Allowance from October 2002 will be 50% of that accrued by the deceased.

10.62 No earnings-related spouse’s pension is paid with Bereavement Allowance.
      However a widow or widower who has qualified for Widowed Parent’s
      Allowance or Bereavement Allowance, and who has not subsequently re-
      married, may inherit an additional pension on reaching pension age, based on
      the deceased spouse’s contributions, at the appropriate rate between 100%
      and 50% depending on the deceased spouse’s age at October 2002. This is
      also adjusted for the age at bereavement or cessation of Widowed Parent’s
      Allowance. The additional pension is reduced to reflect any period of
      contracted-out employment.

       Contributory Jobseeker’s Allowance

10.63 Jobseeker’s Allowance replaced Unemployment Benefit in October 1996 with
      some transitional protection. The rate of contributory Jobseeker’s Allowance
      is £54.65 per week from April 2003 for those aged 25 and over, £43.25 per
      week from April 2003 for those aged 18 to 24 and £32.90 for those under 18.
      Class 1 contributions at the level of 25 times the Lower Earnings Limit must
      have been paid in at least one of the last two contribution years before the
      claim and both years must be qualifying years by virtue of contributions
      and/or credits. The benefit is payable after 3 waiting days for up to 6 months
      in any 2 year period of interruption of employment in a single spell or linked

       spells, provided the contribution and other conditions are satisfied. After
       benefit has been paid for 6 months it cannot be paid again until the claimant
       has requalified by working as an employee for at least 16 hours in each of at
       least 13 weeks in the 26 weeks before benefit is reclaimed and is based on a
       later contribution period.

       Incapacity benefit

10.64 Incapacity Benefit (IB) replaced sickness and invalidity benefits for new
      awards from 13 April 1995. In broad terms, people in receipt of Invalidity
      Benefit (IVB) on that date continue to have their entitlement calculated on the
      old rules for as long as they remain entitled. The contribution conditions and
      rules were altered for new IB claims from April 2001. The paragraphs below
      set out the conditions for new claims after that date. Existing cases were
      subject to the old rules and are fully transitionally protected.

10.65 IB is paid in respect of a claimant who is unable to work because of sickness
      or disability. The claimant must satisfy the contribution conditions: from April
      2001, contributions at the level of 25 times the LEL must have been paid in
      one of the last 3 years before the claim and both the last two contribution
      years must be qualifying years by virtue of contributions and/or credits.
      Those who are under age 20 when they claim IB have the contribution
      conditions waived so long as they otherwise satisfy the conditions for receipt
      of benefit. In certain circumstances other young people under 25 may qualify
      under this rule. IB is paid at three rates:

       A.      IB short-term lower rate at a personal rate of £54.40 per week from
               April 2003 for the first 28 weeks, after three waiting days, unless the
               claimant is an employee in receipt of Statutory Sick Pay (SSP).

       B.      IB short-term higher rate at a personal rate of £64.35 per week from
               April 2003 for cases between 29 and 52 weeks and immediately for
               those transferring from SSP after 168 days (28 weeks), provided they
               satisfy the contribution conditions.

       C.      IB long-term rate at a personal rate of £72.15 per week from April
               2003 thereafter as long as the claimant satisfies the “personal
               capability assessment” and is under pension age. For claimants who
               are aged under 35 at the start of the incapacity an age addition of
               £15.15 per week from April 2003 will be paid and for those aged 35 to
               44 an addition of £7.60 per week from April 2003 will be paid.

       For the first 28 weeks claimants are assessed against their ability to carry out
       the “normal occupation”. For those without a regular occupation, the personal
       capability assessment will apply from the outset. For all other claimants, the
       assessment will apply after 28 weeks, although certain cases are exempt.

10.66 Increases may be paid with IB for an adult dependant who is caring for a
      dependent child or is aged 60 or over, provided their earnings are less than
      the adult dependency addition. The rate is £33.65 per week from April 2003
      for claimants on the short-term rates, and £43.15 per week from April 2003 for
      those on the long-term rate. Child dependency increases may also be paid,
      but these have not been awarded since April 2003, although those in
      payment at that date were transitionally protected.

       Maternity Allowance

10.68 Maternity Allowance is paid to a woman expecting a child who has stopped
      work provided she has worked as an employee, with earnings over £30 per
      week but is not entitled to Statutory Maternity Pay (see paragraph 10.75), or
      has worked as a self-employed person, for at least 26 weeks in the 66 weeks
      ending with the week before the expected week of childbirth. The allowance
      is payable for a maximum of 26 weeks for confinements from April 2003 (18
      weeks prior to April 2003), starting at the earliest 11 weeks before the baby is
      due.

10.69 From April 2003, the amount payable will be £100 per week or, if lower, 90%
      of weekly earnings with a minimum of £27 per week for those earning at the
      Maternity Allowance Threshold (MAT) which is £30 a week from April 2003.
      Those who are self-employed and pay Class 2 contributions qualify for the
      standard rate of £100 per week; those who hold a Small Earnings Exception
      certificate are treated as earning at the MAT.

       Guardian’s Allowance

10.70 This allowance is payable at the rate of £11.55 per week per child in 2003/04,
      to a person who provides a home for, or maintains, a child whose parents are
      both dead. In certain circumstances only one parent need be dead. There
      are no contribution conditions but there is a residence test, and the guardian
      must be entitled to (or treated as being entitled to) Child Benefit in respect of
      the child.

10.71 Child Special Allowance (formerly paid in respect of children of divorced
      couples where the father has died) ceased in 2001.

       Christmas bonus for pensioners

10.72 This £10 Christmas bonus (which is not increased each year) is an annual
      tax-free payment and is paid with the qualifying benefit in the first week of
      December. It is paid to individuals present or ordinarily resident in the UK or
      any other EU member state, who are entitled to either retirement pension,
      Widowed Parent’s/Mother’s Allowance, Widow’s Pension, or long-term rate
      Incapacity Benefit, in the first week of December. Christmas bonus is also
      paid to recipients of certain other benefits, such as Disability Living Allowance
      and war widows pensions, although no allowance has been made for this
      since, in these cases, the bonus is not paid from the National Insurance Fund.

      employers) are then recovered by the employer by reducing the amount of
      National Insurance contributions to be paid. The benefits replace Incapacity

11.3   The Pensions Act 1995 also introduced age-related rebates for contracting

       a personal pension) and this new approach applied from April 1997. To

       from April 1999. The new employer contribution rate from April 1999 was

       with the Government’s intention, it has been aligned with the income tax
       personal allowance. The threshold in 2003/04 is £89 per week. The amount
       of employer National Insurance contributions payable to the NHS was
       unchanged at 0.9% of all earnings, but it was applied to all those earning over
       the Secondary Threshold (rather than the Lower Earnings Limit as
       previously).

11.6   The Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999 made changes to the way
       Class 1 primary (i.e. employee) contributions are calculated. From April
       2000, employees only pay contributions on earnings between the Primary
       Threshold and the Upper Earnings Limit. The Primary Threshold stands at
       £89 per week in 2003/04, having been aligned with the Secondary Threshold
       since April 2001.      Despite the changes to employer and employee
       contributions introduced by the Social Security Act 1998 and the Welfare
       Reform and Pensions Act 1999, entitlement to State benefits, the amount of
       SERPS/State Second Pension and the contracted-out rebate continue to be
       determined with reference to earnings between the Lower and Upper

14     APPENDIX E: METHODOLOGY AND ASSUMPTIONS


14.1   This Appendix summarises the methods and assumptions used in deriving
       the estimates of income and expenditure given in Section 5 and Appendix F
       of this report. Paragraphs 14.2 to 14.8 below describe the key economic
       assumptions used for the review. This is followed by a description of the
       approach used to estimate numbers of contributors and amounts of
       contribution income. The remaining paragraphs set out the assumptions
       underlying the estimates for retirement pensions and other benefits and for
       administration costs.

       Economic assumptions

14.2   The main economic assumptions used in this review are set out in the
       following table.

       Table 14.1: Economic assumptions used for the review

        Assumption
        Price inflation               2.5% per annum
        Real earnings growth          1.5% per annum and 2.0% per annum
        Real investment return        3.5% per annum


14.3   The assumed rate of price inflation is not a critical assumption and it has little
       effect on the results of the review. This is because nearly all items of income
       and expenditure are linked directly or indirectly to price inflation and the
       results have been expressed in constant price terms.

14.4   In contrast, the rate of earnings inflation in excess of prices (real earnings
       growth) is very important for those projections for which it is assumed that
       benefit rates and earnings limits increase in line with prices. This is because
       contribution income grows broadly in line with earnings (although the link to
       earnings will become weaker as increasing numbers of contributors have
       earnings above the Upper Earnings Limit), while benefit expenditure would
       only grow in line with prices (other things being equal). The higher the
       assumed rate of real earnings growth, the lower will be the contribution rates
       shown by the review. A relatively small change in this assumption can have a
       large impact on the projected future contribution rates to the National
       Insurance Fund.

14.5   The historical rate of real earnings growth over the last 50 years has been
       quite variable: there are some periods of high growth (in excess of 2% per
       annum) and others of much lower growth (less than 1.5% per annum). In
       view of this past variation and the importance of the assumption, the principal
       results in Section 5 include two sets of figures: one assuming real earnings
       growth will average 1.5% per annum and another assuming 2% per annum.

14.6   An assumption about the investment return is needed in order to make
       projections of the balance of the National Insurance Fund. However this
       assumption does not affect either the projected expenditure or the projected
       pay-as-you-go contribution rates required to balance income and expenditure



                                          102
 Government Actuary’s Quinquennial Review of the National Insurance Fund as at April 2000



       in each year. The assumption of a return of 3.5% per annum above price
       inflation is broadly consistent with the return earned on the Fund in recent
       years and is also a reasonable assumption for the long-term yield on index-
       linked gilts having regard to experience in other countries (although current
       yields are rather lower than this because of particular features of the current
       market).

       Earnings uprating and price uprating

14.7   The projections for the review have been carried out on two bases: price
       uprating and earnings uprating.

       Price            Under this approach all benefit rates are assumed to increase
       uprating         each year in line with the increase in the Retail Prices Index
                        (RPI). Earnings limits applied in determining contributions are
                        also assumed to increase in line with the RPI, except the Low
                        Earnings Threshold (LET), which, in accordance with
                        legislation, is assumed to rise in line with the growth in
                        average earnings.

                        However, since earnings are assumed to rise more quickly
                        than prices, the LET will in due course reach the Upper
                        Earnings Limit. This is projected to happen in 2055/56
                        allowing for 2% per annum real earnings growth and in
                        2072/73 (i.e. beyond the end of the projection period in
                        2060/61) with 1.5% per annum real earnings growth. From
                        that point onwards it is assumed that the Upper Earnings
                        Limit will rise in line with earnings, but that other earnings
                        limits will remain linked to prices.

       Earnings         Under this approach benefit rates are assumed to increase in
       uprating         line with the rise in average earnings, apart from any
                        SERPS/State Second Pension that is in payment, which is
                        assumed to rise in line with prices (which has always been
                        the practice since SERPS was introduced in April 1978).

                        Earnings limits for contributions purposes are also assumed
                        to rise in line with average earnings. The earnings threshold
                        for paying Class 1 contributions is currently aligned with the
                        single person’s tax allowance (SPTA), which under current
                        Government policy is increased in line with prices. Under
                        earnings uprating it is assumed that the earnings threshold
                        rises in line with earnings, which therefore implies a breaking
                        of the link between the threshold and the SPTA.

       In each case, the review has allowed for the actual rates of benefit and
       earnings limits up to and including 2003/04. For subsequent years the
       benefits and earnings limits have been increased in line with prices or
       earnings as discussed above.

14.8   The contracted-out deduction (COD) in calculating SERPS entitlements for
       those contracted-out between April 1988 and April 1997 is increased after
       pension age in line with prices up to a maximum of 3% each year. For this
       review, it has been assumed that these CODs would increase by 2.25% per
       annum on average. This is slightly less than the assumed rate of price

       inflation of 2.5% per annum, reflecting the fact that in some years inflation will
       exceed 3%, and therefore on average the COD will increase by less than
       prices.

       Contribution income

14.9   Estimates of future contribution income are derived separately for each
       contribution class. Actual known receipts in recent years are used to adjust
       modelled estimates for future years.

14.10 For the main contribution classes, the estimated contribution income was
      obtained by projecting the number of contributors in each class, allowing for
      demographic and other changes, and by applying this to the average incomes
      on which contributions are based in the class. These projections are carried
      out by age and sex. In the case of Class 1 contributions, separate projections
      were performed for gross contributions and contracted-out rebates.

14.11 A simpler approach was adopted for the less significant contribution classes.
      Class 1A and Class 1B contributions were based on information provided by
      the Inland Revenue and this was projected forward in line with the growth in
      earnings. Class 3 contributions were estimated by taking past data on
      amounts received and projecting forward in line with changes in the rate of
      Class 3 contributions.

      applying activity rates, varying according to age and sex, to the future

      economically active were broken down into employees, the self-employed,
      and the unemployed. Finally, employees and the self-employed were sub-
      divided according to whether or not they were liable to pay contributions.

       Benefit projections

14.31 Benefit expenditure is estimated separately for each of the contributory
      benefits, and separately for the basic flat-rate and the additional (SERPS and
      State Second Pension) elements. In general, for flat-rate benefits, numbers
      are estimated from recent data on beneficiaries projected forward with
      allowance for awards and cessations in future years based on past
      experience and taking into account demographic factors. The average rate of
      benefit is projected based on past data and observed trends, making
      allowance for dependency.

14.32 Additional pensions are projected in a slightly different way, by considering
      the earnings on which Class 1 contributions have been paid and applying
      mortality rates and revaluation factors up to pension age. At pension age, the
      revalued “survived” earnings are converted into pension amounts by dividing
      by the relevant accrual rates and this pension is run off in retirement in the
      normal way, allowing for the award of survivors’ benefits.

14.33 For retirement pension and bereavement benefits, adjustments are applied to
      align the estimates for recent years with statistical data.

14.35 The estimated expenditure on basic retirement pension was then obtained by
      multiplying the projected number of recipients by the estimated average
      amounts of basic retirement pension. In doing this, the first stage is to
      calculate the future cost in respect of those already retired (allowing for future
      deaths), who are in receipt of known amounts of pension. Average amounts
      are assumed to remain constant for each cohort, with allowance for higher
      rates payable to those who deferred receiving their pension and on award of
      widows' retirement pensions when a married male pensioner dies. To this
      estimate is then added the cost of pensions to future new awards based on
      the projections of the numbers of pensioners and the estimated average
      amounts at award for future cohorts of pensioners.

      97.3% of the standard Category A pension. This percentage is assumed to
      fall slowly, reaching 96.5% in 2020 and stabilising at this level. This reduction

           Additional pension (SERPS and State Second Pension)

14.48 Additional pension comprises SERPS which accrued from April 1978 to April
      2002 and the State Second Pension that has accrued since April 2002.
      Employees who are members of a contracted-out pension arrangement forgo
      all or part of their additional pension, which is deemed to be replaced by the
      pension from the contracted-out pension scheme. In order to quantify clearly
      the effects of contracting out, the projections have first been carried out
      assuming no employees are contracted-out. A deduction is then applied (“the
      contracted-out deduction” or COD36) in respect of employees who are
      assumed to be contracted-out and this represents the part of the additional
      pension to which they are not entitled as a result of being contracted-out.

14.49 The additional pension at award was calculated by first “surviving” the
      revalued earnings factors for each year with modified population mortality
      rates. For past years, the revalued earnings factors were based on actual
      earnings data and for future years the earnings factors were consistent with
      the earnings used in projecting Class 1 contribution income. Allowance was
      also made for accruals of credited earnings from 2002/03 onwards. The total
      additional pension at award in each future financial year was then calculated
      by multiplying the total revalued earnings factors by the relevant accrual
      rates. The projected expenditure in each future year from these amounts of
      new awards is then derived each year by allowing for mortality and survivors’
      benefits, taking account of the inheritance provisions described in paragraph
      10.47.

       Contracted-out deduction

14.51 As described in paragraph 14.48, the method adopted in projecting
      expenditure on additional pension is first to project the “gross” entitlement to
      additional pension, i.e. assuming that no one is contracted-out, and then to
      apply a “contracted-out deduction” (COD) which represents the additional
      pension employees do not earn as a result of being contracted-out.

14.52 The CODs were calculated in a similar manner to the estimates for additional
      pension described above, based on the earnings factors for the contracted-
      out population. The assumptions made regarding the future number of
      employees who are contracted-out were described in paragraphs 14.22 to
      14.26 above. The mortality rates used in projecting the CODs, both before
      and after reaching pension age, were lower than the rates used for all
      additional pension because experience shows that occupational scheme
      pensioners generally experience lighter mortality by age than the population
      as a whole.

14.53 Where an employee leaves a contracted-out pension scheme with less than
      two years’ service, the scheme may pay a state scheme premium which in
      effect cancels the COD. Allowance was made for this, so that, in the long-
      term, the amounts of COD at award with respect to occupational pension
      schemes were approximately 1% less than they would have been if no state
      scheme premiums were payable.

      factor.

       Guardian’s Allowance

       Christmas bonus for pensioners

      give entitlement to the lump sum. The benefit rate was assumed to remain
      fixed at £10 since there is no requirement in legislation for this to be raised.

       Statutory Sick Pay

14.74 The finances of the National Insurance Fund are not affected by Statutory
      Sick Pay (SSP), as the Fund recovers from the consolidated fund the amount
      by which small employers reduce their contributions to offset any SSP they
      must pay.      The projections for SSP are therefore included only for
      completeness. These deductions amounted to just over £30 million in
      2001/02, as shown in the accounts for that year. In view of the small amount,
      the future estimates were derived by simply adjusting this figure in line with
      the estimates of the numbers of future contributors and the increase in the
      benefit rate.

       Statutory Maternity Pay

14.75 As with Statutory Sick Pay, the finances of the National Insurance Fund are
      not affected by Statutory Maternity Pay (SMP), as the Fund recovers from the
      consolidated fund the amount by which employers reduce their contributions
      to offset the SMP paid. The estimates for SMP are thus included only for
      completeness. Recoveries by employers in respect of expenditure on SMP
      for 2001/02 are estimated in the accounts to be about £635 million. For later
      years it has been assumed that expenditure would vary with the number of
      births, the estimated proportion of women at the child-bearing ages who are
      contributing employees, the benefit rate and, in the case of the earnings-
      related part of the benefit, earnings. Allowance was also made for the
      changes to SMP which have applied since April 2003, described in
      paragraphs 10.75 and 10.76 of Appendix A.

      Earnings Limit. The projections of Maternity Allowance expenditure were

       Adult dependency increases

14.77 Some benefits are increased if the beneficiary has an adult dependant
      defined as a wife or other dependant, with earnings, if any, below a specified
      limit. The amount of the increase is generally about 60% of the insured
      person's benefit.

14.78 In the case of male retirement pensioners, a dependent wife is in practice
      always under pension age as, if she is older, the wife of a retirement
      pensioner is entitled in her own right to a pension on her husband's insurance
      record (if not on her own record). It has been assumed that the proportions
      (by age) with an adult dependant will remain broadly stable up to 2010,
      although with some allowance for a continuation of short-term trends. From
      the year 2010 onwards, allowance has been made for an increase in the
      numbers of men with adult dependants as a result of the increase in female
      pension age: it has been assumed that all married women over age 60 with
      husbands over 65 who qualify for RP, would become adult dependants.

14.79 A similar approach has been adopted for male Incapacity Benefit
      beneficiaries, allowing for the changes in the rules for adult dependency
      additions with Incapacity Benefit which will lead to significantly lower
      proportions entitled in respect of wives under pension age.

14.80 Adult dependency increases are available to women on the same basis as
      men. However the numbers of women receiving such increases are very
      small in comparison with additions for men, mainly because of the greater
      effect of the potential dependant's earnings. The costs of dependency
      additions to female beneficiaries are insignificant and so have not been
      analysed in detail. The projected expenditure is therefore based on current
      overall proportions entitled to this increase allowing for future changes in
      benefit rates.

       Child dependency increases

14.81 Child dependency increases are payable with retirement pension, Incapacity
      Benefit and Widowed Parent’s/Mother’s Allowance awarded before April
      2003. No child dependency increases are given for benefits awarded since
      April 2003. Expenditure on these increases has therefore been modelled by
      taking the latest data on the number of increases in payment, allowing for
      new awards up to April 2003 and then running off the resulting amounts over
      the period until all the children have ceased to be eligible.

       Redundancy payments

14.82 The annual amount of redundancy payments net of recoveries was about
      £230 million in 2001/02, some 0.4% of fund outgo. It was considered
      adequate to base the future estimates on that amount increased in line with
      earnings.

       Administration costs

14.83 The cost of administration in 2001/02 was about £840 million, about 1.5% of
      total expenditure. Of this amount about 60% related to the role of the
      Department for Work and Pensions in administering the payment of benefits
      and the majority of the balance related to the Inland Revenue’s collection of
      National Insurance contributions.

14.84 In future, whilst the numbers of contributors and those in receipt of benefits
      other than retirement pensions are relatively stable, the increase in the
      number of pensioners will tend to increase costs. On the other hand, this is
      likely to be offset by improvements in the efficiency of administration. As
      administration costs are a relatively small proportion of total outgo, it has
      been assumed as a practical expedient that they will increase in line with
      earnings.

       Other income and expenditure

14.85 Other income mainly comprises state scheme premiums which are generally
      payable when an employee leaves a contracted-out occupational pension
      scheme having completed less than two years in the scheme. This payment
      reinstates the individual in SERPS/State Second Pension in respect of his or
      her period of scheme membership. Other income relates to recoveries from
      insurance companies in respect of claims for damages. In total in 2001/02
      other income amounted to about £182 million and in view of the small amount
      (relative to other income items) this has been projected forward in line with
      earnings.

14.86 Other expenditure amounted to £139 million in 2001/02 and largely related to
      a transfer to the Northern Ireland National Insurance Fund, with a much
      smaller payment to the Manx Insurance Fund (which operates in the Isle of
      Man). For simplicity, the other expenditure has been projected forward in line
      with earnings.
