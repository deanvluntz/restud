SUMMARY OF CONCLUSIONS

1.1 Since April 1978 the National Insurance Fund has collected earnings-
related contributions from employed contributors and in return has promised
them earnings-related pensions in due course on top of the flat-rate pensions and
other benefits traditionally provided. It will be about 60 years before expenditure
on the new earnings-related pensions reaches its full level and meantime the
contribution rates required to balance outgo are at a significantly lower level
than will later prove to be necessary. The amount in the National Insurance
Fund has become, and is likely to remain, no more than a modest working
balance and will contribute little real income to the Fund.

1.2 On the basis of population projections in which the total population
increases gradually from 544 million in 1979 to 59 million in 2029, and ultimately
becomes stationary at about 60 million, the Class 1 standard contribution rate
for National Insurance Fund benefits is estimated to increase from 15-4 per cent
in 1985-86 to 16-7 per cent in 2005-6 and to 21-9 per cent in 2025-26. However the
new earnings-related pension scheme is then still some way from full maturity in
regard to benefit expenditure and further increases of up to 2 per cent may be
expected in the standard contribution rate after 2025-26. This assumes that
earnings and prices increase at the same rate in future and that unemployment
is at the level of 6 per cent throughout. (With unemployment at 12 per cent the
contribution rate is currently about 17 per cent of earnings.)

1.3 If, as seems more likely, earnings increase at a higher rate than prices, the
standard contribution rate in the long term would be | or 2 per cent lower if post-
award increases in earnings-related pensions continued to be in line with prices
but other benefits were increased in line with earnings.

1.4 If flat-rate benefits too were uprated over a long period at a lower rate than
earnings, for example in line with prices or at some intermediate rate between
earnings and prices, the increase in the standard contribution rate would be less
steep and it might even not increase at all depending upon how big was the
difference between earnings and benefit increases. In that event, the basic benefit
rates would in due course become very much lower than at present compared with
the level of earnings, even if the purchasing power of those benefits had been
maintained or improved. (For particulars of the estimated standard contri-
bution rates in future years see Table 14 in section 9 of the report.)

1.5 If the proportion of employed persons who are contracted out remains
constant, then in the long term the effect of contracting out on the Fund is not
likely to be great, contribution reductions and guaranteed minimum pensions
each year being almost equal in amount and relatively small in relation to total
income and total expenditure. In the short term, because the total of guaranteed
minimum pensions is very small, the greater the number contracted out and
paying reduced contributions the greater the standard contribution rate required
fromall contributors. (See section 10 of the report.)

1.6 If fertility, which is currently below replacement level, remains below
replacement level in future, then the relative numbers of pensioners and con-

INTRODUCTION

The statutory requirement

2.1 The object of the review, as stated in section 137 subsection (3) of the
Social Security Act 1975, is to determine the extent to which the level at which
the National Insurance Fund stands from year to year may be expected in the
longer term to bear a proper relation to demands in respect of payments of
benefit and, for this purpose, I have taken into account changes made since
5 April 1980.
Sccial Security Acts 1975 to 1982

2.2 The Social Security Act 1975 consolidated the provisions in earlier
legislation establishing a basic scheme of contributions and benefits for national
insurance and industrial injuries. This was modified by the Social Security
Pensions Act 1975 which provided for the introduction in April 1978 of a new
scheme which for employed persons replaced the existing flat-rate pensions for
retirement, widowhood and invalidity by a two-tier system made up of two
components—a basic flat-rate component and an additional component
calculated as a percentage of a band of earnings between a level approximately
equal to the standard flat-rate pension and an upper limit about seven times the
pension. At the same time it was provided that where adequate alternative
provision with a guaranteed minimum pension was made under an occupational
pension scheme an employee could be contracted out from part of the additional
comp ynent of retirement pension and widow’s pension. In that case the em-
ployee and employer are allowed a reduction in the contribution rate payable
on the band of earnings between the lower and upper limits, the same earnings
by reference to which the additional component and the guaranteed minimum
pension are calculated.

2.3 Further changes were introduced by the Social Security (Miscellaneous
Provisions) Act 1977, the Social Security Act 1979, the Pensioners’ Payments
and Social Security Act 1979 and, after the end of the period to which this
review relates, by the Social Security Act 1980, the Social Security (No. 2)
Act 1980, the Social Security (Contributions) Act 1981, the Social Security
Act 1981 and the Social Security (Contributions) Act 1982. These Acts together
with those referred to in paragraph 2.2 constitute the Social Security Acts 1975
to 1982 at the time of this report.

Industrial Injuries and Diseases (Old Cases) Act 1975

2.4 This Act consolidated the provisions in the Industrial Injuries and
Diseases (Old Cases) Acts 1967 to 1974 and related enactments which secured
the payment from the National Insurance Fund of disability and death benefits
where the industrial accident or disease was not covered by either the Workmen’s
Compensation Acts (from 1924) or the National Insurance (Industrial Injuries)
Acts (from 1948). The number of beneficiaries under the Old Cases Act is
dwindling rapidly and expenditure on their benefits each year now represents
considerably less than one-thousandth of the total expenditure of the National
Insurance Fund.

SUMMARY OF CONTRIBUTIONS AND BENEFITS
Classes of contributor

3.1 The population of Great Britain over school-leaving age and under
pensionable age is divided, for the purposes of the Social Security Acts, into
three categories :—

(i) employed persons, i.e. all persons gainfully occupied under a contract
of service or as an office-holder, and certain other persons;
(ii) self-employed persons, i.e. others who are gainfully occupied;
(iii) other persons, non-employed or with low earnings.

3.2 Employed and self-employed persons whose earnings are above specified
minima are required to contribute to the National Insurance Fund and those
with lower earnings and non-employed persons may do so if they wish. Employed
persons who have earnings above the lower limit pay earnings-related Class 1
primary contributions and their employers pay earnings-related Class 1 second-
ary contributions. Since April 1978 no Class 1 primary contributions have been
payable by those at work after pensionable age (65 for men and 60 for women)
but the employer is required to pay Class 1 secondary contributions. Reduced
rates of contribution corresponding to the benefits which may be received by
them from the Fund are payable for members of the armed forces and certain
seamen. Certain employed married women and widows who opted to do so by
11 May 1977 have a reserved right to pay a reduced rate of contribution and
qualify only for industrial injury benefits but their employers pay full Class 1
secondary contributions. Self-employed persons not excepted on account of low
earnings pay flat-rate Class 2 contributions and, on that part of their earnings
above a specified level, they pay earnings-related Class 4 contributions as well.
For both the employed and the self-employed, earnings above a specified upper
limit are ignored for contribution purposes. Non-employed persons and others
not required to contribute may volunteer to pay flat-rate Class 3 contributions.
Principal categories of benefit

3.3 The principal categories of benefit payable from the National Insurance
Fund are the following :—

(i) Unemployment benefit (for employed persons only);
(ii) Industrial injury benefits (for employed persons only);
(iii) Sickness and invalidity benefits (for employed and self-employed
persons only);
(iv) Maternity allowance (for employed and self-employed women only)
and maternity grant;
(v} Retirement pension;
(vi) Widow’s benefit;
(vii) Death grant;
(viii) Lump sum payment to pensioners for Christmas.
Details of the rates of benefit and eligibility are given in Appendix A.

3.6 It will be seen that, mainly in order to make good the loss of contributions
for persons contracted out, the joint rate payable to the National Insurance
Fund had to be increased from 13.25 to 15-25 per cent in April 1978. Of the
further increases amounting to 1:80 per cent by April 1982 about 0-75 was
required to offset a reduction in the Treasury supplement from 18 per cent to
13 per cent (provided in the Social Security (Contributions) Acts 1981 and 1982)
and the remainder to keep the Fund in balance in conditions of increasing
unemployment. The table shows the full contribution rates applicable to the
great majority of employed persons; as mentioned in paragraph 3.2 there are
reduced rates for members of the armed forces and some others. For certain
married women and widows the primary contribution was 2 per cent of earnings,
including 0-4 per cent for the National Health Service. This was increased to
2:75 per cent in April 1981, and to 3-20 per cent from April 1982, including
1-10 per cent for the National Health Service and the Redundancy Fund.

3.7 Putting these figures into perspective, in April 1976 and in April in
subsequent years the upper earnings limit for Class 1 contributions (about seven
times the basic pension rate) has been approximately 14 times the average weekly
earnings of male manual workers, so that the basic pension rate has been about
21 per cent of those average earnings, or 34 per cent including the addition for a
dependent wife. Under the new arrangements introduced in 1978 an additional
component of pension will be payable amounting ultimately to a further 25 per
cent of the band of earnings between the lower and upper limits, i.e. about 20 per
cent of all earnings for a male manual worker on average earnings, giving a
total pension of about 41 per cent, or 54 per cent for a married couple. The
average weekly earnings for all full-time adult workers, combining males and
females, manuals and non-manuals, is much the same as for male manual
workers. For those whose earnings have been below average (for example,
many women) the total State pension will be a higher percentage of earnings and
for those with above-average earnings the percentage will be lower.

3.8 In the case of the self-employed, the upper earnings limit for Class 4
earnings-related contributions has been broadly the annual equivalent of the
upper limit applicable to the weekly earnings of the employed but the lower
earnings limit for Class 4 contributions has been more than double the lower
limit for Class 1 contributions. The flat-rate Class 2 contribution has represented
approximately the result of applying the Class 4 percentage to the lower limit
for Class 4 contributions. Effectively, therefore, the total contributions under
Classes 2 and 4 for self-employed persons who have earnings above the Class 4
lower limit amount to about the same as if the Class 4 percentage had applied to
all earnings up to the upper limit, in similar fashion to the earnings-related
Class 1 contributions for employed persons. For those with earnings below the
Class 4 lower limit (but above the threshold for those with small earnings) only
the Class 2 contribution is payable. Particulars of the threshold, the earnings
limits and the contribution rates for the self-employed during the period covered
by the review and for the years 1980-81, 1981-82 and 1982-83 are shown in
Table 3. (The Class 2 and 4 rates for 1982-83 represent a departure intended to
hold down the Class 2 rate for that year.)

Contracting out

3.13 For those in Class 1 who are contracted out of the new earnings-related
pension arrangements the contribution reductions on earnings between the
lower and upper limits in the first five years are 2-5 per cent (primary) and 4-5
per cent (secondary), a total of 7-0 per cent. Where liability for the contracted-
out pension is transferred back to the National Insurance Fund because a
contracted-out employee changes his job or the pension scheme of which he is
a member ceases to be contracted out, a State scheme premium is payable
comprising either a premium of the amount specified in The State Scheme
Premiums (Actuarial Tables) Regulations 1978 (S.J. 1978 No. 134) or alter-
natively (where the period contracted out was less than five years) repayment
of the contribution reductions actually received. The premium amounts in the
1978 Regulations are based on assumptions consistent with the 7 per cent
contribution reduction.

4,2 It will be seen that income and expenditure have increased considerably,
reflecting the earnings and prices movements which determine contributions and
benefit rates, but the balance in the Fund and the related investment income
have increased even more rapidly. Because section 137(3) of the Social Security
Act 1975 requires me to consider whether in the longer term the balance in the
Fund may be expected to bear a proper relation to expenditure on benefits,
Table 4 indicates also what that relationship has been during each of the five
years under review. The penultimate line of figures expresses the balance in the
Fund at the end of each year as a percentage of the expenditure on benefits
during the previous 12 months. Alternatively, the bottom line shows the mean
value of the balance at the beginning and end of each year as a percentage of
the expenditure on benefits during the year. The latter probably gives the better
comparison and shows that the balance increased relative to the level of expendi-
ture in the first three years of the period and then fell back a little notwithstand-
ing that there was again a substantial excess of receipts over payments during
the fourth and fifth years. This demonstrates the fact that, for the balance to
maintain its value relative to the level of expenditure on benefits, there must be
an increase each year which matches the proportionate increase in benefit
expenditure.

THE OBJECT OF THE QUINQUENNIAL REVIEW

5.1 The object of this review is stated in section 137(3) of the Social Security
Act 1975 to be to determine the extent to which the level at which the National
Insurance Fund stands from year to year may be expected in the longer term to
bear a proper relation to demands in respect of payments of benefit. For this

purpose I am required to take into account current rates of contributions, the
yield to be expected from contributions in the longer term and such other
matters as I consider to be relevant as affecting the present and future level of
the Fund.

5.2 For many years the rates of contribution payable to the National Insur-
ance Fund have been set at a level intended to produce a flow of income which,
together with the corresponding Treasury supplements and income from
investments, would broadly match current expenditure on benefits and costs of
administration, a system which is sometimes referred to as pay-as-you-go.
However, a positive working balance has to be maintained principally in order
to be able to advance money to the Post Office for the purpose of paying
pensions and other benefits. It must also cover the possibility of an unexpected
excess of expenditure over income because it is administratively necessary for
the contribution rates to be determined some four or five months before the
beginning of the tax year in which they will apply and it is not feasible to change
them later even if it becomes clear that they are going to be seriously inadequate.
Thus, once the rates have been determined, it is some 16 or 17 months before
different rates can be applied.

5.3 From discussions with officials of the social security department and
from my own consideration of the possible extent of errors in the estimates of
income and expenditure on the basis of which contribution rates are decided, I
am satisfied that the present working balance (towards the end of the year
1981-82) of about one-fifth of a year’s benefit expenditure, enough for about
10 weeks’ benefits, is adequate in present conditions. However, if the percentage
were to fall much lower it would be necessary to review the position.

5.4 If the present system of aiming at a balance of income and expenditure
each year were to continue, and if there were no large surpluses or deficiencies,
then as the level of expenditure increased in future—as seems inevitable given
expectations on future earnings and prices—the balance in the Fund would
gradually lose ground in relation to the level of expenditure. It would soon
become necessary to budget for a surplus sufficient to increase the Fund in
proportion to the estimated increase in expenditure anticipated. As, generally
speaking, high interest rates accompany high rates of inflation, and interest
rates fall when inflation falls, a first approximation to the surplus required
could be obtained by ignoring all investment income on the ground that it
would be close to the amount required each year to maintain the value of the
Fund in relation to the level of expenditure. In fulfilment of the requirements
placed upon me by section 137(3) of the 1975 Act I have therefore made
estimates ignoring income from investments and have considered. separately the
effect on the results of taking investment income into account and of making
provision for continuing payments to the Northern Ireland National Insurance

THE EFFECT ON THE SCHEME OF FUTURE INCREASES
IN EARNINGS AND PRICES

6.1 Under section 125 of the Social Security Act 1975 as modified by the
1980 and 1981 Acts the Secretary of State is required to carry out an annual
review in order to determine whether the flat-rates of weekly benefit and disable-
ment gratuity have retained their value in relation to the general level of prices
and, if he concludes that they have not retained their value, then he is charged
with laying a draft order for the consideration of Parliament increasing those
benefit rates from a specified future date by amounts estimated to be at least
sufficient to restore their value at that date. Under section 125 as modified by
the 1975 Pensions Act he is required to do the same for earnings-related pensions
so that the amount of an earnings-related pension, which is increased in line
with the general level of earnings up to the time of award, will subsequently be
maintained in relation at least to the movement of the general level of prices.
Under section 13 of the 1979 Act the Secretary of State has since 1978-79 been
required to make a similar annual review of the amounts of maternity grant and
death grant payable but is not charged with laying a draft order to increase those
amounts if he finds that they have not retained their value in relation to the
general level of prices.

6.2 For purposes of the long-term estimates in this report it is necessary to
make assumptions concerning the future benefit increases which might result
from the annual reviews to be made under section 125 of the 1975 Act and
section 13 of the 1979 Act. They can be no more than assumptions because the
law prescribes only that the increase shall be at /east in line with prices und
section 13 of the 1979 Act makes no requirement that maternity and death
grants should be increased at all. Section 125 of the 1975 Act originally provided
for the basic pension rate (but not the unemployment or sickness rate) to
maintain its value each year at least in relation to the movement of earnings or
prices, whichever was the greater. Although the reference to earnings was
deleted by the 1980 Act, Ministers have nevertheless expressed the intention of
increasing benefit rates by more than the minimum provision when economic
growth is restored.

6.3 There is considerable uncertainty concerning the likely movements of
earnings and prices in future, not only during the next few years but also in the
longer term for which it would not now seem reasonable to assume as high a
rate as 3 per cent for the annual increase in real earnings compared with prices
which, on Government instructions, was used for the financial estimates in my
report on the financial provisions of the Social Security Pensions Bill 1975
(Cmnd. 5928). The following considerations are relevant to what it might be
appropriate to assume for purposes of the estimates in this report.

weekly earnings of male manual workers (34 per cent for a married couple). For
those receiving sickness or unemployment benefit on a temporary interruption of
earnings, the benefit rate was generally 17 per cent of average earnings in April

Lower and upper earnings limits for contributions

6.6 Section 1 of the Social Security Pensions Act 1975 provides that the
lower limit should be about equal to the basic pension rate as at 6 May in the
same tax year and the upper limit between 6} and 7} times that rate. Thus, if the
pension rate fell in relative value over 40 years to about 10 per cent of average
earnings, the lower and upper earnings limits would correspondingly fall to 10
per cent and about 70 per cent respectively of average earnings. The reduction
from 21 per cent to 10 per cent in the lower earnings limit would bring into
compulsory contributions a growing number of persons with relatively small
earnings. The reduction in the upper earnings limit to a position lower than
average earnings would seriously diminish the range of earnings on which social

security contributions were levied.
Earnings-related pensions
6.7 Corresponding to such reductions in the earnings limits, there would be a
significant reduction in the band of earnings relevant to the calculation of the
additional component of pension in future years. For example, for an individual
with earnings consistently 14 times the average, relevant earnings at retirement,
obtained in accordance with section 6 of the Social Security Pensions Act 1975

year and to (0:70—0:10) = 0-60 for the 40th year and the pension would
clearly be based on the earliest 20 years. On the other hand, for those consis-
tently on low earnings, for example a woman whose earnings remained at 60 per
cent of the average throughout, pension would be affected only by the lower
limit and at (0-60—0-10) = 0-50 times average earnings would be higher than
the figure of (0-60—0-21) = 0-39 expected at present. The provision that the

Post-award increases in earnings-related pensions

sixth ten years after the time of award and one-third twenty years after award.
For pensioners who survived for more than twenty years the fall would be
greater, although their purchasing power would have been maintained from the
time of retirement by upratings in line with prices.
Maternity grant and death grant

6.9 The amount of maternity grant payable has remained at £25 since
November 1969 and the death grant (maximum) at £30 since October 1967.
These amounts are only a fraction of the amounts which would have been pay-
able currently if they had been uprated annually in line with either prices or
earnings. There are a number of possibilities for the future adjustment of those
benefits following the provisions for annual review in section 13 of the 1979 Act.
However, the Social Security (Maternity Grant) Regulations 1981 (SI No. 1157)

entitlement to maternity grant and the cost of the benefit will correspondingly be
met from general revenue and not from the National Insurance Fund. Also the
government issued a Consultative Document in March 1982 which contains a
proposal, still under consideration, for a new non-contributory funeral grant to
replace the present death grant. The long-term estimates in this report do not
therefore make provision for expenditure on either maternity grant or death
grant.
Short-term benefit rates

6.10 In November 1980 the basic rate of retirement pension was increased by

when that happens, the level of those benefit rates will be reviewed in the light of
circumstances at the time, though in the case of invalidity pension an explicit
undertaking has been given that the previous level will be restored. For purposes
of the estimates of future expenditure in this report I have assumed that the
pre-November 1980 relativities will be restored by 1985 for all those benefits.

Earnings-related pensions

8.12 When a person reaching pension age has paid Class 1 contributions in
any tax year since April 1978 an additional earnings-related component of
pension is payable. The procedure is for the earnings in each completed tax year
(excluding any earnings above the upper limit) to be revalued in line with the
general level of earnings up to the tax year before that in which pension age is
reached; for the amount of the lower earnings limit for the latter tax year (i.e. the
amount not relevant to the calculation of the earnings-related pension) to be
subtracted in each case; and for a pension to be awarded amounting to 1-25 per
cent of the total of the highest 20 figures for revalued relevant earnings.

8.13 So far as persons reaching pension age in the first 21 years of the new
scheme are concerned, therefore, the pension will be 1-25 per cent of the total
revalued earnings for completed years since April 1978 and it will be unaffected
by the provision for selecting the best 20 years. After 31 March 1999, persons
retiring will progressively have more years of relevant earnings from which to
select the best 20 years and pensions in payment will include a gradually increas-
ing proportion of pensioners who will have gained from the selection of the best
20 years, and the average such gain will gradually increase as the period in
employment since 1978 increases.

(i) For each age, the product of the proportion of the population paying

proportion of the unemployed will be receiving benefit from the National

been increasing or decreasing, whether the newly unemployed include large
numbers of unqualified young persons or adults who have been in regular
employment, whether the same individuals remain unemployed for long periods
and exhaust their entitlement or are replaced by others with entitlement to 52
weeks’ benefit. Experience over the years, although perhaps not typical of a
period when it is assumed that the level of unemployment will remain at 6 per
cent, indicates some degree of stability in the proportion entitled to receive
benefit from the National Insurance Fund and the estimates in this report
assume that 50 per cent will be so entitled.

Sickness, invalidity and industrial injury benefits

8.29 The methods of estimating the future costs of those benefits are described
in Appendices D and E so far as flat-rate benefits are concerned. However,
additional earnings-related invalidity pensions are paid to former employees
under the new scheme introduced in 1978. The amounts of earnings-related
pensions were calculated by subdividing the beneficiaries according to when they
become permanently sick and obtaining the amount of earnings-related benefit
accrued at that time by reference to the estimation model used for retirement
pensions purposes.

Costs of administration

8.36 Jt is very difficult to forecast the costs of administration in future years.
Recent analyses have indicated that about three-fifths of the costs reflect the
level of earnings and the remaining two-fifths the level of prices so that, in those
of the six bases in which it is assumed that earnings increase faster than prices in
future, administration costs should fall a little in relation to the level of earnings.
Other analyses have shown that about one-third of the costs are incurred in
paying pension benefits and the remaining two-thirds in paying short-term
benefits to and in collecting contributions from persons of working age, so that
the trend over the years is likely to be more closely related to the relatively
stable numbers at the working ages than to the increasing numbers over pension
age. In addition, part of the costs will remain relatively constant over the years
whatever changes take place in the numbers of contributors, beneficiaries and
pensioners.

THE EFFECT OF CONTRACTING OUT

10.1 The effect of contracting out on the finances of the National Insurance
Fund may be seen by comparing the contribution reductions allowed to con-
tracted-out pension schemes, net of State scheme premiums paid back to the
Fund, with the relief to the National Insurance Fund from guaranteed minimum
pensions (GMPs) paid by contracted-out pension schemes. The figures shown in
Table 16 for basis (a)(iii) are obtained from Tables 12 and 13 and from the latest
estimates for the year 1981-82.

10.2 The estimated net cost of contracting out in the year 1981-82 is £3-14 bn.
Without contracting out, the pay-as-you-go contribution rates (with the excep-
tion of the Class 1 primary rate for married women and widow optants) would
have been about 13-90 per cent instead of the actual figure of 16-50 per cent,
a reduction of 2:60 per cent. In subsequent years, as GMPs paid by pension
schemes increase in amount and contribution reductions fall, the net current
cost to the Fund diminishes and in the long term the effect of contracting out Is
seen to be fairly small, so that the standard contribution rate required would be
much the same with or without contracting out.

10.3 A similar result is obtained by considering the effect of contracting out in
an “ultimate” situation, when the scheme is mature and the population stationary.
At present, the contribution reduction is calculated by discounting the expected
payments of GMPs at 9 per cent, allowing for 8 per cent annual increase in
earnings. Additions are made for expenses and contingencies which partly offset
the effect of discounting at the relatively small rate (in excess of earnings
increases) used prior to retirement. Thus in the ultimate position, with earnings
increasing at 8 per cent per annum, the total amount of contribution reductions
in any year would be only a little less than the total amount of GMPs deducted
from state pensions in that year.

Contribution conditions and eligibility

4, Full-rate contributors in Class 1, i.e. employees, are eligible for all the

eligible for all benefits with the exception of unemployment benefit, industrial
injury benefits and earnings-related pensions. Class 4 contributions by self-
employed persons do not give entitlement to benefit (their entitlement is deter-
mined by Class 2 contributions). Class 3 voluntary contributors are entitled only
to flat-rate retirement pensions and widows’ benefits, the pensioners’ Christmas
bonus and death grant.

7. The standard rate of Category A flat-rate pension is £29-60 a week and it

APPENDIX C
THE ESTIMATED FUTURE POPULATION OF GREAT BRITAIN

1. Asa basis for the estimates of future income and outgo in this report I have
adopted the most recent in the annual series of official population projections
prepared by the Government Actuary in consultation with the Registrars
General in London and Edinburgh. Particulars of that projection, starting from
the estimated total population at mid-1979, have been published on microfiche
by the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys, Series PP2 No. 11 (Fiche No.
7904). As the method of projection used was unchanged from previous years and
as the bases, assumptions and the results of the projection were so close to the
mid-1978 based projections published in Series PP2 No. 10, no projections by
marital condition or on alternative fertility assumptions were made at that time
based on mid-1979 and the description given of the method used and the
demographic factors was much abbreviated. For a detailed study of those
matters the interested reader is referred to earlier booklets in the PP2 Series but
a summary of the assumptions underlying the mid-1979 projections for Great
Britain is given in the paragraphs which follow.

Base population assumptions .

2. The projections are based on estimates by the Registrars General of the
total population at mid-1979 of some 54-4 million, which is derived from the
1971 census-of population, updated to allow for subsequent births, deaths and
migration. The total population is the home population with the addition of
members of HM Forces belonging to Great Britain serving outside Great
Britain, and excluding the forces of other countries temporarily in Great
Britain. The projections are made by calculating the numbers of births and the
numbers of deaths and migrants at each age in forty successive periods running
from one mid-year to the next.

Mortality assumptions moot mo

3. Mortality rates at the beginning of the projection period are based on
recent experience, particularly that of the period 1973-75, but allowing for
some improvement since then, particularly in the case of infant deaths under the
age of 6 months. It has been assumed that mortality rates at some ages will
remain constant, but that at other ages the rates will decrease in geometrical
progression, i.e. by constant proportions each year for the forty years of the
projection period. The mortality rates for selected ages are shown in Table Cl
for the first and last years of the projection period, i.e. 1979-80 and 2018-19,
together with the improvement factors showing the proportionate reduction in
mortality assumed over 40 years.

Migration assumptions

4, The projections assume that there will be a net outward migration from
Great Britain throughout the period of the projection, starting with a net loss of
8 thousand persons in the first year and increasing to 23 thousand a year from
1983 onwards. This includes migration to and from Northern Ireland. For

5. The numbers of births in future years are obtained by applying fertility

reproduce itself with an average family size per woman of a little more than 2

9, Patterns of marriage, divorce and remarriage have been changing in recent

marriage rates and in the average age at first marriage have been relatively
moderate but divorce rates and the numbers of divorcees available to remarry
have increased considerably since the legislative changes of 1969-70 and
remarriage rates have falien. In determining the rates of marriage, divorce and
remarriage to be assumed for the future, it has been assumed that recent trends

social and economic environment could affect marriage and divorce in a variety

APPENDIX D
SICKNESS AND INVALIDITY EXPERIENCE

1. Sickness benefit is payable for the first 168 days of incapacity and invalidity
benefit, which is at a higher weekly rate of benefit, is payable for the remainder
of incapacity up to the time when retirement pension is awarded. Statistics are
prepared each year of all benefits payable in a year commencing on the first
Monday in June and in this appendix this is described as the statistical year
June to May.

2. Entitlement to benefit for sickness beginning in any calendar year is
determined by reference to an individual’s contribution record in the tax year
which ended in the previous calendar year, i.e. one or two years earlier than
payment of sickness benefit and an even longer time before the payment of
invalidity benefit. In calculating sickness and invalidity rates, therefore, a
choice must be made between using as the denominator for those rates the
numbers currently alive and insured for those benefits, or the numbers who had
had qualifying contributions in an earlier period. It is more convenient in
practice for estimation purposes to adopt the numbers currently insured as a
proxy for the true numbers exposed to risk and this is the basis of the rates
shown in this report. The numbers exposed to risk are derived from the numbers
economically active (see paragraph 7.7 of the report) with the exclusion of
members of H.M. Forces and reduced-rate contributors who are not eligible for
sickness benefit, and the inclusion of an estimated number of persons long-term
sick who are not included in the census of employment statistics. The benefit
statistics which provide the numerators in calculating the rates are increased to
allow for sickness benefits payable to civil servants and others not included in the
statistics (see paragraph 8.34).

3. In recent years there has been a significant increase in invalidity rates.
Some information was given on this in my report on the draft of the Social
Security (Contributions, Re-rating) Order 1978 (Cmnd. 7403). Subsequent
studies carried out in the Department of Health and Social Security associate
the increase with the increase in unemployment which increases the pressures on
people with disabilities or in poor health to leave their present jobs and makes it
more difficult for them to find alternative work. Some particulars of the sickness
and invalidity experience in the statistical years 1973-75 and 1978-80 are shown
in Tables D1 and D2. (In the intervening years there was a change in the
procedure for producing detailed statistics, which interrupted the regular flow.)

4. A detailed analysis of the experience in those statistical years showed that
up to May 1979 the sickness rates for men were fairly steady whereas the rates
for women gradually increased, more particularly in the case of married women.
However in the statistical year ending May 1980 sickness rates for all categories
of insured persons showed a significant decrease. In contrast to this the invalidity
rates for all categories of insured persons continued to increase slightly in that
year.

5. In some previous investigations separate rates were calculated for employed
and self-employed persons. In view of the relatively large movement between

APPENDIX F
CONTRIBUTION RATES FOR THE SELF-EMPLOYED
1. The position of self-employed persons in the National Insurance Scheme
is currently under review following replies received in response to the Discussion
Document issued by the Secretary of State for Social Services in October 1980.
Until that review is completed it is appropriate for me to assume that the Class 2
and Class 4 contribution rates for the self-employed will continue to be derived
by the method decided upon by the previous Government in November 1977.
A description of the method was given in my report on the draft of the Social
Security (Contributions, Re-rating) (No. 2), Order 1977 (Cmnd. 7036).
2. The sequence of steps in this method of calculating the self-employed
contribution rates is the following:—

(i) The starting point is the full Class 1 standard contribution rate,
including contributions to the National Health Service and to the
Redundancy and Maternity Pay Funds.

(ii) The proportion of current expenditure which is in respect of industrial
injuries benefits, unemployment benefit, earnings-related supplements
to sickness benefit, widow’s allowance, maternity allowance, graduated
pensions earned under the pre-1975 scheme and contributions to the
Redundancy and Maternity Pay Funds is calculated and subtracted
from (i), in order to exclude that part of the full contribution which
relates to benefits for which the self-employed are not eligible.

(iii) This gives a net contribution rate payable on all earnings up to the
upper earnings limit but takes no account of the additional pensions
being earned by employed persons related to their earnings between
the lower and upper earnings limits. A further deduction is therefore
made from the contribution rate applicable to that band of earnings of
an amount intended to enable the average self-employed person to
provide benefits comparable to the additional pensions being earned
by employed persons.

(iv) Calculation of this further deduction is on similar lines and on similar
bascs to the calculation of the contribution reduction from Class 1
primary and secondary contribution rates for contracted-out employees.
But it is larger than the contracted-out reduction because it must
allow for the cost of post-award pension increases in line with prices
and also for earnings-related invalidity pensions and the other half of
the widow’s and the whole of the widower’s Category B earnings-
related pension which, for contracted-out employees, are paid by the
National Insurance Fund and not by the contracted-out scheme. On
the 1977 basis the contracted-out contribution reduction for 1978-83
was 7 per cent of the relevant earnings, falling gradually in future to
about 3-75 per cent. On the same basis the deduction for self-employed
persons would be 12:75 per cent initially, falling ultimately to about
7 per cent of the relevant band of earnings. The reason for this fall is
that the average accrual rate for the additional pension falls from about

APPENDIX G
THE ULTIMATE COST OF THE SCHEME

1. It is sometimes asked what the cost of the scheme will be in the long term
when the new earnings-related pension system is mature and a steady state is
reached. The danger of answering such a question in equally simple terms is that
it can be too easily assumed that attainment of a steady state is likely, or even
inevitable, whereas in practice it is rather unlikely given the many conditions
which would have to be fulfilled for that position to be reached and the uncer-
tainties concerning their fulfilment. Nevertheless, the advantage of such a
presentation is that it can disclose the effect of a number of the factors involved,
and their inter-relationship with each other, more clearly than in estimates in
which other factors are producing changes.

2. The first condition to be satisfied is that the population should be stable;
that is that the numbers of men and women at each age and the proportions
married, relative ages of husbands and wives etc. should remain the same or
alternatively that the numbers should be either increasing or decreasing at a
constant rate. The estimates in this appendix shown the position with a station-
ary population, assuming no further mortality improvement beyond the rates
shown for 2018 in Table Cl and assumirg no migration so that the population
is maintained by births alone. It will be appreciated that with an expanding
population the required contribution rate would be lower than with a stationary
population, and with a declining population it would be higher, and even with a
stationary population the contribution rate would be higher if a greater degree
of mortality improvement were to be assumed.

3. The estimates in this appendix are intended to demonstrate the effect of
some of the non-demographic factors involved. The reader is warned against
assuming that changes in the demographic factors can be ignored and that a
trend towards a stationary population is inevitable.

4. It must also be assumed that there is no change in the numbers who are
employed, self-employed, or voluntary contributors, in the numbers sick,
unemployed, etc. or in the numbers contracted-out, and as contributions are
directly related to earnings it must be assumed that the distribution of earnings
about the average and the relationship to that average of the lower and upper
earnings limits which apply for contribution purposes do not change. Moreover,
if a steady state (in terms of prevailing earnings levels) is to be attained in a
scheme in which contributions are earnings-related, then benefit rates too must
be assumed to be earnings-related. Thé exception to this rule is the additional
pension which is increased in line with the general level of earnings up to the
time of retirement but only in line with prices thereafter; in that case, the
assumed annual rate of increase in real earnings compared with prices must not
change.

5. If it were not for contracting out it would not be necessary to specify the
actual annual rate of increase of earnings and prices assumed in the ultimate
position but only the single annual rate of increase in real earnings compared
