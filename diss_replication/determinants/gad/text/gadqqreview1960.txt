NATIONAL INSURANCE ACTS, 1946 TO 1959
‘Report by the Government Actuary on the
Second Quinquennial Review
To THE Lorps COMMISSIONERS OF HER MAJESTY’S TREASURY.
May IT PLEASE YOUR LORDSHIPS:

In accordance with the terms of Section 39 (1) (a) of the National
Insurance Act, 1946, I have reviewed the operation of the Acts during the
period 1st Avpril, 1954, to 31st March, 1959, and I submit the following
Report.

I. INTRODUCTION

1. The present review is the second of the full investigations which are
due to ‘be made every five years into the operation of the National Insurance
scheme, the report on the first quinquennial review having been completed
in November, 1954, and published as H.C. Paper No. 1 of 1954-55. Since
then four interim reviews* have been made under Section 39 (1) (6) of the
1946 Act and three non-statutory reportst have been made to the Minister
of Pensions and National Insurance in connection with Bills proposing
major changes in the scheme. Notes on these and other changes since
the last review will be found in Section III of this report.

2. The Act of 1946 directs that in the quinquennial reviews the Govern-
ment Actuary shall make a report to the Treasury on the financial condition
of the National Insurance Fund and the adequacy or otherwise of the
contributions to support the benefits. The National Insurance Act, 1959,
was passed after the end of the period covered by this review and is not
expected to come into operation until April, 1961, but it would be unrealistic
to ignore its provisions in the present report and accordingly in considering
the condition of the Fund full account has been taken of the changes it will
introduce. Further, the adequacy or otherwise of the contributions can only
be judged in the light of the new financial arrangements of the 1959 Act.

TI. SUMMARY OF CONTRIBUTIONS AND BENEFITS

3. Paragraphs 4 to 10 describe the scheme of National Insurance in force
at the end of the period under review. The changes that will result from
the full implementation of the National Insurance Act, 1959, are summarised
in paragraphs 11 to 15.

pensionable age is divided, for the purpose of the National Insurance Acts,
into three classes:

{i) employed persons, ie. all persons gainfully occupied under a
contract of service ;
(ii) self-employed persons, i.e. others who are gainfully occupied ;
(iii) non-employed persons.

Contributions

6. Insured persons are normally required to pay the weekly contribution
appropriate to their class, but students, self-employed and non-employed
persons with incomes not exceeding £156 a year and persons in a few
other special groups need not contribute unless they choose to do so.
Contributions are not payabie by employed persons during periods of
unemployment, nor by employed or self-employed persons during periods
of incapacity for work due to sickness or injury.

7. In the case of employed persons at work a weekly contribution is
payable by the employer even if the employed person is not required to
contribute. In addition, for all classes, the Exchequer makes supplementary
payments in respect of every contribution paid.

Contributions

between insured persons and their employers. The exercise of an option not
to pay the minimum contribution (e.g. by an employed married woman) will

13. The Act provides for graduated additions to retirement pensions for
insured persons and widows to be earned by the payment of graduated
contributions (see Appendix 1). It also introduced an improved scale of
increments for deferment of retirement which was brought into operation
on 3rd August, 1959.

Non-participating employments

14. An employer of adults who are members of a superannuation
scheme which satisfies certain conditions may apply for them to be
treated as being in “non-participating employment ”. If the application is
granted such employees will ibe “ contracted-out ” of the provisions of the
Act relating to graduated contributions and benefits but they will remain
entitled to flat-rate benefits at the present rates and will pay initially the
present contributions set out in Table 1. In April, 1965, and at five-yearly
intervals thereafter until April, 1980, these weekly contributions will be
increased by 1s. 6d. a week, 9d. from the insured person and 9d. from the
employer, unless smaller increases are prescribed.

Exchequer supplements

15. Exchequer supplements under the 1959 Act are to be calculated on
the following ‘basis :—

(i) in respect of contributions for employed adults, whether in non-
participating employments or not—one-quarter of the National
Insurance portion of the minimum rates of contribution, and of the.
quinquennial increases in those contributions ;

(ii) in respect of employed juveniles—one-quarter of the National Insur-
ance portion of the contributions ;

(iii) in respect of self-employed and non-employed persons—one-third
of the National Insurance portion of the contributions.

There is a proviso, however, that the amount of Exchequer supplement
to be paid in any financial year shall not be less than £170 millions.

The National Insurance Act, 1956

18. This Act increased the maximum amounts that retirement pensioners

The Family Allowances and National Insurance Act, 1956

“19. This Act raised the upper age limit for payment of family allow-
ances to age 18 and in consequence the definition of a dependent child for
National Insurance purposes was correspondingly extended. The Act also
made considerable changes in the system of widows’ benefits, including
a provision for paying the “personal” element of a widowed mother’s
allowance to a widow who had living with her a child under age 18 who
had left school. The allowances for widows’ children were also increased.
In view of the comparatively small net financial effect of the changes on the
National Insurance scheme, contribution rates were not altered.
The National Insurance Act, 1957

20. This Act enabled retirement pensioners to elect to be treated as no
longer retired and to earn increments, It also changed the conditions
under which widow beneficiaries attaining aged 60 can become entitled to
retifement pensions and made a number of other changes in the system of
benefits. Contribution rates remained unchanged.
The National Health Service Contributions Act, 1957

21. Under the National Insurance Act, 1946, the weekly National Insur-
ance contributions included sums of 10d. (men) and 8d. (women), the
income from which was transferred to the Exchequer and used to meet some
part of the cost of the National Health Service. Under the National
Health Service Contributions Act, 1957, the National Insurance contributions
were reduced by these amounts but at the same time it was enacted that
insured persons and their employers would in future have to pay separate
National Health Service contributions at the increased rates of 1s. 8d. (men)
and is. 4d. (women). These contributions were later increased to their
present amounts by the National Health Service Contributions Act, 1958.
For convenience a single weekly stamp is still used for National Insurance,
Industrial Injuries and National Health Service contributions.

IV. INCOME AND OuTGO—IsT APRIL, 1954, To 31st Marcu, 1959
Summary of Accounts

23. The transactions of the National Insurance Fund and the National
Insurance (Reserve) Fund for each financial year during the period under
review are summarised in Appendix 2 to this report. As in the first quin-
quennial review and the subsequent interim reports, the figures shown in
the published Accounts have been adjusted to bring into their proper years
certain transactions carried through at later dates. The summary of the
Accounts shows that, over the five-year period, income exceeded outgo by
about £100 millions and, as a result, the amounts held in the two Funds
increased from £1,404 millions at the beginning of the period to £1,505
millions at 31st March, 1959. Ali of this excess of income accrued during
the first three years of the quinquennium ; in 1957-58 income and expendi-
ture were almost equal and in the final year, 1958-59, expenditure was £16
millions in excess of income, despite an additional Exchequer payment of
£39 millions.

24, Of the £1,404 millions held by the two Funds at 1st April, 1954,
£335 millions was held in the National Insurance Fund and £1,069 millions
in the Reserve Fund. In 1955, when the balance in the National
Insurance Fund had increased to nearly £400 millions, my predecessor advised
the Minister of Pensions and National Insurance and the Treasury that,
as in his opinion a fund of about £300 millions was an adequate working
balance for financial transactions under the National Insurance scheme,
a sum of £100 millions should be transferred from the National Insurance
Fund to the Reserve Fund. This transfer was made during 1955-56. At
the end of the period, 31st March, 1959, the National Insurance Fund
amounted to £337 millions and the Reserve Fund to £1,168 millions.
Analysis of income and outgo

25. In the four interim reports published since the first quinquennial
review the experience of the first four years of the period has been
discussed in some detail and there were no unusual features calling for
special comment in the year 1958-59, apart from the grant of about
400,000 retirement pensions to late age entrants* and their wives in July,
1958, to which reference was made in the Eighth Interim Report. The
trend of certain individual items of income and outgo over the five-year
period is discussed in the following paragraphs.

26. On the income side, contributions of insured persons and employers
have increased by more than 50 per cent. from about £450 millions in
1954-55 to nearly £700 millions in 1958-59, while the expenditure on benefits
has increased from about £500 millions to about £880 millions, ie. by
about 75 per cent. Most of the increase in contributions results from the
higher rates prescribed by the National Insurance Act, 1954, and the
National Insurance (No. 2) Act, 1957.

V. ESTIMATES FOR THE FUTURE
The basis of the estimates

36. The first requirement for a calculation of the expected income and
outgo in future years is an estimate of the future population of Great
Britain, sub-divided by sex, age and marital status. The assumptions under-
lying this estimate are explained in Appendix 3.

37. Most of the other items in the actuarial basis of the estimates have
been decided upon in the light of the experience of the National Insurance
scheme from its inception in 1948 and, in particular, during the quin-
quennium 1954 to 1959. For the years 1961-62 onwards allowance has
been made for the effect of the provisions of the National Insurance Act,
1959. The past experience of the National Insurance scheme cannot, how-
ever, give any indication of the effect of the introduction of contributions
and benefits based in part on the earnings of insured persons, and it has
therefore been necessary to use information supplied by the Board of Inland
Revenue. The basis adopted is described in Appendix 4.

38. In three respects the estimates have been based on Government instruc-
tions given for the purpose of these calculations:

(i) It has been assumed that from 1966-67 onwards the average rate
of unemployment will be 3 per cent. (instead of the 4 per cent.
assumed for the first quinquennial review), The rates adopted for
earlier years rise on an arbitrary scale from 12 per cent. for 1960-61
and 1961-62 to 2 per cent. for 1962-63 and 1963-64 and 24 per
cent. for 1964-65 and 1965-66.

44. Another change results from the same Act. Widows’ pensions will
continue payable beyond age 60 until notification of retirement from work
or attainment of age 65, and will be accounted for as expenditure on
widows’ ‘benefits, whereas previously the corresponding expenditure was
charged to retirement pensions. The effect of this change is greater than
might be supposed, as it is not confined to widows at work beyond age 60.
Most widow pensioners are not in employment at that age but can gain
no advantage by notifying retirement. In view of this, no administrative
action is taken to encourage them to give notice of retirement, and
widows’ pensions will thus frequently continue until age 65 when the
widow will automatically ‘be deemed to retire and the pension will be
converted into retirement pension on her own insurance. This procedure,
which was designed to encourage widows to go on working and earn incre-
ments, has complicated the presentation of statistics and financial estimates,
since the attainment of age 60 has ceased to ‘be the dividing line for widows
between widows’ pensions and retirement pensions.

VI. THe ADEQUACY OF THE CONTRIBUTIONS
65. Section IV of the Report on the First Quinquennial Review described
the system of finance in operation on 3ist March, 1954. It was based on
the principle that the contributions payable by and in respect of insured
persons would be related to the “actuarial contribution”, i.e. the con-
tribution for an entrant at the initial age which would be equivalent on
average to the cost of benefits to which he and his dependants would become
entitled assuming the continuance of a fixed rate of interest and the current
rates and conditions of benefit. In accordance with this principle the
adequacy of the contributions was assessed by comparing the actuarial
contributions calculated on the basis then adopted with the rates actually
payable, excluding the additions of 4d. a week which had been made to the
contributions in October, 1951. These additions, for which provision had
been made in the 1946 Act, were intended to relieve the Exchequer of
part of the increase in the cost of benefits, mainly retirement pensions, which

66. The process of placing on the contributor a part of the rindi Yor

extended under the National Insurance Act, 1954, and the National Insur-

67. The method of finance introduced by this Act has been termed the
“ pay-as-you-go” or “‘ assessment”? system because the aim is to charge
rates of contribution itthat will result in an approximate balance between
the income and the outgo of the National Insurance Fund over short periods
of years. Thus from April, 1961, the National Insurance Funds would
remain at about the same level, with relatively minor fluctuations, without
Exchequer aid other than the supplements prescribed by Section 1 (3) @

contributions payable under the National Insurance Acts to support the
benefits has now to be determined by comparing the estimates of future
income and outgo.

68. The figures in Table 14 show that the income of the Fund is expected
to exceed the outgo over the five year period beginning in April, 1961,
and again from 1976 onwards. For the 10 years 1966-76, however, the
estimates show deficits which will average between £15 millions and £20
millions a year. These deficits are less than 2 per cent. of the income of
the Fund, so that even small departures from the assumptions could suffice
to balance income and outgo ; for example, if the average rate of unemploy-
ment from 1966-67 onwards were 24 per cent. instead of 3 per cent., the
financial position would be improved by over £15 millions a year.

69. To sum up, it appears that over a long period the contributions, with
the proposed quinquennial increases, should prove adequate to support the
benefits of the scheme although this may not be true over the years 1966-76.
It is particularly difficult at the present time to make close estimates of the
future progress of the scheme because of uncertainties as to such important
factors as the trend of earnings and the effect of the option to contract-out.
When the next quinquennial review comes to be made the working of the
scheme should enable some of these uncertainties to be removed and the
estimates should be correspondingly more reliable. It does, however, appear
that any general increase in benefits would have to be accompanied by a
provision for increasing the income in order to maintain the “ pay-as-you-go ”
basis of the scheme.

3. Insured persons’ contribution cards are exchanged each year on one of four

relate to any one week in the year.

Deferment of retirement

13. Expenditure on retirement pensions is affected by the extent to which
insured: persons defer their retirement beyond the minimum pensionable age, thus
forgoing their pensions but earning increments which are paid on eventual retire-
ment or at age 70 (men) or 65 (women). In the first review it was assumed
that 35 per cent. of men would take pension immediately at age 65, that there
would be an even flow of retirements during the next five years, and that 25 per
cent. of those attaining age 70 would still be at work at that age. For women,
the proportion taking pensions at age 60 was assumed to be 60 per cent. and
15 per cent. of those attaining 65 were assumed to be still at work.

14. An examination of the statistics of awards of pension to men who attained
age 65 in the period July, 1951, to July, 1954 (and hence reached age 70 by
July, 1959) indicated that for these men the proportion retiring immediately at
age 65 was nearly 40 per cent. as against the 35 per cent. assumed; the pro-
portion still at work at age 70 was, however, very close to the 25 per cent.
assumed. There are also indications that from mid-1957 onwards there was an
acceleration of retirement, both amongst those reaching age 65 and amongst
those who had reached that age previously and had deferred retirement; as a
result about 43 per cent. of those attaining age 65 in the year beginning in July,
1957, retired immediately. It is, of course, too early to say how this will affect
the proportion still at work at 70.

15. All the statistics referred to in the last paragraph relate to men reaching
the minimum pensionable age before July, 1958, and exclude the experience of
the late-age entrants who, under the special provisions of the 1946 Act, were
deemed to retire on Sth July, 1958. From that date the general provisions of
the scheme apply to those men reaching age 65 who were first brought into
insurance in 1948, and it may well be that the extent to which they will defer
retirement will differ from that of the classes who were insured under the earlier
schemes. The statistics for the period mid-1958 to mid-1959 indicate that
about 47 per cent. of men reaching age 65 in the period retired at once, but it
is not clear how much of the increase in this percentage was due to the inclusion
of those newly insured in 1948 and how much to a different retirement pattern.

16. The extent to which insured persons postpone retirement is affected by
a number of influences and, in particular, by the growing numbers entitled to
an occupational pension. A factor operating in the other direction is the drive
to extend the employment opportunities open to the elderly. It was decided to
assume that 45 per cent. of men will retire at age 65, this representing broadly
the experience of the years 1957 to 1959, but not to allow for any future change.
As a consequence, the proportion assumed to be still at work at age 70 has been
reduced to 20 per cent.

17. It would appear that women have also retired, on average, rather earlier
than was expected. The statistics have proved difficult to interpret because of
the changes brought about by the National Insurance Act, 1957, affecting widows
who attain age 60, but it is probable that the increase in the proportion retiring
at the minimum age was less than for men. It has been assumed that 65 per
cent. of women who are contributing at age 60 will retire immediately on attain-
ing that age (cf. 60 per cent. at the first review), ‘but that the proportion still at
work at age 65 will remain at 15 per cent.
