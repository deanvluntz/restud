I. INTRODUCTION

1. The present review is the third of the full investigations which are due to
be made every five years into the operation of the Nationai Insurance scheme,
the report on the previous quinquennial review having been published as
H.C. Paper No. 220 of 1959-60. Since then four interim reviews* have been
made under Section 39(1)(5) of the 1946 Act and two non-statutory reportst
have been made to the Minister of Pensions and National Insurance in con-
nection with Bills proposing major changes in the scheme. The main changes
since the last review are summarised below.

National Insurance Acts 1959 and 1960

2. The 1959 Act, which was mentioned in my Report on the Second
Quinquennial Review, introduced graduated contributions and benefits which
came into effect in April 1961. Flat-rate contributions for employed persons
within the scope of the graduated scheme were to be reduced from the same
date, but there was to be no major change in flat-rate benefits. This Act also
improved the scales of increments for deferment of retirement in respect of
contributions paid from August 1959. The rates of benefits and contributions
which actually became effective in April 1961 were, however, those of the
1960 Act which amended the 1959 Act by increasing the flat-rate benefits and
contributions, although it did not alter its provisions regarding graduated
contributions and benefits. The main weekly benefit rates were raised from
50s. Od. to 57s. 6d. for a single person, and from 80s. Od. to 92s. 6d. for a
married couple.

Family Allowances and National Insurance Act 1961

3. This Act provided for increments to a widow’s retirement pension to be
granted in respect of contributions paid by her late husband while he was over,
and she was under, pension age. It also prescribed an earnings limit below
which an apprentice can qualify as a child dependant.

National Insurance Act 1963

4. Benefit rates were increased from dates in March and May 1963 to basic
levels of 67s. 6d. for a single person and 109s. for a married couple; to meet
the additional cost the flat-rate contributions were raised from June 1963 and
the upper limit of the range of weekly earnings liable to graduated contributions

6. During the period four changes in the earnings limits have increased the

relaxation of the earnings rule for widowed mothers was introduced by the
National Insurance Act 1963 which fixed a limit of 26s. a week below which
the personal part of such an allowance could not be reduced by reason of
earnings.

Ij. SUMMARY OF CONTRIBUTIONS AND BENEFITS
Persons Iasured

7. The population of Great Britain over school-leaving age and under
pensionable age is divided, for the purposes of the National Insurance Acts,
into three classes:

(i) employed persons, i.e. all persons gainfully occupied under a contract
- of service; :
(ii) self-employed persons, i.e. others who are gainfully occupied;
(iii) non-employed persons.
Married women are treated specially; subject to the satisfaction of certain
conditions they may elect whether to pay contributions or not. Modified
conditions of insurance apply to a number of special groups such as members
of the armed forces, seamen, airmen and students.
Contributions

8. With certain exceptions insured persons are required to pay weekly flat-
rate contributions appropriate to their class and employed persons also pay a
percentage of their earnings between £9 and £18 a week unless they are
contracted-out of the graduated part of the scheme (see paragraph 10). Self-
employed and non-employed persons with incomes below £208 a year, married
women, widow beneficiaries, students and a few other special groups may elect
not to pay flat-rate contributions. Nor are such contributions payable by
employed persons while unemployed or by employed or self-employed persons
while incapacitated for work through sickness or injury; in such events credits
are normally given to ensure that title to benefits is maintained.

9. In the case of employed persons at work, flat-rate and graduated National
Insurance contributions are payable by employers at rates equal to those of the
employed persons and similar employers contributions are payable for such
employees who are not themselves paying flat-rate contributions.

at the maximum level, and the equivalent pension which has accrued to an
employee who leaves contracted-out employment below the retirement age has
to be preserved, either in a superannuation scheme or, by means of a “ payment
in lieu ” of contributions, through the National Insurance scheme.

12. In addition to the flat-rates shown in Table 1, employed persons who are
not contracted-out and their employers each pay 44 per cent. of the employee’s
earnings between £9 and £18 a week, the maximum graduated contributions
for a person earning £18 or more in any week being 7s. 8d. each from the
employed person and employer. These contributions are collected in association
with P.A.Y.E. income tax.

13. The 1959 Act provides for the rates of contributions to be increased in
April 1965, and at five-yearly intervals thereafter up to 1980, without any
increase in benefits. The Acts specify the amounts of the increases, which are
the same on each occasion, but smaller increases than those laid down in the
Acts may be prescribed by the Minister of Pensions and National Insurance,
with the consent of the Treasury. As the Acts stand, the rates shown in Table |
for employed persons not contracted-out would be increased by 5d. a week,
with equal amounts payable by the employer, while the graduated contributions
would be increased for each party by } per cent. of the relevant earnings. The
contributions of contracted-out employed persons and of their employers as
well as those of self-employed and non-employed adults would be increased by
10d. a week.

14. Exchequer supplements to the flat-rate contributions are calculated on
the following basis on the National Insurance portion of the contribution and
any future quinquennial increases :—

(i) in respect of contributions for employed persons—one-quarter of the
contributions at the basic rate, no Exchequer supplement being payable
on the additional contributions paid in respect of persons who are
contracted-out;

(ii) in respect of self-employed and non-employed persons—one-third of the
contributions.

Graduated contributions do not attract Exchequer supplements.
Benefits

15. The Acts provide for the following benefits:

(i) unemployment benefit (for employed persons only);

(ii) sickness benefit (for employed and self-employed persons);

(iii) maternity benefits, comprising maternity grant, home confinement grant
and maternity allowance;

(iv) widows’ benefits, comprising widow’s allowance, widowed mother’s
allowance and widow’s pension; under transitional arrangements some
widows, who would not otherwise be entitled to benefit, have a reserved
right to a pension of 10s. a week;

(v) guardian’s allowance and child’s special allowance;

(vi) retirement pension, including graduated additions and, under tran-
sitional arrangements, contributory old age pension of 10s. a week for
some widows;

(vii) death grant.

Summary of Accounts

17. The transactions of the National Insurance Fund and the National
Insurance (Reserve) Fund for each financial year during the period under
review are summarised in Appendix 2 of this Report. Expenditure exceeded
income in each of the five years except 1961-62, when there was a smail surplus
of £13 millions. The net deficit for the whole five-year period was £1214
millions, and the combined Funds fell by this amount from nearly £1,506
millions on Ist April 1959. There were no transfers of capital between the two
Funds during the period, and the balance of £1,384 millions at 31st March 1964
was made up of £1,168 millions held in the Reserve Fund and £216 millions in
the National Insurance Fund.

Analysis of income and outgo

18. The trend of income and outgo over the whole five years is discussed in
the following paragraphs. The experience of the first four years of the period
has already been discussed in some detail in the Interim Reports published
since the second quinquennial review. It should be noted that the particulars
shown for 1963-64 represent less than a full year’s transactions at the rates in
force at the end of the period since the 1963 Act increases were not brought
into effect until the end of May for retirement pension and other long-term
benefits, and the beginning of June for contributions.

19. On the income side, contributions by insured persons and employers
rose by 56 per cent, from £708 millions in 1959-60 to £1,105 millions in 1963-64.
Of this increase, £222 millions resulted from the introduction of graduated
contributions, mentioned in paragraph 12 of this report, and most of the balance
was due to increases in the rates of weekly flat-rate contributions. The growth
in the number of contributors slackened and there was only a small increase in
flat-rate contributions from employed married women because an increasing
proportion chose not to contribute themselves. The yield from graduated
contributions showed an increase because of the growth in the employed pop-
ulation and increases in average earnings, and because the ceiling up to which
these contributions are charged was raised to £18 in June 1963.

IV. ESTIMATES FOR THE FUTURE
The basis of the estimates

29. Underlying the estimates of future numbers of contributors and
beneficiaries and of income and outgo which are presented in this section of the
report is a projection of the population of Great Britain, analysed by sex,
age and, for women, marital status. The assumptions on which this projection is
based are described in Appendix 3, and details of the projected populations are
given in Table 34 in Appendix 5. The numbers given in this projection have been
combined with factors relating to the various classes of contributors and
beneficiaries. These factors were adopted in the light of the experience of
the scheme in recent years, with such allowance as seemed necessary for future
trends. A detailed description of the bases of the calculations is given in
Appendix 3.

30. in three respects the estimates have been based on Government instructions
given for the purposes of this review.

(i) It has been assumed that from 1965-66 onwards the average rate of
unemployment will be 2 per cent. (For long term forecasts the comparable
rate assumed in the first quinquennial review was 4 per cent., and in
the second quinquennial review 3 per cent.) For 1964-65 the number
unemployed was assumed to average 375,000, equivalent to a rate of
about 1-+6 per cent.

(ii) The level of earnings has been assumed to remain constant throughout
the period covered by the estimates.

(iii) Contracting-out has been assumed to remain at its present proportions.
This implies that the numbers in non-participating employment will
rise slowly in line with the growth in the employed population, from
44 millions at present to over 5 millions in 30 years’ time.

31. The assumption of a constant level of earnings, which has been taken
as the average level in 1964-65, is appropriate for the purpose of assessing, on
the “ pay-as-you-go’ principle, the adequacy of the contributions for the
scheme as it stands at present. Increases of earnings are expected in future
but it may be assumed that, as in the past, these would be accompanied by
enlargements of benefits. The financial effect of such changes and the resulting
position of the scheme would be examined afresh on each occasion so as to
restore the balance of income and outgo which is required under a “ pay-as-
you-go”’ arrangement; it is clearly not possible to forecast the form or the
incidence of such increases, nor the contribution changes which will accompany
them, but the figures based on constant earnings indicate the relative position
in such circumstances.

Contributors and Beneficiaries
Contributors

32. The numbers of contributors in each class were estimated for 1963 and
for five-yearly intervals thereafter up to 2003, and are summarised for certain
years in Table 3 below. A detailed analysis of the numbers in 1963 will be found
in Appendix 5, Tables 36 and 37.

33. Table 3 shows that the number of contributors is expected to rise slowly
from nearly 25 millions in 1963 to 27 millions in 1983, and more rapidly
thereafter to reach 324 millions by 2003. The number of men increases from
nearly 16$ millions to over 214 millions during the forty-year period, while the
number of women contributors rises in much the same proportion, from
8} millions to nearly 11 millions. With one exception the growth in the overall
number of contributors corresponds closely to the growth in the total population
of contributing age (men between ages 15 and 65 and women between ages
15and 60) throughout the period covered by the estimates. Ithas been announced
that the school-leaving age will be raised from 15 to 16 in the academic year
1970-71, and the consequent fall in the number of National Insurance con-
tributors at that time has been allowed for. The numbers are also affected by
the tendency for more persons to continue their education after the minimum
leaving age.

34. Two important points should be noted about the numbers of women
contributors shown in Table 3. The first is that the proportion of married
women in the total population is expected to rise. By itself, this would be
expected to restrict the growth in the number of contributors, since National
Insurance contributions are paid for only a minority of married women, whereas
most single women contribute. The second point is the strong tendency in
recent years for more married women to come into employment, and this is
expected to continue. Broadly, these factors offset each other, so that the
estimated future number of women contributors increases in line with the total
population of contributing age. By 2003, however, two-thirds of all women
contributors are expected to be married, compared with a proportion a little
greater than one-half in 1963. Since employed married women may, and most
in fact do, choose not to pay contributions themselves, almost all the increase
in the number of women contributors between 1963 and 2003 is among the
“employer’s contribution only ” cases.

APPENDIX 1
RATES AND CONDITIONS OF BENEFIT

1. In return for weekly contributions, the National Insurance scheme provides
benefits in the event of unemployment, sickness, maternity, widowhood, orphanhood,
retirement and death. Title to benefit at the standard rates quoted in the following
paragraphs is dependent on the full satisfaction of contribution conditions. Most
benefits are subject to reduction if contribution records are deficient.
Unemployment and sickness benefits

2. The standard rate of these benefits is 67s. 6d. a week (with additions for
dependants*). Unemployment benefit is payable for the first 30 weeks of unemploy-
ment occurring in a “period of interruption of employment” due to sickness or
unemployment (see below); after the first 30 weeks, benefit may be paid for additional
periods, up to a maximum of a further year, according to the insured person’s
contribution and benefit records. Sickness benefit, however, is normally paid for all
sickness occurring in such a period, subject to the limitation that not more than
52 weeks of such benefit are payable in any one period of interruption of employment
until 156 contributions have been paid.

3. A “period of interruption of employment” is defined so that two periods of
unemployment or sickness are regarded as part of the same period unless they are
separated by at least 13 weeks. Benefit is not paid for isolated days of unemployment
or sickness nor for the first three days of a period which lasts for less than 12 days.

4. A married woman receives benefit at a lower rate (46s. a week), unless she is
living apart from, and is not receiving any financial help from, her husband, or is
maintaining a husband incapable of self-support, when the full weekly rate of 67s. 6d.
is paid. The rate of benefit for juveniles under 18 is normally 38s. 6d. a week.
Maternity benefits

5. On the confinement (or in anticipation of the confinement) of an insured woman,
or the wife of an insured man, a maternity grant of £16 is payable. If more than one
child is born the sum paid is increased to £16 for each child. If the confinement is in
the mother’s home or if she is confined elsewhere at her own expense a home confinement
grant of £6 is also paid.

6. In addition to these benefits, gainfully-occupied women who are themselves
paying contributions receive a maternity allowance of 67s. 6d. a week (with appropriate
additions for dependants) for a period normally of 18 weeks.

Widows’ benefits

7. For the first 13 weeks of widowhood the widow of an insured man receives a
widow's allowance of 95s. a week (with additions if she has dependent children) unless
she was over 60 and her husband was entitled to a retirement pension. Widow’s
allowance may be followed by a widowed mother’s allowance payable at the rate of
67s. 6d. a week, ‘with additions of 37s. 6d. for the first child, 29s. 6d. for the second
child and 27s. 6d. for each subsequent child so long as the widow has a dependent
child under age 19 who has not yet left school. A widowed mother’s allowance at the
“* personal ” rate of 67s. 6d. is payable to a widow whose children have left school but
who has a young person under age 19 living with her. If on the termination of widow's
allowance the widow is not entitled to widowed mother’s allowance she receives a
widow’s pension at 67s. 6d. a week if she was between 50 and 60 at widowhood and had

then been married for not less than three years. Further, if the widow had attained
age 60 at widowhood, a retirement pension is usually payable on her husband’s
insurance, either from widowhood or following a widow’s allowance. Widow’s
pension is also payable on the termination of widowed mother’s allowance if the widow
is then over age 50 (age 40 if her husband died before 4th February 1957) and not less
than three years have elapsed since marriage.

8. Widowed mother’s allowance or widow’s pension is reduced if the widow’s
earnings exceed certain limits, and benefit ceases if the widow remarries.

9. When a widow receiving onc of the widows’ benefits attains the minimum

pensionable age of 60, the benefit may continue to be paid beyond that age, though

for each 12 such contributions he has paid after she had attained age 60. Thus, if a
man earns the maximum of 21 increments his pension at age 70 will be 88s. 6d. a week
and his wife may receive 52s., increasing to 88s. 6d. on widowhood. Persons who
defer retirement are eligible for unemployment and sickness benefit during the period
of deferment. A widow retirement pensioner also receives increments of 6d. a week
for every 12 contributions paid by her late husband after he had reached 65 but before
she had reached 60.

15. Graduated additions to the flat-rate pensions described above are payable at the
rate of 6d. a week for each £7 10s.* of graduated contributions paid by a man or £9*
bya woman. A widow over age 60 is entitled to one-half of any graduated addition
earned by her late husband, together with any addition earned by her own graduated
contributions. Graduated additions are subject to the retirement condition and further
amounts are added in respect of graduated additions forgone and contributions paid
during deferment of retirement.

Death grant

16. On the death of an insured person, or of certain dependants of an insured
person, a death grant is payable. The grant is normally £25 on the death of an adult,
with smaller amounts on the death of a child, but a reduced grant of £12 10s. is payable
on the death of those aged 55 to 65 (men) or 50 to 60 (women) on 5th July 1948;
no death grants are payable on the death of persons who were over 65 (men) or 60
(women) on Sth July 1948.

6. Migration. The net amount of migration to and from Great Britain has
fluctuated widely since the War. It is affected both by economic circumstances and by
Government policy, and estimates of future net migration can be little more than
notional figures, at any rate for the long term. In the early part of this year a Working
Party of interested Departments advised that it would be reasonable to assume a net
inward migration into Great Britain of about 30,000 a year. Though in the last few
years the inward movement has been predominantly male, the most recent figures
showed approximate equality in numbers between the sexes and this equality has
been assumed to continue. Little information is available about the age distribution
of migrants, but it is clear that they are largely young men and women and there is
evidence of a small outward movement at older ages; the age distribution of migrants
assumed for the projections took account of these factors.

7. Future Births. The number of future legitimate births depends both on the
numbers of married women and on the size and spacing of their families. For women
in England and Wales there has been a steady trend towards earlier marriage. This
may well be halted for a time as the large number of girls born in 1947 attain marriage-
able age, but in the longer term the trend will probably be renewed. It was therefore
assumed that by 1973 marriage rates at ages under 20 would be 20 per cent. greater
than in recent years. It was also assumed that, as a consequence, marriage rates at
ages over 25 would ultimately decrease; otherwise the proportion of women remaining
spinsters at age 30 would be unduly low.

8. Having made estimates in this way of future numbers of married women in England
and Wales, it was necessary to consider the rate at which they are likely to build their
families. Interpretation of statistics of past family-building is difficult because of the
effects of abnormal war and post-war conditions. It is, however, clear that, although
women who married in the early 1950’s were relatively slow to start their families,
they bore more children in the period between 3 and 9 years from marriage than those
married a few years earlier. It seems probable that women married in the early
1950’s will eventually have families rather Jarger than those of women married in the
1940's. In contrast, those married in the late 1950’s started their families relatively
quickly, but it is too soon to say whether this early child-bearing will result in a
substantial increase in average size of completed family.

Numbers of contributors

15. The estimated numbers of contributors given in Table 3 represent the average
numbers of persons within the scope of the insurance scheme during each year
including cases where contributions are excused because of unemployment, maternity
or incapacity for work, and correspond to the figures published annually in the Interim
Reports on the scheme, and in the Annual Abstract of Statistics. The estimates are
derived principally from the flat-rate contribution records of individuals, which are
maintained by the Ministry of Pensions and National Insurance from the insurance
cards or other documents used to provide evidence of payment of flat-rate contributions.
The cards have a currency of twelve months, but in order to spread the work of issuing
them over the year, it is arranged for one quarter to be exchanged on each of four
dates, at the beginning of March, June, September and December. Where fiat-
rate contributions are paid by methods other than stamped cards, the documents
used also cover periods of a year ending on one of these four dates. Statistics have
been compiled from a 2 per cent. sample of the insured population, divided between
these four exchange dates, the status of an individual being determined in general by
the stamp or credit recorded for the last week of currency of his insurance card.
Thus the statistics derived from the sample relate to four dates spread over the year. .
The numbers in the sample on any one date represent only 4 per cent. of the insured
population, and estimates based on one date only would be subject to considerable
margins of error when analysed by age-groups, particularly for the smaller classes.
Furthermore, because of seasonal fluctuations, the numbers in any particular week
may not be typical. It is therefore preferable to average the results for the four
exchange dates in each year. The averages so obtained give a measure of the mean
numbers over the year, although they do not relate to any one week in the year.

16. There is a relatively small number of contributors for whom no information is
available for the last week of the contribution year, but who may nevertheless remain
within the scope of the scheme. The statistical tabulations for 1960 and later years
have been extended to provide a more satisfactory basis for estimating the number
and insurance class of such persons by reference to their contribution records during
the years immediately before and after the exchange dates.

17. Since the last quinquennial review, there has been another change in the
statistics. It was then the practice to record the insurance class of a contributor not
only for the last week of his contribution year, but also for a week three months
earlier, and this extra record was used in conjunction with the records of persons who
had exchanged their cards three months earlier. Although this procedure yielded
more information from the sample, it necessarily increased, by three months, the time
taken to prepare the statistics. On balance, it seemed advantageous to return to the
procedure of recording each person’s status at the end of the contribution year only,
and this change was made during 1959.

Graduated Contributions

25. Graduated contributions are collected through the P.A.Y.E. system along with
Schedule E income tax, and thus the basic records from which statistics must ultimately
be derived are the tax deduction cards on which employers enter individual earnings
and contributions. When the cards are received by the Ministry of Pensions and
National Insurance after the end of the tax year, the contributions paid by each employee
are recorded to provide a basis for awards of graduated benefits. For statistical
purposes the records of contributors in the 2 per cent. sample are extracted and, for
one-half of these, the earnings for the year are ascertained from a microfilm record of
the original tax documents and linked with the basic data and records of graduated
contributions. The data include two classes of persons who did not pay graduated
contributions; first, those contracted-out, and secondly, employed persons who were
not liable for graduated contributions because they did not earn £9 or more in any
week during the year. Some of the latter group have subsequently become liable
because the level of earnings has risen since the period to which the statistics relate.
Although contributions are assessed on periodical (weekly, monthly, etc.) earnings,
many tax deduction cards show only the total earnings and contributions paid during
the year and it is thus annual rather than weekly amounts which are available for use.

26. When compiling the statistics it was necessary to divide the data between
participants (i.e., those within the ambit of the graduated scheme, including people
with small earnings referred to above) and the contracted-out. Some problems were
enceuntered in making this classification since the details given on the tax deduction
cards were not always complete. Moreover, it was not always possible to distinguish
between employed contributors and persons such as certain directors, and pensioners
under occupational pension schemes, who are taxed through the P.A.Y.E. system but
who are not employed contributors for National Insurance purposes. There was a
residual group of persons who, in the absence of other information, had to be
classified arbitrarily. It is hoped to overcome these problems in future by a method

31. A“ payment in lieu of contributions ” becomes due after a period of contracted-

being as follows.

(i) Sickness benefit is only payable to employed married women or widow
beneficiaries who have been paying contributions themselves. About 70 per

(iii) Persons over the minimum pensionable ages of 65 (men) or 60 (women) are

65 (women).

(iv) Members of the armed forces and foreign-going mariners while at sea are
excluded.

(v) Sickness benefit is not usually paid for the first three days of a spell of sickness
which does not last 12 days and most illnesses lasting less than four days are
not reported io the Ministry.

(vi) Persons who suffer prolonged illness before they have paid 156 contributions
are subject to a limit of one year’s sickness benefit unless they are able to
resume work for 13 weeks. The statistics are thus slightly deficient in long-
term sickness, but the number of persons in question is very small.

(vii) There is a special administrative arrangement whereby most non-industrial
civil servants have undertaken not to claim sickness benefit under the scheme
until an illness has lasted six months; the deductions from pay (equal to the
amount of sickness benefit), which would otherwise be made, are waived.

Basis of the Investigation

5. The sample data have been obtained from the sickness records of all persons
whose National Insurance numbers end in 4B or 8B. The insured population is
divided into four groups whose contribution cards are exchanged in ivfarch, June,
September and December respectively. The suffix letter B distinguishes those whose
cards are current for years beginning on the first Monday in June and the selection of
the two final digits 4 and 8 within this group thus leads to a 5 per cent. sample.

6. The selection of a contribution year as the period for tabulating sickness benefit
makes it possible to secure a check on the completeness of the statistical sample by
reference to other information necessarily recorded in connection with contribution
records. There are two particular advantages in favour of the years beginning in June.
First, the sickness of a whole winter is included in one set of annual tables and,
secondly, the task of completing the statistical records in the Ministry’s local offices at
the end of each period occurs in the summer when there are fewer sickness claims.
