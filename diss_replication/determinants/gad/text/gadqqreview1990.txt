Section 1
Summary of the Review
1.1 A major purpose of the five-yearly reviews by the Government Actuary of
the operation of the Social Security Acts is to establish the rates of contribution
likely to be required in future years to meet the cost of the benefits provided for
under the National Insurance Scheme.
1.2 The scheme is financed on the pay-as-you-go principle with the contri-
bution rates set from year to year to produce the income needed to meet
current expenditure on benefits and costs of administration. The rates of
contribution are determined by the level of benefits and by the relative
numbers of contributors and beneficiaries, these numbers being very materially
affected by demographic factors. Projections are made for a period of 60 years
into the future to illustrate the impact of demographic changes and the gradual
maturing of the provisions of the Scheme.
1.3 The main benefit under the Scheme in terms of cost is the retirement
pension, which currently accounts for about 75 per cent of total expenditure.
The population at present includes about 10 million people over pension age
and this number is expected to remain broadly constant over the next 10 years.
Thereafter, with the changing age structure of the population and steady
reductions in mortality rates, the number will increase gradually to reach a
peak of over 14 million in the 2030s. The numbers in the population at the
working ages are expected to remain relatively stable, on the main assumption
of births averaging 2.0 per woman in the long-term future. As a result there wiil
be a significant deterioration in the ratio of the number of contributors to the
number of pensioners under the scheme which, other things being equal, would
require steady increases in contribution rates over the early decades of the next
century, followed by a reversal of the trend after the year 2035.
1.4 Another factor which will tend to increase contribution rates is the
gradual build-up of the cost of earnings-related additional pensions. Employees
started to earn additional pension in April 1978 and no-one will have earned a
full entitlement until 1998. Thereafter it will be a further 20 years or so before
all pensions in payment reflect the additional earnings-related element.
1.5 The rates of contribution required to meet the pay-as-you-go cost of the
Scheme are also affected by changes in the relationship between the flat-rate
benefits and the earnings on which contributions are paid. The Act requires the
main benefit rates to be reviewed each year and increased at least to reflect
movements in the Retail Price Index. However, the income of the Scheme
arises mainly from earnings-related contributions and will thus grow broadly in
step with changes in the general level of earnings, which can be expected over
the long term to increase rather faster than prices. If the flat-rate benefits are
increased in line with prices the effect will be to offset the increases in
contributions required as a result of the cther factors referred to above.
1.6 The extent, if any, to which flat-rate benefits will be increased in future by
more than is required to maintain their present purchasing power will be a
matter for the government of the day, having regard to pressures on public
expenditure, the growth of alternative sources of income for pensioners, such
as occupational and personal pensions, and other factors. Estimates are shown
in the report on illustrative alternative assumptions about future upratings,
namely, (i) that, as required by the Act as a minimum, upratings will continue
to be in line with the movement in prices and (ii) that upratings will maintain

1.7 On the basis of upratings in line with prices and the assumptions set out in

falling significantly in the more distant future. This broadly confirms the

projections. On the prices uprating basis the fall in the level of flat-rate benefits

the growing cost of additional pensions over the next 40 years. Thereafter a
gradual fall in the contribution rate can be expected as the demographic trends
are reversed and the impact of the 1986 Act is fully reflected in the costs.
1.8 With upratings in line with earnings, and the same assumptions in regard
to other factors, there would be no offset to the factors leading to higher rates
of contributions and the required rates for those not contracted-out would rise
steadily from the present 17/4 per cent to 20 per cent after 20 years and a peak
of 25 per cent after 40 years, before declining gradually to 23 per cent by 2050.
1.9 It should be emphasized that these estimates are not forecasts of what
contribution rates will in the event be needed but projections of what would
happen on the basis of the stated assumptions. The demographic and economic
assumptions underlying the estimates are inevitably subject to a considerable
degree of uncertainty, particularly for the more distant future, and the effects
of variations in the more important assumptions are given in Section 10 of the
report.
1.10 Contributions at the current level should be broadly adequate to meet
the cost of the benefits and the costs of administration over the next 40 years if
benefits are uprated in accordance with the minimum required by the Act.
However, considerable fluctuations may occur in the short term because, for
example, of changes in the relationship between prices and earnings, the level
of unemployment, etc. It may be necessary to make modest adjustments to the

’ tate of contribution from time to time to deal with these situations, since the
National Insurance Fund is no more than a working balance and is not intended
to be sufficient to smooth out significant fluctuations from year to year in
outgo. The report suggests that the Fund should not be allowed to fall below
one-sixth of one year’s expenditure on benefits and costs of administration.
1.11 The contracted-out rebate, on the assumptions underlying the 1987
review, may be expected to fall from 5.8 per cent currently to 3.4 per cent in
2020-21 because of the reducing cost to occupational pension schemes of
providing guaranteed minimum pensions. Thus the net contribution rates for
the contracted-out will rise gradually over the next 30 years even though the
standard rate of class 1 contribution may remain steady.

Section 2
Introduction
2.1 The National Insurance Scheme is financed on the pay-as-you-go prin-
ciple with rates of contribution set to produce the income to meet current
expenditure on benefits and costs of administration. With this system of finance
the rates of contribution required may alter significantly over the years, as a
result of the maturing of the benefit rights under the scheme or on account of
demographic or other factors leading to changes in the relative numbers of
pensioners and contributors. For this reason the legislation governing the
National Insurance Scheme in Great Britain has always made provision for
5-yearly reviews by the Government Actuary on the operation of the scheme,
including long term projections of the expenditure and of the corresponding
rates of contribution likely to be required over the years.
2.2 The requirement for such reviews under the current legislation arises from
Section 137 of the Social Security Act 1975 which provides for 5-yearly reviews
with the object, as stated in section 137(3), of determining “the extent to which
the level at which the National Insurance Fund stands from year to year may be
expected in the longer term to bear proper relation to demands in respect of
payments of benefits”. The present review is the second of the full investiga-
tions into the operation of the Scheme under this section of the 1975 Act and
relates to the five years up to April 1985. Account has been taken of changes in
the legislation relating to the Scheme up to and including the Social Security
Act 1989. Although the Social Security Acts 1986, 1988 and 1989 were passed
after the effective date, it would have been unrealistic to ignore their provisions
in considering the future financial condition of the Fund in this review.
2.3 The report on the first Quinquennial Review was completed in July 1982
and published as H.C. Paper No 451 of 1982-83. Since that date regular annual
reports, currently under Section 121(1) and 123A(6A) of the Social Security
Act 1975 and Section 63(11) of the Social Security Act 1986, have been made
on the likely short-term financial effect on the National Insurance Fund of
amendments to the provisions for contributions and benefit rates. Long-term
financial projections for the Scheme were given in the Government Actuary’s
report, published as Cmad 9711, on the financial effect of the Social Security
Bill 1986, which introduced major changes to the earnings-related component
of retirement and other pensions. The present review updates those estimates
in the light of further changes in the legislation and developments in the factors
determining future income and outgo as described in this report.
2.4 At the time of writing, the Social Security Bill 1990, which proposes a
number of changes affecting the National Insurance Fund, is before Parlia-
ment. The main results in this report do not allow for these proposed changes
but the estimated effect on the required contribution rates in future years is set
out in Section 11 of the report.

Section 3
Social Security Acts 1980 to 1989
3.1 The main legislative changes which have affected the entitlement to
benefits and the structure of contributions in the National Insurance Fund
during the quinquennium since the previous review, or which have been
enacted subsequently, are summarized briefly in the following paragraphs.
Further comments are included in later sections where the details of contri-
butions and benefits relevant for the financial projections are set out. Many of
these changes have a significant effect on the expected future income or
expenditure. The changes to the provisions governing the regular reviews of the
benefit rates and contribution rates and the limits determining contribution
liability are considered in Section 7.
3.2 The first Quinquennial Review took account of the Social Security Acts
enacted between 1980 and 1982 in projecting future contribution income and
benefit expenditure. However, the changes introduced by these Acts also affect
comparisons of actual expenditure in the five years 1980-81 to 1984-85 and so
are included in the following summary.
3.3 The 1980 Act transferred, with effect from 1982, expenditure on the
maternity grant from the National Insurance Fund to be a direct charge on
money provided by Parliament.
3.4 The 1980 (No 2) Act phased out the earnings-related supplement paid
with short-term benefits, i.e. unemployment benefit, sickness benefit, matern-
ity allowance and widow’s allowance. It also introduced a lower level of
uprating for these short-term benefits and for invalidity benefit. This lower
level of uprating applied for the November 1980 uprating but subsequent
upratings have been such that the reduced benefit level resulting from this Act
now persists only for maternity allowance and sickness benefit. The 1980 (No 2)
Act also introduced an abatement of unempioyment benefit for beneficiaries
over age 60 who were in receipt of an occupational pension of more than a
certain amount. The age limit for this was reduced to 55 by the 1988 Act.
3.5 The Social Security and Housing Benefits Act 1982 introduced statutory
sick pay payable by employers with reimbursement from the National Insur-
ance Fund. The original provisions governing statutory sick pay were amended
in the Social Security Act 1985 and, as a result, for most employees statutory
sick pay is now paid where sickness benefit would previously have been
payable. The 1982 Act also abolished injury benefit and amended the terms
under which industrial disablement benefit is payable.
3.6 The 1985 Act introduced reduced rates of contribution for people with
low earnings and required employers, but not employees, to pay contributions
on earnings above the upper earnings limit.
3.7 The 1986 Act made substantial changes to the earnings-related additional
pensions paid to retirement, widow and invalidity pensioners. In particular:
(a) the ultimate target for additional pension was reduced from 25 per
cent to 20 per cent of relevant earnings, the reduction being phased in
from the year 2000 onwards;

(b) instead of being based on the average of the best 20 years revalued
earnings, the additional pension is to be based on revalued earnings

(c) from April 2000, additional pensions paid to widows or widowers will
be one half instead of the whole of the pension which would have been

3.8 The 1986 Act also introduced several changes in relation to contracting
out. As well as relaxing the requirements to be met by defined benefit schemes
who wish to contract out, it became possible for group money purchase
schemes and individual money purchase personal pensions to be used for
contracting out, provided that a minimum level of contributions is paid into the
scheme and provided that the pensions that are eventually put into payment
satisfy certain conditions. The Act also provided for payments equivalent to the
contracted-out rebate to be made to providers of personal pensions, plus an
additional 2 per cent cf relevant earnings for the years 1987-88 to 1992-93. The
2 per cent incentive was also available from 1988-89 to 1992-93 for newly
contracted-out occupational schemes.
3.9 The 1986 Act made a number of other changes to National Insurance
benefits. The flat-rate widow’s allowance payable during the first six months of
widowhood was replaced by a lump sum widow’s payment of £1,000 payable on
the death of a male contributor. The ages of eligibility for full rate and reduced
rate widow’s pension were increased from 50 to 55 and from 40 to 45
respectively. Death grant was abolished. Child’s special allowance was abol-
ished for new awards. Industrial death benefit, payable to a widow whose
husband has died as a result of an accident at work or because of an industrial
discase, was also abolished for new awards. The terms under which industrial
disablement benefit and other allowances paid to those injured at work or
suffering from an industrial disease are awarded were amended. Statutory
maternity pay was introduced, replacing maternity allowance for most eligible
employees. The contribution conditions for the residual maternity allowance
were amended. Reduced rates of unemployment and sickness benefits and
maternity allowance, payable to people who only partially satisfied the
contribution conditions, were abolished.
3.10. The 1988 Act amended the contribution conditions for unemployment
and sickness benefits.
3.11 The 1989 Act altered the contribution structure for employees, replacing
from October 1989 the structure introduced by the 1985 Act. The structure for
employers’ contributions was unchanged. The Act also abolished the Treasury
supplement which had been progressively reduced after 1980-81.
3.12 The 1989 Act also abolished the earnings rule for retirement pensions,
amended the conditions governing entitlement to unemployment benefit,
reduced earnings allowance and retirement allowance, and restored the
position of women widowed before 11 April 1988.
3.13 The current Social Security Bill 1990 proposes a number of changes
affecting the National Insurance Fund. Expenditure on industrial injuries
benefits will no longer be met by the National Insurance Fund but instead be a
direct charge on voted expenditure. Expenditure on statutory sick pay and
statutory maternity pay will be met by a transfer from the Consolidated Fund.
This will reduce the expenditure to be met by contributions and thus reduce the
contribution rates needed in future compared to what would otherwise be
required. In addition, the Bill proposes to phase out additional pension with
invalidity benefit whilst preserving amounts accrued to the current financial
year. The main results in this report do not allow for these latest changes but
the estimated effect on the contribution rate is set out in Section 11.

Section 4
Summary of contributions and benefits

Classes of contributions 4.1 The population of Great Britain over school-leaving age and under
pensionable age is divided, for the purpose of the Social Security Acts, into
three categories:

(1) employed persons, i.e. all persons gainfully occupied under a contract
of service or as an office-holder, and certain other persons;

(2) self-employed persons, i.e. others who are gainfully occupied;

(3) other persons, not employed or with low earnings.

4.2 Employed and self-employed persons whose earnings are above specified
minima are required to contribute to the National Insurance Fund. Those with
lower earnings and non-employed persons may do so if they wish. Employed
persons who have earnings above the lower limit pay earnings-related Class 1
primary contributions; their employers pay earnings-related Class 1 secondary
contributions. No Class 1 primary contributions are payable by those at work
after pensionable age (65 for men and 60 for women) but the employer is still
required to pay Class 1 secondary contributions.
4.3 Certain employed married women and widows, who exercised an option
before 11 May 1977, have a reserved right to pay a reduced rate of contri-
bution; their employers still pay full Class 1 secondary contributions in respect
of them. They qualify for industrial injury benefits, and may also be entitled to
receive statutory sick pay and statutory maternity pay but are not entitled to
other benefits except where title arises through their husbands’ insurance.
Self-employed persons who have not been granted exception on account of low
earnings pay flat-rate Class 2 contributions and, on their profits between
specified levels, earnings-related Class 4 contributions. Employees and the
self-employed do not pay contributions on earnings above a specified upper
limit. Since 1985 employers have had to pay contributions on their employees’
earnings above the upper limit as well as on earnings up to the limit.
Non-employed persons and others not required to contribute may pay flat-rate
Class 3 contributions on a voluntary basis.

Benefits 4.4 The principal categories of benefit payable from the National Insurance
Fund, before the effect of the 1990 Social Security Bill, are as follows:

(1) retirement pension;

(2) widows’ benefits;

(3) unemployment benefit (employed contributors only);

(4) sickness benefit, for those who do not qualify for statutory sick pay,
and invalidity benefit (employed and self-employed contributors
only);

(5) maternity allowance (for employed women who do not qualify for
statutory maternity pay and self-employed women only);

(6) lump sum payments to pensioners for Christmas bonus;

(7) industrial injury benefits (for employed persons only);

Basic retirement pension 4.5 The standard rate of Category A basic pension is payable to retired male
contributors from age 65 and to retired female contributors from age 60,
subject to the contribution conditions being satisfied. People who do not claim
their pension at those ages will be awarded higher pensions when they
subsequently claim, the increase being “th of 1 per cent for each week’s
deferment up to a maximum of five years.

4.6 For the pension to be paid at the full standard rate, contributions must
normally have been paid or credited for about nine-tenths of the working life
and no pension at all is payable where contributions and credits cover less than
one quarter of the working life. Since April 1978 any period in which a person
has home responsibilities looking after children or disabled persons has been
excluded from the definition of working life, making it easier for such a person
to satisfy the contribution conditions.
4.7. A married woman whose husband is receiving a Category A retirement
pension at the standard rate may receive a Category B pension of about 60 per
cent of the amount of her husband’s pension based on her husband’s contri-
bution record if she is over age 60 and retired. A dependent wife who is under
age 60 does not receive a pension, but her husband may receive an addition to
his pension for a dependant. A married woman may also be entitled to a
Category A retirement pension related to her own contributions. In this case
she will usually receive the higher of her Category A or Category B entitle-
ment. A widow or a widower who is over pension age will receive a retirement
pension at the full standard rate, subject to the contribution conditions being
satisfied. Divorced persons may take into account contributions paid by the
former spouse up to the time of divorce, instead of their own contributions in
the same period, if that would lead to a more favourable result.
Graduated pension 4.8 Units of graduated pension were earned by employees who paid grad-
uated contributions in the period between April 1961 and April 1975. Each
£7.50 paid by a man and each £9.00 paid by a woman gave entitlement to a
pension unit of 2.5 pence a week. Since November 1978 the value of the unit
has been uprated each year and is currently 6.14 pence a week. Increments to
graduated pensions are awarded where retirement is deferred and widows and
widowers (subject to certain conditions) can inherit one half of the graduated
pension to which the deceased spouse was entitled.
Earnings-related additional 4.9 Since April 1978 employees who have paid full Class 1 contributions have
pension been entitled to an earnings-related additional pension. The earnings on which
contributions are paid in any tax year are revalued in line with the general level
of earnings, up to the year before that in which pension age is attained. The
lower earnings limit in the latter year is then subtracted to obtain the revalued
relevant earnings in respect of each contribution year. The revalued relevant
earnings are then averaged over the period from age 16, or April 1978 if later,
to retirement age. Periods for which a contributor has been awarded credits in
respect of sickness or has been eligible for home responsibility protection are
excluded from the calculation of the average.

4.10 Under the Social Security Act 1986 additional pension will eventually be
20 per cent of average earnings as defined above and may then be regarded as
accruing uniformly over the working life between age 16 and pension age.
However, for those over age 16 when the scheme of additional pension took
effect in April 1978, the working life for this purpose is taken as the years
between April 1978 and pension age subject to a minimum of 20 years. Also
under the scheme as originally enacted in the Social Security Pensions Act
1975, the pension was 25 per cent of average earnings as defined in that Act.
The 1986 Act preserved the right to benefits accrued before 1988 and the new
benefit level is phased in only gradually under transitional provisions. The
result is that the accrual rate will be 25/N per cent in respect of earnings up to
April 1988, where N is the number of tax years in the earner’s working life from
April 1978 to pension age, with a minimum value of 20. The accrual rate in
respect of earnings from April 1988 depends on the year in which pensionable
age is attained. Those retiring before April 2000 will receive a pension based on
an accrual rate of 25/N per cent for all earnings. For those retiring from then up
to April 2009 there will be a phased reduction in the accrual rate from 25/N to
20/N per cent. For later retirements, the accrual rate for post-1988 earnings will
be the ultimate rate of 20/N per cent. A complete summary of the accrual rates
is given in Tabie D4 of Appendix D which sets out the detailed assumptions
underlying the projections of future expenditure.

4.11 Until April 2000 a widow or widower may inherit the whole of the
deceased spouse’s additional pension, subject to the combined pension not
exceeding the maximum additional pension to which an individual could be
entitled. From April 2000 a widow or widower will be entitled to receive half of
the additional pension which was payable to the deceased spouse, subject to the
same overalt limit.

Contracting out 4.12 Members of occupational pension schemes meeting required conditions
may be contracted out of a part of the additional pension. In that case both the
employees and their employers are allowed a reduction in the rate of National
Insurance contributions payable on the band of earnings between the lower and
upper earnings limits as described in paragraphs 4.25 and 4.26 below.
Contracted-out defined benefit schemes are required to provide guaranteed
minimum pensions (GMPs) at the retirement ages under the State Scheme and
to a spouse on the death of a member. Occupational schemes operating on a
money purchase basis can also be used for contracting out provided that the
contributions made to the scheme in respect of employees are not less than the
amount of the contracted-out contribution reduction and provided that the
pensions from the scheme satisfy certain requirements. Individuals can choose
to effect a personal pension arrangement which is approved for the purposes of
contracting out. In this case the individual and their employers will pay the full
National Insurance contributions applicable to those who are not contracted-
out and the equivalent of the contracted-out contribution reduction (together
with an allowance in lieu of tax relief on the employee’s share) will be paid by
the Department of Social Security (DSS) into the personal pension arrange-
ment chosen by the individual.

4.13 The GMPs which schemes have to provide accrued at the rate of 25/N per
cent on relevant earnings between April 1978 to March 1988, where N is as
defined in paragraph 4.10 above, except that there is no adjustment in respect
of credits and home responsibility protection. From April 1988 onwards GMPs
have accrued at the rate of 20/N per cent of relevant earnings. The earnings to
which these percentages are applied are broadly the same as the earnings
counted for additional pension. The additional pension payable from the
National Insurance Fund will be reduced by the amount of any GMPs to which
an individual may be entitled. Contracted-out defined benefit occupational
pension schemes are required to uprate GMPs accrued after April 1988, when
in payment, in line with movements in prices, up to a limit of 3 per cent a year.
No increases are required for GMPx which accrued before April 1988.

Section 6

The Scope of the Quinquennial Review

6.1 The object of this Review, as stated in Section 137(3) of the Social
Security Act 1985, is to determine the extent to which the level at which the
National Insurance Fund stands from year to year may be expected in the
longer term to bear a proper relation to demands in respect of payments of
benefit. For this purpose the Government Actuary is required to take into
account current rates of contribution, the yield to be expected from contri-
butions in the longer term and such other matters as he considers to be relevant
as affecting the present and future level of the Fund.

6.2 The National Insurance Scheme is operated on a pay-as-you-go basis, i.e.
the rates of contribution payable are set at a level intended to produce a flow of
income which in each year will broadly match current expenditure on benefits
and costs of administration. It has been the aim to maintain the National
Insurance Fund as a reasonable working balance sufficient to ensure the
smooth operation of the scheme. Thus, for example, at the end.of the 1988-89
financial year the balance in the Fund represented about 40 per cent of benefit
expenditure during the year. In these circumstances the Fund will remain
adequate to meet the demands upon it over future years provided contribution
rates are maintained at levels sufficient to generate the income to meet current
expenditure. The main object of the review is to establish the rates likely to be
required for this purpose in the future.

6.3. A factor which in isolation would lead to a steady increase in the rates of
contribution required for many years ahead is the gradual maturing of rights to
earnings-related additional retirement and: other pensions. Rights to such
additional pensions have been accruing only since April 1978. It will be 1998
before those retiring will have been able to secure an additional pension based
on a full period of accrual and over 20 years thereafter before the pensions in
payment for nearly all pensioners reflect this level of pension. Furthermore, the
reductions in the rate of accrual and the other modifications introduced by the
Social Security Act 1986 are being phased in gradually and will not be fully
refiected in the cost of pensions until the middle of the next century. The
estimates in this report must therefore cover a long period into the future in
order to show the effect of the developing cost of additional pensions.

6.4 Apart from the increase in the cost of additional pensions, the main
factors liable to produce a significant change in the contribution rates required
to keep income and expenditure in balance are:

(i) factors resulting in a change in the relative numbers of contributors
and pensioners in the future. These factors are mainly demographic
but include also social and economic factors such as changes in the
proportion of women in employment or in the rate of unemployment;

(ii) any divergence between the future rates of increase in earnings and
the rates of increase in the flat-rate benefits and in the earnings limits
used in the assessment of earnings-related contributions and benefits.

6.5 The relationship between earnings levels and benefit rates is fundamental
in determining the contribution rates required under the scheme. In particular,
with the Fund’s income largely linked to the movement of earnings, rates of
increase in the flat-rate benefits below the rates of growth of earnings would,

Balance in the Fund 6.8 Although, as explained above, the adequacy of the Fund to meet the
demands in respect of payment of benefits will basically depend on rates of
contribution being set from year to year at levels to produce the income
required to meet the expenditure under the Scheme, it is necessary also to
ensure that the balance in the Fund remains sufficient to ensure the smooth day
to day operation of the Scheme. As the National Insurance Fund has no
borrowing powers, the balance must be sufficient to cover the consequences of
an unexpected excess of expenditure or short-fall in income in the short term,
given. the inevitable time lag before such developments may become apparent
and appropriate measures approved and implemented to restore the position.
In addition, a working balance has to be maintained principally in order to be
able to advance money to the Post Office and other agencies for the purpose of
paying pensions and other benefits.

6.9 From consideration of the possible extent of errors in the estimates of
income and expenditure on the basis of which contribution rates are decided,
and of the need for a degree of liquidity to provide cash balances for the paying
of benefits, it would seem advisable to maintain a minimum balance of about
one-sixth of a year’s benefit expenditure.

6.10 The minimum balance of the Fund on this criterion will increase with the
growth in benefit expenditure as a result of the annual reviews of benefit rates
and the other factors referred to in this report. It is thus necessary to budget for
a small-excess of income over expenditure for this purpose. However, the Fund
is invested and generates investment income which over recent years has been
at a level which, other things being equal, would have broadly maintained the
teal value of the Fund relative to expenditure. Although there are fluctuations
from year to year in the rate of return on the Fund’s investments relative to the
rate of growth in expenditure, it is reasonable to assume that, when considered
over a period of time, the investment income will provide broadly the amounts
required to ensure the growth of the Fund balance in step with expenditure.
Investment income has therefore been left out of account in estimating the
rates of contribution likely to be required in future years, which have been
calculated as the rates required to generate income equal to the expected
expenditure on benefits and administration.

Section 7
The Effect of Future Increases in Earnings and Prices
7.1 With the income of the National Insurance Fund arising very largely from
earnings-related contributions, a major factor in determining the rates of
contribution required in future years will be the relationship between the level
of benefits and the level of earnings. Section 63 of the Social Security Act 1986
requires that the Secretary of State shall in each tax year review the change in
the general level of prices over the previous year. If there has been an increase
in prices, the Act requires benefit rates to be increased by at least that
percentage. A requirement for an annual review of benefit rates and increases
at least in line with the movement of prices has formed part of the legislation
since the Social Security Act 1973, except that between May 1974 and May 1980
the requirement was that for the long-term pension benefits the increase should
be in line with the growth in earnings if this exceeded the growth in prices. Prior
to 1973 increases in benefits were on. an ad hoc basis on the initiative of the
government of the day, and in practice over the years benefits were increased
broadly in line with average earnings. Since 1980 the increases have been in line
with the movement of prices.

the rate of growth of earnings and benefit rates can be very significant for the

Section 10

Sensitivity Analysis

10.1 Any estimates of future benefit costs and contribution rates, especially
those made far into the future, are inevitably subject to wide margins of error.
The way in which the various factors affecting the results will develop is
uncertain even for the short term and becomes increasingly speculative in the
more distant future. It is important therefore that too much emphasis should
not be placed on a single set of estimates but regard should be had to the range
of possibilities on different assumptions. The estimates in Section 9 are given
on different assumptions for the basis of uprating of flat-rate benefits and the
earnings limits and show the extent to which this affects the contribution rates
required in the future. This section provides estimates of the effect of changing
certain of the other assumptions which are the major source of uncertainty in
terms of their impact on future contribution rates.

Demographic factors 10.2 The estimates in Section 9 are based on the principal mid-1987 based
population projection, but variant projections based on higher and lower
assumptions for future mortality and fertility rates were published along with
the principal projection. These have been used to estimate the changes to the
estimated contribution rates as a result of different assumptions in regard to
these factors. The sensitivity analysis has been restricted to those variant
assumptions for mortality and fertility which lead to increases in future
contribution rates, ie those with lower fertility and mortality rates. Correspond-
ing results showing lower estimated contribution rates would be obtained on
the basis of the alternative demographic assumptions with higher fertility and
mortality rates.

10.3. The lower mortality variant assumes that mortality improvement will be
half as much again as in the principal projection. As a result, the numbers in the
population are higher at all ages than in the principal projection. However, the
effect is greater at older than at younger ages and, in particular, there is a
significant increase in the number of pensioners relative to the number of
contributors, leading to increases in the required contribution rates.
10.4 The lower fertility variant assumes that the average family size will
ultimately reach 1.8 for women born in 1979 and later rather than 2.0 as in the
principal projection. This change in the annual number of births has a delayed
effect on the estimates. A change in the assumption for the number of births
hardly affects the contribution rate until that generation has passed school
leaving age and joined the working population. As time passes, more and more
of the working population is made up of the generations that are yet to be born.
On the lower fertility assumption, these generations are smaller, so that there
will be fewer contributors and the contribution rate will thus have to be higher;
for many years there will be little effect on the cost of benefits as retirement
pensions will be unaffected and most of the other benefit costs are concen-
trated at the older ages. As these smaller generations reach pension age, the
smaller number of pensioners would reduce pension costs although the main
effect of this will be beyond the period covered by the projections. Moreover, if
the lower fertility persisted, the fall in the numbers of pensioners would be
accompanied by a continuing fall in the number of contributors.

D.1 This appendix describes the methods and assumptions used in deriving
the estimates of income and expenditure given in the report. The assumptions
used in deriving the projected numbers of future contributors and pensioners

Contribution income D.2 In obtaining estimates of future contribution income regard must be had
not only to the assumed increases in average earnings but also to the
distributions of earnings in order that allowance can be made for the effect of

for example with upratings in line with prices. Information on earnings from
the Department of Employment’s New Earnings Survey (1988) was used to
construct separate distributions for men and women and for full-time and
part-time employees. The estimated average weekly earnings in 1990-91 terms
for each of these categories is shown in Table D1. The averages shown apply to
all employees including those with earnings below the lower earnings limit who
are not required to pay contributions. The assumed 1.5 per cent a year growth
in real earnings was taken to apply to each category and throughout the whole
of each earnings distribution.

D.58 Expenditure on SMP in 1990-91 is estimated to be £296m, some 1 per

Industrial disablement D.61 Industrial disablement benefit is payable for disablement due to an
benefit industrial injury or disease and normally commences after 15 weeks of
disablement. The rate of pension benefit depends on a medical assessment of
the degree of disablement (see paragraph A.12). Additional payments are
made where the disabied beneficiary requires constant attendance, is exceptio-
nally severely disabled or there has been a significant fall in earnings as a result
of the disability. Of these, the latter, the reduced earnings allowance (REA), is
of most importance financially, representing about two-fifths of the total
annual expenditure on disablement benefits.

proportions entitled continue in future.

Administration costs D.72 Itis estimated that the cost of administration in 1990-91 will be about £1
billion, slightly over three per cent of total expenditure. In future, whilst the
numbers of contributors and those in receipt of benefits other than retirement
pensions are relatively stable, the increase in the number of pensioners will
tend to increase costs. On the other hand, this is likely to be offset by
improvements in the efficiency of administration. As administration costs are a
relatively small proportion of total outgo, it has been assumed as a practical
expedient that they will increase in line with earnings.
