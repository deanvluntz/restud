# 5. Empirical Strategy

## 5.1 Specification

The estimating equation is the empirical counterpart of the policy rule derived in Section 3: an autoregressive distributed lag model, ARDL(1), in log-level form,

[[EQ]]
ln mₜ = α + ρ ln mₜ₋₁ + β₁ Δ̂dₜ(10) + β₂ τₜ₋₁ + β₃ Δln Bₜ₋₁ − β₄ uₜ₋₁ + γ 𝟙[t < 1975] + εₜ,
[[/EQ]]

where mₜ is controlled inflows per thousand (lagged) working-age residents, Δ̂dₜ(10) is the projected ten-year change in the OADR from the current NPP vintage, τₜ₋₁ is the lagged tax take per working-age person, Δln Bₜ₋₁ the lagged annual log change in the debt-to-GDP ratio, uₜ₋₁ the lagged unemployment rate, and 𝟙[t < 1975] the indicator for the years in which the dependent variable is measured on the non-British rather than the non-EU basis (Section 4.1); its coefficient γ absorbs the level difference between the two definitions and is reported, not suppressed. The log-level form implies that policy responds proportionally to fiscal state variables, so that a given projected deterioration calls forth a given percentage adjustment of the inflow, and, as reported in Section 6.1, it is also the form favoured by specification tests. The lagged dependent variable captures the adjustment costs of the model and the persistence of flows documented throughout the UK literature (Mitchell and Pain, 2003; Hatton, 2005). A second specification replaces τₜ₋₁ with a deterministic trend; the two cannot be separated statistically (their correlation over the sample is 0.98), so both are reported in Table 2, and the fiscal specification is the one carried through the remainder of the paper.

The choice of estimator deserves brief defence against the alternatives. An instrumental-variables design would be preferable in principle, but Section 4.4 documented that no valid instrument for UK migration exists in the literature. Difference-in-differences and regression-discontinuity designs are unavailable for want of a control group or an assignment threshold: the policy regime applied to the whole non-EU margin at once, and its changes were continuous rather than discrete. A cross-country panel would trade the UK's institutional depth for breadth the hypothesis does not yet need; it is the natural second study (Section 8), not the first. Within the single-country time-series setting, the ARDL framework is the standard recommended for UK migration data (Mitchell and Pain, 2003): it accommodates the persistence of flows, nests the static model, supports bounds testing for level relationships among I(0)/I(1) variables, and delivers long-run multipliers with interpretable structural content under the model of Section 3.

Inference throughout uses heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors, with Newey–West errors reported as a check; conventional OLS standard errors are shown for comparison in Online Appendix Table B2.

## 5.2 Identification: timing, threats, and partial remedies

The design claims that the regressors are predetermined with respect to the current year's inflow shock: the fiscal controls are lagged realisations, and the projection variable is a forecast fixed before the flows it is asked to predict. Within-year reverse causality from inflows to the regressors is thereby excluded by construction. Three threats survive this timing argument and are taken in increasing order of seriousness.

The first is the lagged dependent variable. Ordinary least squares is consistent here but biased in finite samples: the lagged regressor is not strictly exogenous, and the resulting attenuation of the autoregressive coefficient is of order −(1 + 3ρ)/T (Hurwicz, 1950; Kendall, 1954). At T = 55 and ρ ≈ 0.46 this is roughly −0.04, so the persistence estimates below are lower bounds, and with them the long-run multipliers, which divide by (1 − ρ). The direction is therefore conservative for the paper's magnitudes. A recursive-design residual bootstrap of the fiscal specification (2,000 replications, exogenous regressors held at their observed values) estimates the bias at −0.10, raising the persistence estimate from 0.46 to approximately 0.56 and the implied long-run response to the projection from 1.5 to approximately 1.8 per cent per point, with a recentred bootstrap percentile interval of [1.2, 2.6] per cent. The estimated bias exceeds the analytic value of −0.043 because the latter is derived for a pure autoregression, while the estimating equation adds four persistent and mutually correlated regressors. A Monte Carlo calibration confirms the procedure: on simulated data with known persistence at the same sample size, mean OLS bias reproduces the analytic prediction and the bootstrap correction is nearly unbiased. The bias operates on the autoregressive term and cannot manufacture significance of the projection term. It is also distinct from consistency: serially correlated errors would correlate the lagged term with the *contemporaneous* disturbance, which is why the Breusch–Godfrey tests reported throughout are a precondition for treating it as predetermined.

The second is publication timing. NPP vintages are labelled by base year but released roughly a year later; assigning them by base year assumes the policymakers saw the projections before the public did. For the GAD era (1954–2006), when the projections were produced inside government on Treasury-agreed assumptions, the assumption is institutionally grounded; it is weaker for the ONS era. The conservative alternative dates each vintage from the year after its base, so that the projection in force is the latest vintage published by the time the year begins. Publication dates are held for the vintages from the 1975-based, and dating them conservatively reassigns the vintage in force in 22 of the 43 years from 1977; Section 7 re-estimates on that basis.

The third and most fundamental threat is that the projections are not mechanically exogenous: every NPP vintage embeds an assumption about future net migration, so the regressor contains a function of the expected path of the dependent variable. Three points bound the resulting concern. First, the direction of the mechanical contamination is attenuating for the hypothesis: higher assumed migration lowers the projected OADR (migrants enter the working-age denominator), so a vintage that anticipates the current expansion of inflows produces a smaller Δ̂dₜ(10), biasing β₁ toward zero, not away from it. Second, the demographic arithmetic limits the contamination in the numerator: the old-age population ten years ahead consists almost entirely of persons aged 55 and over who are already resident, so the numerator of the projected ratio is essentially invariant to migration assumptions at the ten-year horizon. Third, the clean remedy is implemented for every vintage that publishes its components of change, which is every vintage from the 1975-based: from each vintage's net-migration assumptions, Section 7 rebuilds the projected OADR under zero net migration from the observation year forward and re-estimates both specifications with the purged regressor on 1977–2019. The results are essentially unchanged, so the finding does not depend on the migration content of the projections.

The design's identification status, stated plainly: the estimates are not experimental, and the paper does not claim they are. The claim is narrower and, within its limits, strong: a predetermined official expectation of ageing predicts subsequent policy-controlled inflows conditional on the fiscal state; the mechanical channel from migration into the expectation is removed outright by the zero-migration reconstruction; and the confounding structures most naturally levelled at the result are tested and rejected by the placebo margins of Section 7. The agenda of Section 8 specifies the designs that could carry the claim the rest of the way.

Two further design choices bear on identification. Classical measurement error in the IPS dependent variable inflates standard errors without biasing coefficients, and the LTIM check (Section 7) addresses the non-classical components the ONS has documented; and the working-age denominator is lagged one year so that current inflows cannot enter both sides of the equation through the population count.

A fourth, generic threat is an omitted common driver: some force that both worsened projected ageing and attracted non-EU migrants through non-policy channels. This is the threat the falsification tests are built to address: a confounder operating through labour demand or general attractiveness should load onto EU inflows, which were uncontrolled and highly elastic, and onto the total dependency ratio insofar as the driver is demographic rather than fiscal. Section 7 shows it does neither.

## 5.3 Diagnostics and specification protocol

Every reported specification is accompanied by a fixed battery of diagnostics, each of which earns its place in this setting. Ramsey's RESET test screens for neglected nonlinearity and, indirectly, omitted-variable bias expressible as functions of the fitted values; it rejected the level-form specification and forced the move to logs (Section 6.1). The Breusch–Godfrey test, at first and second order, and preferred to Durbin–Watson, which is invalid with a lagged dependent variable, is a condition for consistency, not a formality: serially correlated errors would correlate ln mₜ₋₁ with the disturbance, so passing it is what licenses treating the lagged term as predetermined. Breusch–Pagan tests for heteroskedasticity, with HAC inference as a second line of defence. The Jarque–Bera test on residuals matters more than usual because, at 55 observations, exact t- and F-inference leans on approximate normality rather than asymptotics. Maximum variance inflation factors monitor the multicollinearity that a short trending sample invites.

Because the variables are trending, the time-series properties receive separate treatment. Orders of integration are established by sequential testing (ADF with trend on levels and drift on differences, lags selected by information criterion, with KPSS as the confirmatory mirror), and a series that fails both stages is flagged rather than forced into a class (Online Appendix Table B1); every regressor is I(0) or I(1), the condition for validity of bounds testing, with one non-regressor series left unresolved. The Pesaran–Shin–Smith bounds test provides the formal test of a level relationship, distinguishing a genuine long-run relation from the spurious co-movement of drifting series, with the associated error-correction form yielding long-run multipliers. The power of all unit-root procedures at this sample size is limited, and the reading of their results in Section 6.3 is correspondingly restrained.

Because the theory pins down the conditioning set only up to broad categories, some specification search was unavoidable. The protocol was fixed in advance: candidates were limited to the variables named by the institutional literature (Section 4.3), retained or excluded on joint statistical and theoretical grounds, and every candidate tested is reported (Section 6.1), including the failures. The headline coefficient's robustness across every specification tried, not the significance of any single regression, is the basis of the paper's claim.

# 6. Results

## 6.1 Specification development

A brief account of the path to the final specification guards against the impression of a curated result; full output for every step is available on request.

The starting point for the projection horizon was a five-year window, matching the electoral cycle, the Treasury's biennial Spending Reviews, departmental review cycles of three to five years, and the five-year forecast horizon of Budget-time fiscal projections. It was at first implemented as a weighted average of projected OADR changes with weights declining uniformly in distance. The initial level-form specification regressed the inflow rate on this measure, the lagged tax take per working-age person, and lagged unemployment, inactivity, and productivity growth. It produced a strongly significant projection coefficient but failed the Breusch–Godfrey test (p = 0.018), motivating the autoregressive term that the adjustment-cost logic of Section 3 independently implies. An interaction between the projection and the tax burden, prediction P2 taken literally as a modifier rather than a level shifter, generated extreme collinearity (maximum VIF above 100) and was abandoned in favour of the additive form.

The candidate controls resolved as follows. No measure of growth (aggregate real GDP, GDP per capita, output per worker) attained significance in any variant, unsurprising for output per worker given the UK's prolonged productivity stagnation, and consistent with a policy rule oriented to the extensive rather than the intensive margin. Neither gilt yield mattered, suggesting the debt *stock* rather than its price is the operative constraint. The Budget-time growth forecast was significant at the five per cent level in some variants, but only at the cost of destabilising the remaining coefficients, and was excluded. The inactivity rate hovered at the margin of significance, was nearly time-invariant over the sample, and was dropped when degrees of freedom required it. The level-form ARDL passed all diagnostics except RESET, which favoured the log-level form; in logs, the projection horizon that fits best lengthens from five to ten years, consistent with the primacy of the Treasury's planning horizon over the electoral cycle, and an empirical answer to the horizon question posed above. That the statutory minimum horizon for the government's own published long-term projections is likewise ten years (Section 2.4) is a useful coincidence: the specification the data select is the one the fiscal framework mandates. The lagged log change in the debt ratio enters strongly. The trend variant arises because the trend and the tax burden are nearly collinear (r = 0.98) and cannot be jointly included: the tax burden has the theoretical claim (P2) and the trend the cleaner exogeneity. Entered together, with the collinearity caveat that implies (maximum VIF of 63), the tax term holds (0.064, p < 0.001) while the trend turns negative and insignificant (−0.003, p = 0.50) and the projection coefficient is essentially unchanged at 0.0084, so the trend is best read as the tax burden's atheoretical stand-in. Table 2 reports both; every subsequent result is stated in the fiscal specification, and each has been re-estimated with the trend in its place without changing any coefficient's sign or significance class (Online Appendix Table B3).

The controls carry the signs the model predicts. The lagged tax burden (P2): each additional thousand pounds of annual tax per working-age person is associated with 5.3 per cent higher inflows. This is the substitution effect at the heart of the salience mechanism: governments inheriting a heavier tax burden face a higher marginal political price on the loud instrument and lean harder on the quiet one. Its positive sign is worth pausing on, because a naive fiscal-need story might predict the opposite (high taxes indicating adjustment already achieved). The debt trajectory (P3): the coefficient of 0.53 on the lagged log change in debt/GDP implies that a one-percentage-point faster rise in the debt ratio is followed by roughly 0.5 per cent higher inflows, so the years of sharpest fiscal deterioration are those followed by the largest expansions of the quiet margin. Lagged unemployment (P4): a percentage point of unemployment reduces inflows by about 2 per cent, consistent with both the political-cost channel and conventional labour-demand effects on migration; the two cannot be separated here, and no interpretation rests on the distinction. Persistence (ρ ≈ 0.46 in column 1 and 0.66 in column 2, both understated by the finite-sample attenuation of Section 5.2) matches the flow persistence documented across the UK literature.

The fit is tight (adjusted R² of 0.95), though with a lagged dependent variable and trending data the R² overstates explanatory success; the economically meaningful statement is that the projection term remains individually significant at the one per cent level under HAC and Newey–West inference in the presence of the autoregressive term, the trend (or its fiscal twin), and the business-cycle control. RESET, Breusch–Pagan and Jarque–Bera fail to reject in both columns, and Breusch–Godfrey at first order does too; at second order it rejects in the fiscal specification (p = 0.03) and at third and fourth order likewise (p = 0.015 and 0.013), while the trend specification passes at every order. The dependence is a property of the error rather than a missing dynamic (a second lag of the dependent variable does not remove it; Section 7), which is why inference throughout is HAC and the long-run interval is also computed by moving-block bootstrap (Online Appendix B). The fiscal specification's maximum VIF (12.4) exceeds the conventional threshold of 10, but it belongs to the lagged tax burden (12.4) and the lagged dependent variable (10.9), two trending levels that share the growth of the period; the projection term's VIF is 1.8. Rules of thumb for the VIF are in any case poor guides to whether collinearity matters, which depends on whether the estimates are precise enough for the question at hand (O'Brien, 2007). Finally, the trend specification, with a maximum VIF of 7.4, returns a projection coefficient of 0.0066, so nothing in the headline result depends on the collinearity of the fiscal specification.

The magnitudes sit comfortably within the UK literature where comparison is possible. The unemployment semi-elasticity of around 2 per cent per point is of the order found in studies with and without free-movement flows in the sample, and its presence in *lagged* form accords with Hatton's (2005) finding that migration responds to labour-market changes rather than levels. The persistence parameter brackets the range in Mitchell and Pain (2003). The projection term has no published counterpart, which is the paper's point, but its long-run multiplier can be benchmarked internally: between 1997 and 2002 the projected ten-year OADR change rose by some nineteen points, which through the long-run multiplier of Table 3 implies an eventual expansion of the non-EU inflow rate on the order of three-tenths of a log point, a substantial share of the observed rise over those years, and a measured alternative to the unexplained structural dummies of the Bayesian averaging literature.

## 6.3 Cointegration and the long run

The regressors are a mixture of I(1) and I(0) series (Online Appendix Table B1). The mixture is itself informative, since it rules out the possibility that the level relationship is an artefact of common integration order, and it dictates the testing framework: residual-based cointegration methods presume all-I(1) systems, whereas the bounds procedure of Pesaran, Shin and Smith (2001), implemented with the R package ARDL (Natsiopoulos, Tzeremes and Finnan, 2026), is valid for any combination of I(0) and I(1) regressors, which is why it, together with ADF tests on the residuals, carries the inferential weight here. The level relationship requires justification beyond significance. ADF tests reject a unit root in the residuals of both specifications on both samples (core: p = 0.022 and 0.046; extended: p = 0.01 and 0.02). The bounds F-test on the error-correction form rejects the null of no level relationship at the one per cent level on the core sample (F = 6.16, p < 0.01) and the t-version at five per cent (t = −4.09, p < 0.05); on the extended sample, with the basis indicator as a fixed regressor, both reject at one per cent (F = 7.18; t = −4.95), beyond the I(1) bound. The statistics are exact; the p-values come from a stochastic response surface and are reported to two decimals accordingly. This is supportive evidence of cointegration, but with samples of this length the tests have limited power, and the honest statement is that the data are consistent with a long-run equilibrium relationship rather than proof of one.

That adjustment speed is consistent with the administrative propagation lags of entry policy discussed in Section 3, where rule changes work through application pipelines and sponsor behaviour over quarters rather than weeks, and it implies that annual data, while coarse, do not miss the bulk of the dynamics.

## 6.4 Reading the history through the model

The estimated rule acquires content when laid against the sample's political history, because the projection variable moves at times that no party-political account selects. Figure 1 plots the two series.

In the 1960s the projected ten-year change was large and positive, above thirty points in 1963–65 as the cohorts born before the First World War approached pension age, and it fell through the decade to zero by 1973 and below it by 1974, as the small cohorts of the war years entered the window; controlled inflows fell from 140,000 to 105,000 over the same years, under governments of both parties that restricted Commonwealth entry in 1962, 1965 and 1968. The projected change stayed negative through the late 1970s and first turned positive again in the early 1980s, as the leading edge of the post-war birth surge entered the projections' ten-year window; non-EU inflows rose in 1981–85, under a Conservative government committed publicly to restriction, a decade and a half before New Labour. The delta declined through the late 1980s and turned negative around 1990, after which inflows stagnated; it returned to positive territory in 1996, and inflows resumed growth in the final year of the Major administration, before the change of government. In 1997 the delta reached a then-record 6.1 points, and the vintages of the early 2000s repriced the future sharply: in 2002–03 the delta averaged roughly 25 points as the first baby-boom wave arrived a decade from pension age. Through the estimated short-run coefficient, projections of that size call for expansions of inflows on the order of 20 per cent per year from the projection channel alone; gross non-EU inflows cleared 300,000 for the first time in 2004, more than triple their level a decade earlier and more than double the 1964 figure that the restrictions of the 1960s had been designed to reduce, the lag between signal and response being what the adjustment costs of Section 3 imply.

Read through the model, then, the celebrated post-1997 policy turn was not the arrival of a new ideology but the arrival of the baby boomer at the projection horizon (women of those cohorts reaching the then-female state pension age of 60): a government of either party, facing the same vintages, is predicted to have expanded the quiet margin, and the pre-1997 record shows governments of the other party doing exactly that whenever the projections instructed it. Accelerations timed to the projections rather than to changes of administration are the pattern a party-specific structural-change account leaves unexplained and the fiscal account predicts; Section 7 runs the comparison formally. The counterfactual is worth stating plainly: had the 1997 election gone the other way, the model expects the subsequent expansion to have occurred in any case, in different rhetoric.

# 7. Robustness and Falsification

Table 4 collects the main exercises on the extended sample, 1965–2019, all in the fiscal specification; the trend-specification counterparts are in Online Appendix Table B3 and change no conclusion, and the full panels behind this section, on both samples, are Online Appendix Tables B6–B35. Every check is repeated on the core sample, 1977–2019, the cleaner object (an unspliced dependent variable, annual and complete vintages), and the core result is reported beside the extended one, with any material difference named. Figure 4 at the end of the section summarises every variant on the extended sample. Exercises that need data the pre-1975 vintages or surveys do not supply (zero-net-migration projections, publication dating, the EU margin, the LTIM basis) are run on the core sample alone and say so. The placebo tests carry the greatest evidential weight because they are tests the fiscal hypothesis could have failed.

*The two samples.* The core sample is the earliest start the non-EU series allows. Extending the dependent variable back to 1965 on the non-British basis (Section 4.1) adds fourteen years under a different entry regime, a different measurement basis, a different unemployment source, and projections built from five-year age groups with interpolated tenth years, and the projection coefficient moves from 0.0082 to 0.0081. The years added are consistent with the estimate rather than an independent test of it: over 1965–78 the projected change declines almost linearly (its correlation with time is −0.97), so on those years alone a trend would absorb it. On the full extended sample a linear trend leaves the projection at 0.0084, and step indicators for the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1968 and the entry into force of the Immigration Act 1971 leave it at 0.0082. Given the intercept and the basis step, those two indicators are identified only inside the pre-1975 window (the 1968 step contrasts 1965–67 with 1968–74, and the 1973 step is numerically an indicator for 1973–74), so the column lets the pre-1975 level float in three sub-periods and the coefficient does not move; the positive 1973–74 step is not a tightening effect but the Ugandan Asian admissions, the rush of dependants before the 1971 Act bit, and EEC nationals, who entered freely from accession on the same day and are counted in the non-British series until the 1975 change of basis. The identifying variation lies from 1979 onwards, where the projection is not trending (correlation with time −0.09). Defining the controlled margin as non-British citizens throughout gives 0.0054, diluted after 2004 by EU inflows the government could not ration; ending that series in 2003, before the A8 accession, restores 0.0081.

*Zero-net-migration projections.* This check addresses the mechanical-endogeneity threat of Section 5.2 directly. From each vintage's published components of change, the projected OADR at every horizon is recomputed under zero net migration from the observation year forward, by removing the vintage's cumulative assumed net migration from the projected working-age denominator (Online Appendix A); the regressor then cannot contain the vintages' migration assumptions. The response of controlled migration to projected ageing is not an artefact of the projections' own migration assumptions.

*Publication dating.* Dating each vintage from the year after its base rather than by base year (Section 5.2) reassigns the vintage in force in 22 of the 43 years from 1977, moving one observation by more than fifteen points. The estimate barely moves: 0.0090 (HAC s.e. 0.0015) against 0.0082 under base dating on those years, significant at 0.1 per cent and with diagnostics still passing; the zero-migration variant returns 0.0076 (0.0016). Both falsifications survive: the total dependency ratio falls to 0.0002 (p = 0.91) and the EU placebo stays insignificant (p = 0.28). The timing assumption does not carry the result.

*News versus level.* A slow common trend could in principle produce a relationship between two smoothly rising series. The vintages allow a sharper test, run here on the 1977–2019 years whose vintages are annual and complete. In the twenty-two of those years in which a new vintage came into force, the projection the Treasury saw can be split into the part the previous vintage had already reported for the same years and the revision the new vintage introduced, which is news on publication (standard deviation 5.3 points across those years, range −15.6 to +9.8). Entered separately, the inherited part carries 0.0092 (p < 0.001) and the revision 0.0029 (p = 0.53); the two are not statistically distinguishable (F(1, 36) = 1.64, p = 0.21), and the trend specification gives 0.0078 and 0.0040 (p = 0.41; equality p = 0.44). The response to news has the right sign and about a third of the size of the response to the inherited level, but twenty-two one-off revisions in annual data cannot estimate it precisely: the test is consistent with the projections working through what they newly report, and cannot establish it. Establishing it is what the event-study design of Section 8 is for.

*The projection's marginal contribution.* Because the specification carries a lagged dependent variable, adjusted R² reaches 0.94 before the projection enters, and the increment it adds to R² is correspondingly small; the informative comparison is the nested one. A HAC Wald test of dropping the projection term gives F(1, 48) = 66.4 (p < 0.001), and the information criterion improves by 23.6 points when it is restored — a difference conventionally taken to leave no support for the restricted model. The functional-form evidence agrees: without the projection, RESET falls to p = 0.055, against p = 0.37 with it. On the core sample the comparison is the same in kind: F(1, 37) = 32.2, an improvement of 14.9 points, RESET at p = 0.12 without the projection against 0.41 with it, and in the trend specification an outright rejection (p = 0.048 against 0.078). The omission does not merely cost fit; it leaves detectable misspecification.

*Old-age versus total dependency.* If the mechanism is fiscal, the projection that matters is the old-age ratio, since pensioners are expensive, not the total ratio, whose child component carries a far smaller fiscal price (Section 2.4). The projected ten-year change in the total dependency ratio is rebuilt from the same vintages, with the same interpolation before 1975. On the extended sample the total ratio is not null on its own (0.0015, p = 0.001): in the 1960s the projected changes in the child and pensioner populations moved together, so the total ratio is then a noisy proxy for the old-age ratio, and it swings between +106 and −64 points across the decade. The horse race settles it: entered together, the old-age ratio holds at 0.0076 (p < 0.001) and the total ratio falls to 0.0004 (p = 0.48); the total ratio's own equation is also the only one in the battery to fail the Breusch–Godfrey test. On the core sample, where the vintages are annual and the child swings of the 1960s are absent, substituting the total ratio for the OADR change eliminates the result outright: the coefficient is 0.0020 (p = 0.16), the same substitution at every horizon and in levels as in deltas likewise yields nothing, against the no-projection baseline the old-age ratio improves the information criterion by 14.9 points where the total ratio improves it by 0.1, and entered together the old-age ratio holds at 0.0080 (p < 0.001) while the total ratio at 0.0015 is insignificant (p = 0.18). A generic demographic confounder, or a data-mined artefact of trending series, has no reason to select the pensioner-specific ratio so cleanly; a fiscal mechanism must.

*The uncontrolled margin.* Might some unobserved driver, such as rising UK attractiveness, labour demand, or network effects, correlate with projected ageing and move all migration, with policy irrelevant? One version is even behavioural: migrants themselves targeting ageing destinations where labour-market competition is projected to thin. EU inflows provide the test, responding freely to every such driver but not to UK entry policy. EU inflows are identified in the survey from 1975. Re-estimating the full specification on them, constructed identically, over 1977–2019 returns an OADR-projection coefficient of 0.0030 (p = 0.41); only the persistence term survives. Figure 2 shows the contrast directly, as added-variable plots in which the fitted slope is the regression coefficient: a clear conditional relationship on the controlled margin, none on the uncontrolled one. Whatever moved non-EU inflows in step with projected ageing did not move the flows the government could not ration. The confound would have to operate exclusively on the controlled margin, which is to say it would have to *be* policy.

*The policy-era alternative.* Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011) attribute the post-1997 expansion to a liberal policy era, captured by a step indicator from 1998 — which their 2007 sample end makes indistinguishable from a 1998–2007 window. Adding their full indicator set (POL97, POL02 for the 2002 skilled-worker route, and enlargement indicators for 1986, 1995 and 2004) to the fiscal specification leaves the indicators jointly irrelevant under HAC inference (F(5, 43) = 1.09, p = 0.38), while the projection holds at 0.0074 (p < 0.001); the 1998 step alone gives 0.0070 and the 1998–2007 window 0.0072. The reason is that the indicator nearly discretises the projection over the years the literature studies: the ten-year delta averages 14.9 points within the window and −4.5 after 2007, against 0.6 over 1977–97, so the literature's dummies were standing in for the projections. Two checks on the 1977–2019 sample confirm the reading. Interacting the step with the projection returns a negative interaction (−0.0072, p = 0.34), the opposite of what a post-1997 account requires; and in Mitchell, Pain and Riley's own differenced form none of the indicators comes near significance (the smallest p-value is 0.40), while the projection keeps its sign, if not its precision (0.0050, p = 0.18 for non-EU inflows; 0.0057, p = 0.098 for total inflows). Where the indicators do earn their place is on total inflows including the EU margin the government could not ration (F(5, 32) = 5.50, p < 0.001), with the projection falling to 0.0028 (p = 0.31) — the EU placebo arriving from the other direction, reported with the caveat that this specification reaches a maximum VIF of 56 (Online Appendix B).

*Recognition of the mechanism elsewhere.* The salience model makes a prediction about who advertises the instrument. Westminster held the entry lever throughout the sample and used it without ever campaigning on it; Scotland's devolved government, which does not hold it, campaigns for it explicitly and publishes this paper's regressor as a policy target, calculating that maintaining the Scottish dependency ratio requires the working-age population to grow by some 32,000 a year and proposing a devolved visa to achieve it (Scottish Government, 2018, 2020). Advocacy without the instrument carries no price for the inflows themselves, so visibility of the demand runs inverse to possession of the lever — which is what pricing policy by political exposure predicts, on a case the model was not built from. The demographic arithmetic, meanwhile, is evidently understood well beyond the Treasury.

*The scaling choice.* Expressing inflows per thousand working-age residents is the analyst's decision, not the policymaker's: entry policy rations admissions, and nothing obliges a Chancellor to think in per-capita terms. The result does not depend on it. On gross controlled inflows, with no population estimate on either side of the equation, the projection coefficient is 0.0085 (HAC s.e. 0.0010) on the extended sample and 0.0082 on the core, significant beyond the 0.1 per cent level in both. In levels rather than logs the pattern holds and acquires a directly interpretable magnitude: each further point of projected ten-year OADR deterioration is associated with roughly 1,300 additional gross arrivals per year (1,335, HAC s.e. 255). Nor is the tax term a ratio artefact of the shared working-age denominator (Kronmal, 1993): it is unchanged on gross inflows and unchanged when the log of the working-age population enters as its own regressor (projection 0.0085, the population term p = 0.37 on the extended sample; 0.0083 and p = 0.13 on the core).

*Tax burden measures.* The choice of tax measure can be tested rather than argued. Replacing national accounts taxes per working-age person with the same taxes per resident gives 0.0077 (core sample 0.0081), and with labour taxes, income tax and National Insurance contributions per working-age person, 0.0091 (0.0098), the tax term significant at 1 per cent throughout; the household-tax and VAT variants of the OBR database, available from 1975, give 0.0081 to 0.0098 on 1977–2019 (Online Appendix Tables B4 and B5). Measuring the burden as a share of GDP instead removes the tax term (p = 0.66) and weakens the specification (RESET p = 0.047), a share of output being a burden on no one in particular; the average effective tax rate on labour following Mendoza, Razin and Tesar (1994) behaves like the per-capita measures (0.030 per percentage point, p = 0.017; projection 0.0064). The composition of the burden can be tested on the Bank of England's central government receipts by tax (Thomas and Dimsdale, 2017; Section 4.3), each measure in place of the national accounts total. Income tax gives 0.0077, National Insurance contributions 0.0092, the two together 0.0090, all direct taxes 0.0079, and total receipts 0.0074, each tax term positive and significant at 1 per cent; taxes on products and production give 0.0054, VAT 0.0068 and corporation tax 0.0057, and those three columns are the only ones in the battery to fail the RESET test (p = 0.04, 0.04 and 0.02): a burden on production is not a burden on the working-age voter. The core sample gives the same ordering (income tax 0.0079, contributions 0.0102, direct taxes 0.0076, receipts 0.0075, indirect 0.0060, VAT 0.0065, corporation 0.0063, the last three again failing RESET). Within contributions, the employee's share does the work: employee contributions per working-age person enter at 0.78 (p < 0.001) against 0.42 for employers', and entered together only the employee term survives (0.57, p = 0.002; employer 0.19, p = 0.09); on the core sample 0.69 against 0.44, and together 0.52 (p = 0.01) against 0.18 (p = 0.28). This is the tax the mechanism is about. National Insurance contributions are the earmarked receipts of the National Insurance Fund from which the state pension is paid, so the projected growth in the pensioner population is, in accounting terms, a projected shortfall in exactly this tax; the burden that best predicts the reach for the quiet instrument is the one that would otherwise have had to rise to meet the projection, and within it the part that appears on the employee's payslip. The salience argument could not ask for a cleaner alignment: the taxes a Chancellor would otherwise have to raise on working-age voters to fund pensions are the ones whose inherited weight predicts the expansion of the tax base by other means. What the burden is divided by matters; what is counted in it does not, beyond the finding that the taxes on labour carry it.

*Unemployment measures.* The unemployment rate in the specification is the ILO measure, extended before 1975 with the Bank of England's historical rate. It is not the only gauge the Treasury read. With the Bank's spliced administrative series in its place the projection is 0.0083 on both samples, and with the claimant count (available to 2016) 0.0085 on the extended sample and 0.0086 on the core, the unemployment term negative and significant in every column. The measure does not matter.

*Measurement basis.* On the LTIM series (1991 onward), which corrects the IPS's known deficiencies with Home Office and Labour Force Survey data, the projection coefficient is larger and significant at the 0.1 per cent level (0.0159, HAC s.e. 0.0040, on 28 observations), evidence that the headline result is not an artefact of the survey deficiencies documented in Section 4.1.

*Functional form and horizon of the signal.* The projected level of the OADR at the ten-year horizon, in place of the projected change, returns a similar coefficient (0.0075 against 0.0081 on the extended sample; 0.0085 against 0.0082 on the core), and for a reason that supports the change formulation rather than competing with it: the level is the current ratio plus the projected change, so the level model constrains the two to a common coefficient. Entered separately, the projection holds at 0.0084 while the current ratio contributes nothing (0.0004, p = 0.83), and the equality restriction is rejected (F(1, 47) = 41.2, p < 0.001), which is why the level form fits worse by 15.6 points of the information criterion; on the core sample the projection holds at 0.0083, the current ratio at 0.0002 (p = 0.95), the restriction is rejected at F(1, 36) = 8.28 (p = 0.007), and the level form fits worse by 6.2 points. The change formulation is retained as the measure of news relative to the inherited position, and the data agree. On the extended sample the horizon profile is monotone: nothing at one year (0.0018, p = 0.81), 0.0071 at three (p = 0.04), 0.0072 at five, 0.0074 at seven and 0.0081 at ten, the information criterion improving from −73.6 to −99.2 across the horizons. Figure 3 plots the coefficient at every horizon from one to ten years on the core sample, whose vintages publish every horizon. The projection term is significant at each horizon from three years on, in changes and levels and in weighted averages across horizons, with the ten-year delta fitting best, while the total dependency ratio registers at no horizon. The exception is instructive: at one and two years the change carries no signal (p = 0.47 and 0.11), consistent with policy responding to medium-term ageing news rather than to next year's arithmetic. The result does not depend on the choice of any single medium-term horizon.

*Dynamics.* The fiscal specification's residuals show serial correlation at the third and fourth orders (Section 6.2). A second lag of the dependent variable does not absorb it: the added lag is small and insignificant (−0.05, p = 0.65 on the extended sample; −0.06, p = 0.65 on the core), the information criterion worsens, the Breusch–Godfrey tests do not improve, and the projection coefficient is unchanged at 0.0080 on both samples. The dependence is a feature of the error rather than a missing dynamic, which is what HAC inference and the block bootstrap of Online Appendix B are designed to handle.

*Timing placebo.* With 55 annual observations, a finite-sample check that does not lean on asymptotic inference is worth having. Rotating the projection series through the sample years keeps its own persistence and distribution but breaks its timing relative to inflows; re-estimating under each of the 54 rotations gives a distribution of placebo t-statistics against which the actual one can be ranked. None comes close: the largest absolute t-statistic among the rotations is 5.33, against 8.15 for the actual alignment, so the randomisation p-value is 1/55 = 0.018, the smallest this design can deliver; on the core sample the largest rotated statistic is 3.97 against 5.68 (p = 1/43 = 0.023). The result depends on when the projections moved, not merely on how they are distributed.

*Sample and inference.* Results survive every start from 1965 to 1979 (0.0079 to 0.0083), the substitution of the trend for the tax burden, and OLS, HAC or Newey–West inference, so the result is not carried by any single political era, entry regime, or macroeconomic episode; on the core sample the starts 1979, 1983 and 1987 give the same. Split samples are less precise but consistent: 1965–91, 1992–2019, 1965–2003 (before the A8 accession) and 1975–2019 give 0.0087, 0.0134, 0.0106 and 0.0083, and on the core sample 1998–2019 gives 0.0110 and 1977–2003 0.0120; the first half of the core sample alone, 1977–97, in which the projections barely moved, returns 0.0063 with a wide interval. Figure 4 collects the projection coefficient from every variant in this section on the extended sample: all twenty-four intervals exclude zero and overlap the baseline, while the EU placebo and the total-dependency term estimated alongside the old-age ratio include it; the total-dependency term on its own does not, for the reason given above.

*Stability over time.* Re-estimating the fiscal specification on expanding samples gives 0.0088 with the sample ending in 1990, 0.0083 in 1994, 0.0101 in 1998, 0.0110 in 2001 and 0.0082 in 2010, every one within the full-sample band. Rolling 25-year windows return coefficients between −0.001 and 0.013, significant at 5 per cent in 23 of the 31 windows; the exceptions are the windows that lie entirely within 1966–1995, in which the projections barely moved. The OLS-CUSUM test does not reject constancy (p = 0.99), and on 1979–2019 the sup-F test for a single break at an unknown date returns p = 0.46. The relationship is not a creature of any sub-period, although its precision depends on the variation in projections that began in the mid-1990s.

*The instrument.* The projection should move the lever governments actually held, not only the outcome. Work permits and first permissions, the main control on labour migration from outside the Commonwealth and, after 1973, from outside the EEC, are available annually from 1946 to 2008 (Bauer and Salt, 2020). Because permits were set as numbers rather than growth rates, gross permits in levels are the reported form, with the same right-hand side as the fiscal specification and no basis step, the permit series having no change of basis. On 1965–2008 the projection enters at 0.61 thousand permits (p < 0.001), about 610 additional permits a year for each point of projected ten-year OADR rise, the same with the Commonwealth employment vouchers of 1962–72 added to the lever (0.61 on 1964–2008), and the total dependency ratio entered alongside contributes nothing (0.03, p = 0.38) while the old-age ratio holds at 0.54. On 1977–2008 the coefficient is 0.53 (p < 0.001), and with the total ratio alongside 0.51 against −0.05 (p = 0.74). The series is read from their Figure 1, reproduced in the replication package, and checked against the totals in their Annex 1. Adding permits to the inflow equation leaves the projection coefficient unchanged, so the response to projected ageing ran through work permits and, in larger part, through the other routes the government controlled.

*Emigration.* Emigration is not governed by the policy rule, and if admissions merely replaced departures, lagged emigration would enter the inflow equation positively. It does not: emigrants of all citizenships per working-age person add nothing to the equation for controlled inflows (−0.004, p = 0.83 on the extended sample; −0.001, p = 0.95 on the core), nor do British emigrants (p = 0.60 and 0.84), nor either to the equation for gross inflows (p ≥ 0.61), and the projection stays at 0.0082 to 0.0086 throughout.

*British citizens.* A second uncontrolled flow gives a less tidy answer than the EU margin. British citizens arriving from outside the EU (identified from 1975; ONS, 2020a) are not subject to entry control, yet the projection enters their inflow equation at 0.0065 (p = 0.04) on 1977–2019. The result is weak where the controlled result is strong: on 1979–2019 adding the projection improves the fit of the British equation by 2.8 points of the information criterion against 15.6 for non-EU inflows, the effect disappears in either half of the sample, and half the year-to-year variation in the British series is sampling error (published intervals of ±17 per cent, against ±9 per cent for non-EU inflows). It is also not where returning emigrants would put it: British citizens returning from Oceania and North America show no response, while the effect is concentrated in arrivals from Sub-Saharan Africa (0.0178, p = 0.012) and Asia (0.0074, p = 0.024), regions where, as former parts of the Empire, many British passport holders are citizens by descent or registration rather than emigrants who left the UK (Sriskandarajah and Drew, 2006), a population that nationality law shaped deliberately through the British Nationality Act 1981 (Tyler, 2010), the British Nationality (Hong Kong) Act 1990 and the Nationality, Immigration and Asylum Act 2002. Part of this flow is therefore likely to be first-time migration that resembles non-EU immigration in every respect but the passport, and whose size was itself a matter of policy. Controlling for the British flow directly leaves the projection at 0.0061 (p < 0.001), a lower bound on the policy response. On the British series that reaches back to 1964, which counts arrivals from all origins (ONS, 2020b), the response is weaker still: 0.0032 (p = 0.07) on the extended sample and 0.0064 (p = 0.03) on the core, and with that flow held constant the projection is 0.0076 and 0.0064.

## 7.1 What the fiscal model predicts without the projection

The fiscal hypothesis makes a claim about a missing variable: that the Treasury's own projection of ageing belongs in the rule that set admissions. A fair test asks two questions of the fiscal model without it. First, how well do the fiscal inputs alone determine controlled inflows, and is there an observed fiscal or macroeconomic variable that would improve that model on its own? Second, does any such variable also improve the model once the projection is added, or does the projection do work no observed variable can? The pool of candidates is the set of variables the Treasury observed, all lagged a year unless they are start-of-year information: the debt-interest share of spending and of GDP, the current deficit and debt ratios, taxes as a share of GDP, the Budget's own borrowing forecasts (OBR, 2026b), realised welfare spending, the Government Actuary's projected contribution pressure, gilt and bill rates, the real rate and the gap between the gilt yield and nominal growth, vacancies and the vacancy–unemployment ratio, wage inflation, GDP-deflator inflation and inflation expectations, the real exchange rate, the current account, the trade balance and the terms of trade, the labour share, real house-price growth, and real growth. Origin-country variables and the party in government are not candidates: they are not part of the Treasury's fiscal framework. The panels are Online Appendix Tables B18–B25.

*The fiscal model alone.* Without the projection the fiscal inputs still explain inflows, but worse in every respect. On the extended sample the information criterion is 23.6 points worse (−75.6 against −99.2), RESET falls to p = 0.055, the persistence coefficient rises from 0.46 to 0.66 as the lagged inflow absorbs what the projection explained, the tax term falls from 0.053 to 0.033, and the debt term loses significance (0.19, p = 0.28); on the core sample the criterion is 14.9 points worse, RESET falls to 0.12, persistence rises from 0.47 to 0.59 and the tax term to 0.039. Out of sample the gap is the same order: in recursive one-step-ahead forecasts on an expanding window, the root mean squared error over 1990–2019 is 0.145 without the projection and 0.113 with it on the extended sample, and 0.153 against 0.123 over 1997–2019 on the core, a fifth lower with the regressor fixed in advance.

*Can an observed variable do the projection's work?* Each candidate was added alone to the fiscal model without the projection, then beside the projection, on both samples and on the same observations, in levels and, for every positive-valued control, in logs; the same screen was run for gross inflows (Online Appendix Tables B18–B20). A variable that extends the fiscal model must improve both halves. In levels none does. The variables that improve the no-projection model are inflation (10.1 points of the criterion on the extended sample, 7.5 on the core), total managed expenditure as a share of GDP (8.5 and 3.1), the gilt yield (7.2, and 1.9 on the core), the Budget's borrowing forecast (6.5 and 5.3), the Actuary's earnings-basis contribution pressure (5.3 and 2.7), participation (4.8 and 3.9) and the bill rate (3.3 on the extended sample only); every one of them fails RESET in that model, and every one is null once the projection is added, worsening the criterion beside it. Each improves the projection-free model because it proxies the projection's low-frequency information, and each dies when the projection is in. In logs one candidate passes both halves on both samples: the log of vacancies per working-age person improves the no-projection model by 6.7 and 8.4 points and, beside the projection, enters at 0.15 (p = 0.01) and 0.19 (p = 0.01), improving the criterion by 4.4 and 3.6 points on its own rows and taking the projection to 0.0074 and 0.0067; the log of the vacancy–unemployment ratio does the same (8.0 and 9.5; then 0.12 and 0.17, p = 0.02 and 0.01; projection 0.0071 and 0.0064), and both do so for gross inflows as well (6.8 and 5.0 points beside the projection). This is the labour-demand result of Section 7.2 in the functional form that fits: demand measured in logs is the one observed variable that both predicts admissions without the projection and adds to the model with it, and it costs the projection about a fifth of its coefficient while leaving unemployment imprecise (−0.014, p = 0.10; −0.013, p = 0.16), the two being near-collinear. It does not replace the projection: the no-projection model with log vacancies still fails or nearly fails RESET (p = 0.06 and 0.02), and the projection beside it keeps its significance and its horizon profile. The pre-stated selection rule, forward selection from the fiscal model without the projection admitting only variables observed throughout both samples with the same sign and p < 0.10 in both, selects CPI inflation first (−0.017, p < 0.001 on the extended sample; −0.018, p = 0.005 on the core; the Bank's preferred CPI measure, Thomas and Dimsdale, 2017) and then the indicator for the Bank of England's independence from 1998, and stops; the log-vacancy terms, admissible on their own, fall out once inflation is in. The second selection is the 1998 policy-era step of Section 7 under another name, and the two together are what the projection-free model finds: a price regime and a break in 1998. Beside the projection the selected pair is weak (inflation −0.006, p = 0.10 and 0.22; the 1998 step 0.12, p = 0.09, and 0.11, p = 0.16), the projection holds at 0.0055 and 0.0057 (p < 0.001 on both samples) and adds 5.5 and 3.6 points to the criterion on top of it, and the selected model forecasts worse than the fiscal baseline out of sample (0.121 against 0.113; 0.142 against 0.123). Neither selected variable is a policy variable by the paper's own test: CPI inflation predicts the EU flow the government could not ration (−0.029, p = 0.006), as does the deflator (−0.021, p = 0.017), which is besides the growth rate of the index that already converts the tax term to real terms, and both correlate at about −0.6 with the lagged inflow, high in the 1970s when the 1971 Act had cut admissions and low in the decades in which they rose; the 1998 step is null on the EU margin (p = 0.75) and, as Section 7 showed, discretises the projection over the years it covers. Inflation belongs among the controls of the next subsection, not in the rule; the log-vacancy result is reported there as the one augmentation that meets the criterion, and its place in the headline specification is a decision left explicit rather than made by fit. *Debt interest.* The one non-demographic pressure with a signal is the share of spending pre-empted by debt interest, the Treasury's measure of the room it did not have. On its own it is null in the projection-free model (p = 0.55 and 0.58). Beside the projection it is positive and significant, 0.018 (p = 0.05) on the extended sample and 0.021 (p = 0.02) on the core, and the projection rises to 0.0096 and 0.0108: the interest burden and projected ageing are negatively correlated over the sample (−0.47 on the core), high interest in the 1980s coinciding with the projections' low years, so each is better identified with the other held fixed. The variable passes the tests a policy-pressure variable should: it is null on the EU margin (p = 0.33) and leaves the projection null there, it enters the gross-inflow equation as it enters the rate equation (0.020, p = 0.02; projection 0.0102 on the extended sample, 0.0107 on the core), and with it in the specification the total-dependency ratio, the horizon profile, the zero-migration and publication-dated projections, the permit lever and the timing placebo all return their verdicts unchanged. It complements the projection rather than rivalling it: with debt interest and CPI inflation both in the model the criterion is 3.1 points better than the baseline on the extended sample (4.8 on the core) and the projection is 0.0078 and 0.0091, while without the projection the same additions leave debt interest null and RESET rejecting (p = 0.011 and 0.002). A heavier interest bill, given projected ageing, meant more admissions; but no reading of the interest bill predicts admissions without the projection. It is reported as the lead column of the debt panel below and not carried into Table 2: the primary specification is the Treasury's documented framework, and a variable that earns its place only conditionally is a finding about the mechanism, not a change to the rule.

## 7.2 Further economic and fiscal controls

The projection could still stand in for something the Treasury watched more closely. The Bank of England's *Millennium of Macroeconomic Data* (Thomas and Dimsdale, 2017) supplies annual averages of the labour-market, price and public-finance series the Treasury read, to 2016. The series are carried to 2019 from their current official sources, spliced onto the 2016 Millennium values: vacancies, unemployment, the LFS rate and earnings growth from the ONS labour-market series AP2Y, MGSC, MGSX and KAB9, CPI inflation from series D7G7 (ONS, 2026); the Treasury bill rate from the Bank's tender rate IUMAAJNB and, after its discontinuation in mid-2017, Bank Rate IUMABEDR less the 2016–17 mean gap between the two (Bank of England, 2026); the real effective exchange rate from the BIS real narrow index (Bank for International Settlements, 2026); real house prices from the UK House Price Index (HM Land Registry, 2026); debt interest and total managed expenditure from the Historical Public Finances Database (OBR, 2026a); the current account, trade balance, terms of trade and labour share from the ONS balance-of-payments and national-accounts series (ONS, 2026). The replication package lists each splice with its series identifier. Every column that uses an extended series is re-estimated to 2016 in the replication output and does not change. The controls are grouped by what they measure (Online Appendix Tables B26–B35), and each is added to the fiscal specification with unemployment retained: unemployment is the documented Treasury input, and dropping it for a labour-demand measure that happens to fit better would be the data-driven choice the specification protocol of Section 5.3 forbids.

*Labour demand.* Labour demand matters in the way conventional migration economics expects, and it is the one addition that moves the coefficient at all. Vacancies per working-age person beside unemployment enter at 0.013 (p = 0.05) on the extended sample, with the projection at 0.0072, and at 0.017 (p = 0.07) with the projection at 0.0064 on the core; the vacancy–unemployment ratio adds nothing beside unemployment (p = 0.12 and 0.60; projection 0.0074 and 0.0076); and the annual change in the log ratio, the tightening of the market rather than its level, enters at 0.068 (p = 0.05) and 0.084 (p = 0.17), with the projection at 0.0078 on both samples. Unemployment and vacancies in levels are near-collinear (their ratio is the tightness measure), so both lose precision together; substituting vacancies for unemployment, reported for reference and not as a candidate specification, takes the projection to 0.0067 and 0.0059. Measuring labour demand directly therefore bounds the demand share of the response at about a quarter and leaves the fiscal channel intact. The labour-demand channel appears exactly where the fiscal channel does not: on EU inflows, the uncontrolled margin, vacancies enter at 0.038 (p = 0.05) while the projection is −0.002 (p = 0.52), and work permits over 1965–2008 respond to both, the projection at 0.55 thousand per point beside vacancies at 0.59 (p = 0.03).

*Wages, rates, expectations, productivity and institutions.* Wage inflation enters negatively and marginally (−0.007, p = 0.05 on the extended sample, projection 0.0065; −0.011, p = 0.08 and 0.0068 on the core); real wage growth, the Treasury bill rate, the real short rate, inflation expectations at either horizon (entered for the year in which the forecast was made, as the projection is), productivity growth, the benefit replacement ratio and union density are all null, with the projection between 0.0076 and 0.0088 on the extended sample and 0.0079 and 0.0094 on the core. Realised inflation, whether the GDP deflator or the CPI, is negative and marginal at best (deflator −0.007, p = 0.10, projection 0.0069; CPI −0.006, p = 0.10, projection 0.0066 on the extended sample; p = 0.12 and 0.22 on the core), and Section 7.1 gives the reason it is not carried further. With the vacancy ratio, wage inflation, the bill rate, short-term expectations, TFP growth and vacancies entered together the projection is 0.0080 on the extended sample, where none of the six is individually significant, and 0.0127 (p < 0.001) on the core, where expected and realised wage inflation take opposite signs and the VIF exceeds 30; adding emigration or the change in the tightness ratio changes nothing.

*Realised spending.* The Treasury could be responding to bills already arriving rather than to those projected. The historical public finances database divides spending by function, with social security split between pensioners and non-pensioners from 1978–79 (OBR, 2026a). On 1979–2019, adding the previous year's spending on health, on pensioners, on non-pensioners, or on health and pensions together — as shares of GDP, and health also per working-age person — leaves the projection between 0.0074 and 0.0089, significant at 1 per cent throughout, while the spending term is never significant (p ≥ 0.65) and never improves the information criterion; replacing the projection with realised spending worsens the criterion by 9 to 18 points. The Bank's longer series tell the same story on both samples: current grants to the personal sector, as a share of GDP or in real terms per working-age person, and total managed expenditure as a share of GDP are null beside the projection, which stays at 0.0080 to 0.0083, and in the projection's place realised grants worsen the criterion by 28 points on the extended sample and 19 on the core and fail RESET (p = 0.03 and 0.05). Controlled migration tracks projected costs, not incurred ones.

*The current balance and the borrowing plans.* The specification's debt term is the annual log change in the debt ratio, the trajectory rather than the position. The position adds nothing: last year's net borrowing (p = 0.96 and 1.00), net debt (p = 0.73 and 0.99) and taxes as a share of GDP (p = 0.66 and 0.09) leave the projection at 0.0077 to 0.0082. Borrowing in place of the trajectory enters at 0.015 (p = 0.05; 0.019, p = 0.04 on the core) with the projection at 0.0091 and 0.0093, and in the projection's place it has the wrong sign, a larger deficit followed by fewer inflows (−0.026 and −0.024), and fails RESET: a non-demographic "close the deficit" account does not carry the result. Nor do the government's own borrowing plans. The spring Budget's forecast of borrowing for the coming year, the change in borrowing it planned, and its furthest-horizon forecast, from the OBR's database of historical official forecasts (OBR, 2025; from the 1971 Budget), are null beside the projection (p ≥ 0.26; projection 0.0069 to 0.0082) and, in its place, again carry the wrong sign (−0.017, p = 0.004; −0.019, p = 0.02 on the core) and fail RESET. The rule responded to a projected burden, not to the deficit it was running or planning.

*Debt dynamics.* Debt interest as a share of spending is the finding of Section 7.1: 0.018 (p = 0.05) and 0.021 (p = 0.02) beside the projection, which rises to 0.0096 and 0.0108, and null alone. Debt interest as a share of GDP behaves the same way with less precision (0.023, p = 0.18; 0.030, p = 0.10 on the core; projection 0.0096 and 0.0104); the ten-year gilt yield and the gap between it and nominal growth are null (projection 0.0074 to 0.0080). The cost of borrowing mattered through the budget it pre-empted, not through the rate.

*The Government Actuary's projections.* The Quinquennial Reviews of the National Insurance Fund (1960–2017; Section 4.3) project the joint Class 1 contribution rate the Fund would require. The ten-year change in that rate under earnings-linked benefits is the Actuary's own translation of ageing into a tax, and it predicts inflows on its own (0.010, p = 0.005 on the extended sample; 0.044, p = 0.02 on the core), but it is null beside the population projection (p = 0.42 and 0.43; projection 0.0075 on both samples), fails RESET in the projection's place (p = 0.006 and 0.02) and fits 18 and 12 points worse there. Under the uprating policy actually in force, price indexation from 1980 to 2010, every review from 1982 to 2010 projected the required rate to fall, and that series carries nothing anywhere (p ≥ 0.17). The Treasury acted on the demographic projection, not on the actuarial rate its indexation policy had made look affordable.

*The Treasury view.* Macpherson's (2014) account of the department's standing doctrine suggests a last set of candidates: free trade, sound money, scepticism about demand management, doubts about raising revenue "beyond a certain point", spending control, the supply side, and institutions and rules (Online Appendix Table B32). Each has a measurable counterpart in the Bank's dataset or the public finances: trade openness, broad money growth, participation, the distance of taxes as a share of GDP from their highest value of the previous ten years, real growth of total managed expenditure, and step indicators for the Bank's operational independence (from 1998), the golden rule (1998–2009) and the OBR era (from 2010). Beside the projection all are null (p ≥ 0.16; projection 0.0070 to 0.0095), with one telling exception: the 1998 independence indicator enters at 0.12 (p = 0.04) on the extended sample and 0.11 (p = 0.07) on the core, and on its own it is the single strongest predictor in the projection-free model (8.8 and 6.1 points of the criterion). It is, of course, the policy-era step of Mitchell, Pain and Riley in another guise, and it behaves as that step did: it discretises the projection over the years after 1997 and adds nothing to it. The revenue ceiling, the measure closest to Macpherson's "certain point", improves the projection-free model modestly on the core sample (3.0 points) and is null beside the projection (p = 0.58); openness, money growth, spending growth and the OBR era carry nothing anywhere. The doctrine that raised the cost of the visible instruments is not itself a rival predictor of the quiet one.

None of these additions is carried into the headline specification: the labour-market variants in levels improve the information criterion by one or two points at most, the one variant that meets the criterion of Section 7.1 (vacancies in logs) is a decision for the headline rule that this paper leaves explicit, the debt-interest variant earns its place only conditionally, and the specification of Table 2 is the one that survives on every sample and every year the data cover. The augmented models are reported for what they show: that measuring labour demand directly bounds the demand share of the response at about a quarter, that the interest burden sharpened the response rather than substituting for it, and that no realised or planned fiscal quantity carries the projection's information.

# 8. Out-of-Sample Events, Limitations, and Agenda

*The post-2019 episode.* The estimation sample ends at Brexit, after which the policy regime changed discretely. The subsequent history is directionally consistent with the fiscal account: a government elected on a mandate to reduce migration instead liberalised the non-EU regime, abolishing the Resident Labour Market Test, lowering salary thresholds for settlement and shortening the settlement path (Migration Observatory, 2021), amid a pandemic that raised the debt-to-GDP ratio by more than ten percentage points in a single year and against official projections of renewed ageing as the second, larger baby-boom cohort of the 1955–65 wave approached retirement (ONS, 2019). Net migration rose from 184,000 in 2019 to over 900,000 in 2023, driven overwhelmingly by non-EU entry (Migration Observatory, 2026; BBC News, 2025). Part of that rise is mechanical rebalancing across origins under equalised entry conditions, one reason no structural claim is made on five years of post-regime-change data. The episode is noted because the model predicts the *direction* of the response to the fiscal shock, and the leading alternative, the disposition of a governing party that had just campaigned in the opposite direction, does not.

*Limitations.* Three are material. First, identification: the zero-migration reconstruction of Section 7 treats all net migrants as working-age, an approximation that the vintages' age-specific migration assumptions would refine. Second, aggregation: the dependent variable pools work, study, and family entry, and category-level responses cannot be recovered from the IPS. The pooling itself is defensible on the grounds given in Section 2.3: every category was policy-controlled, every category adds working-age residents, and students in particular arrive with work rights and spending power, with recorded departures well below arrivals over the sample. But the model's sharper implication, that the response should concentrate where administrative discretion and youth are greatest, is testable only with category-level administrative data (item (vi) below). Third, mechanism: outside the 2000–2001 episode documented in Section 2.5, the evidence remains institutional and statistical. The model explains why documentary confirmation should be scarce, since the instrument's value lies in its silence, but the drafting record of Section 2.5 shows that the reasoning was written down at least once and can therefore be found; the archival programme below extends that search to the Treasury's own files and to the Conservative-era decades the released record does not cover.

*Agenda.* Each limitation admits a concrete remedy, listed in order of importance. (i) Refine the zero-migration reconstruction of Section 7 using the vintages' age-specific migration assumptions and the official variant projections published alongside later NPPs, replacing the all-working-age approximation. (ii) Exploit the biennial NPP releases as information shocks, sharpening the news test of Section 7: the revision between successive vintages, driven substantially by mortality and fertility surprises such as the repeated upward revisions to longevity across the sample, is plausibly exogenous news about the fiscal future, and Home Office quarterly visa grants by category provide an outcome with the frequency to support an event-study design around release dates, multiplying the effective sample and permitting the timing of policy responses to be estimated rather than assumed. Concretely: decompose each revision into its mortality, fertility, and migration-assumption components using the published assumption sets, and estimate the response of visa grants by category in the quarters after each release to the mortality-and-fertility component alone. The outcome is grants rather than rule changes, consistent with Section 2.3, since a single Statement of Changes can loosen and tighten across origins at once and admits no directional coding. That design uses only news orthogonal to migration by construction, and would be decisive in a way the present annual reduced form cannot be. (iii) Take the mechanism to the archives: text analysis of Budget speeches, Red Books, and Treasury fiscal-sustainability documents for dependency-related language, correlated with subsequent Statements of Changes; and Treasury files at the National Archives, opened through the mid-1990s by the thirty-year rule, so the sample period's first half is already inside the declassification window. Undigitised Treasury forecast material (HM Treasury, 1997) would further relax the annual-data constraint. (iv) Test external validity in a cross-country panel using UN projection vintages and comparative migration-policy databases: several high-income countries saw politically unclaimed expansions as their boom cohorts approached retirement, and the model predicts the channel is strongest where fiscal authority is centralised and migration policy executive-controlled. (v) Model the pension-age and migration instruments jointly; the Pensions Act 1995 is a within-sample realisation of the alternative extensive-margin response, legislated with the low-salience profile the model predicts. (vi) Extend the realised-spending check of Section 7, which uses the OBR's functional split from 1978–79, with state pension and health expenditure per working-age person from DWP benefit-expenditure tables and PESA outturns over the whole sample; since the OADR terms already carry the demographic component of age-related spending, such a variable adds only policy generosity, and its expected role is that of a check rather than a rival. (vii) Estimate category-level responses across work, study and family entry on Home Office administrative data, testing whether the response concentrates where age profiles are youngest and discretion greatest.

# 9. Conclusion

This paper asked whether the sustained and politically ‘homeless’ expansion of controlled immigration to the United Kingdom between 1964 and 2019 can be explained within the outlines of fiscal policy. The answer returned by the data is consistent with this hypothesis. A newly assembled series of the government's own biennial ageing projections predicts controlled inflows with a short-run response of 0.8 per cent per projected point of ten-year OADR deterioration and a bias-corrected long-run response of approximately 1.8 per cent, conditional on the Treasury's fiscal state variables and with signs matching the salience model of instrument choice, and the estimate is the same over fifty-five years as over the forty-three on which an earlier version rested. The relationship is absent where the fiscal account says it should be: projections of the fiscally cheap total dependency ratio carry nothing once the old-age ratio is present, and EU inflows, which no British government could ration, do not respond.

Beyond its substantive claim, the paper contributes a reusable object and a reusable frame: the reconstructed vintage series of official British ageing projections from 1963 onward, extendable through the archival route documented in Online Appendix A to the first projection of 1954, against which any hypothesis about the policy influence of demographic expectations can now be tested; and the treatment of policy instruments as priced by institutional salience, which also predicts the observed preference for legislating pension-age increases decades before they bind.

The interpretation is disciplined by what the design cannot yet deliver. The projections are the government's own and embed migration assumptions; the documentary record of the mechanism, though it now includes the released 2000 drafts in which the instrument-choice reasoning appears and is then removed before publication, covers a single episode; and the sample is one country and fifty-five years. The agenda set out above, covering variant projections, forecast-revision shocks at higher frequency, archival text analysis, and cross-country comparison, is the path from a robust and repeatedly falsifiable association to a causal account.

The finding bears on a question larger than migration policy. Democracies facing slow-moving fiscal pressures possess instruments that differ enormously in visibility: taxes announced from a despatch box, pension ages legislated decades before they bind, entry rules amended by statutory instrument on a quiet parliamentary afternoon. The British case suggests that sustained, consequential policy can flow through the least visible instrument precisely because it is least visible, and that the resulting gap between what democracies vote on and what their governments do can persist for decades without any actor needing to conceal anything, because the concealment is supplied by the institutional architecture itself. As the demographic transition arrives across the rich world and the fiscal arithmetic that Britain confronted becomes general, understanding when governments reach for quiet instruments, and what that choice does to democratic accountability, seems among the more consequential questions the political economy of public finance can ask.
