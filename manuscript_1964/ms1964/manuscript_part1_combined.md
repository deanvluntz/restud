# 1. Introduction

Across the period studied, the UK experienced a sustained rise in policy-controlled immigration. Gross inflows of foreign nationals subject to entry control fell from 140,000 in 1964 to a low of 73,000 in 1977, under successive Acts restricting Commonwealth entry, and then rose to 343,000 by 2019 — across a spectrum of Conservative, Labour, and Coalition governments (Office for National Statistics, 2020a, 2020b). Notably, no major Westminster party ever campaigned on a platform of higher immigration during this period or in living memory; several campaigned explicitly on reducing it. The disconnect between stated policy and realised outcomes became a defining feature of British politics, contributing to the 2016 referendum vote to leave the European Union, in which control of migration was the most commonly cited motivation among Leave voters (Centre for Social Investigation, 2018). Yet existing economic and political models of migration offer no consistent account of why policy-controlled immigration fell and then rose over more than half a century, under governments of every stripe.

This paper proposes and tests a fiscal explanation: that successive governments used the least visible instrument available to them, controlled immigration, to offset the fiscal cost of projected population ageing. The empirical framework is an autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) regression of log controlled inflows (non-British citizens until 1974, when every foreign national required permission to enter, and non-EU citizens thereafter; scaled by the lagged working-age population) on the projected ten-year change in the OADR and a parsimonious set of fiscal state variables: the lagged tax take per working-age person, the lagged annual log change in the debt-to-GDP ratio, and the lagged unemployment rate. The central estimate implies that a one-point increase in the projected ten-year OADR deterioration is associated with approximately 0.8 per cent higher controlled inflows on impact and, once the finite-sample attenuation of the autoregressive coefficient is corrected, approximately 1.8 per cent in the long run. The uncorrected least-squares estimate, recorded for comparability, is 1.5 per cent; the correction runs in the conservative direction throughout. The coefficient is stable across horizons, functional forms, sample start dates, and measurement bases, and it is the same on the two samples the paper reports side by side: the core sample of 1977–2019, on which every projection vintage is complete and the dependent variable is measured on a single basis, and the extended sample of 1965–2019, which reaches back through the change of entry regime in 1973 and the change of measurement basis in 1975 and moves the coefficient by one unit in the fourth decimal place. Two falsification exercises support a fiscal-policy interpretation. Projections of the *total* dependency ratio, which include children, whose fiscal cost is a fraction of that of pensioners, carry no information once the old-age ratio is present. And the same specification estimated on *EU* inflows, which policy could not ration, yields an OADR coefficient indistinguishable from zero. The existing candidate explanations each fail on timing or on scope. Labour-demand and network accounts explain why migrants wished to come but not why a state with full legal control over the relevant margin repeatedly chose to admit them, against its stated platform, in rising numbers. Explanations based on the ideology of the governing party fail because the rise did not change when the government did: the expansion proceeded under Thatcher and Major before Blair, and accelerated again under governments elected on restrictionist manifestos. The econometric literature's best answer to date is candid about its own limits: the largest and most careful study attributes the bulk of the rise to unexplained "structural change" captured by dummy variables (Mitchell, Pain and Riley, 2011). A satisfactory explanation must identify a pressure that operated continuously across governments, was visible to all of them, and made expanding this particular margin the path of least resistance for each.

The explanation is motivated by the framework established by the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR). As part of their national economic forecasts, the UK’s fiscal watchdog envisions multiple scenarios based on different projections of future net migration, where larger inflows are associated with greater fiscal sustainability. Their stated assumption is that since inward migrants are traditionally concentrated in working ages, then higher net migration raises the ratio of taxpayers to dependents and materially improves the projected path of public debt. For reference, the 2014 exercise projected that roughly doubling assumed net migration, from 105,000 to 225,000 a year, would leave public sector net debt in 2063–64 at 41 per cent of GDP rather than 83 per cent (OBR, 2014). If past governments have internalised this arithmetic, and if raising migration is politically cheaper than raising taxes or cutting age-related spending, then official demographic forecasts should predict the migration flows that policy controls. This possibility is strengthened with an understanding of the OBR’s history, elaborated upon in Section 2.1. In short, the authority inherited many features from its predecessor in the Treasury, including arguably an institutionalised ideology with far reach in the British state.

The fiscal logic has a second premise, without which arithmetic alone explains nothing: the instrument must be politically cheaper than its alternatives. Raising taxes or cutting age-related spending requires announcement in the Budget, the most scrutinised event in the British political calendar, legislated through a Finance Bill whose defeat is treated as a matter of confidence. Migration rules, by contrast, are amended by statutory instrument, without debate or vote. A government confronting a projected deterioration in the ratio of pensioners to workers therefore faces three margins of adjustment priced very differently by its institutions, and the cheapest is the quietest. The body of the paper develops this instrument-choice logic formally and shows that Britain's specific institutional architecture (a doctrinally continuous Treasury with authority over every department, an executive integrated with the legislature, and a delegated rule-making channel for entry policy) supplied the conditions for the quiet margin to be used.

The hypothesis I test is therefore specific: that projected deteriorations in the OADR, as published in the government's own biennial NPPs, predict subsequent inflows of non-EU migrants, conditional on the fiscal variables that the institutional literature identifies as the Treasury's decision inputs. The choice of non-EU flows as the outcome is central to the design. Under EU free movement, European inflows responded to market conditions and were beyond the reach of the Home Office; non-EU entry, by contrast, was rationed throughout the period by a discretionary work-permit regime and, from 2008, a points-based system. If demographic forecasts shape policy, their imprint should appear on the controlled margin and not on the uncontrolled one. This contrast supplies the paper's principal falsification test.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it assembles a new annual panel of official demographic forecasts: I reconstruct the projected old-age dependency ratio, at horizons of one to ten years, from every National Population Projection vintage between the 1963-based projection and the 2018-based; the six vintages before 1975 survive as five-year age distributions or summary tables and are converted to the working-age and pension-age definitions of the later series (Online Appendix A). Most survive only in un-indexed scans on the National Archives; the access route recovered in the process reaches back to the first, 1954-based, projection, so the full history is available to later work. To my knowledge no previous study has compiled the UK's projection vintages into a usable time series, and no study of any country has tested whether such projections predict policy-controlled migration. Second, the paper provides a political-economy rationale for the reduced form. I set out a stylised model in which a fiscally dominant finance ministry allocates adjustment across three instruments (taxes, borrowing, and controlled migration) in proportion to their political salience. Britain's institutions generate the stark salience asymmetry described above, and the model delivers the estimating equation's signs as ex ante predictions rather than ex post rationalisations. Third, the paper offers a documented alternative to the leading existing explanation of the UK's migration expansion. Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011) attribute around three-quarters of the rise in net immigration in the decade to 2009 to unexplained structural change, proxied by dummy variables and attributed to the policy disposition of the post-1997 Labour government. I show that the timing of non-EU inflows tracks the projected ten-year deterioration in the OADR across the whole period 1965–2019, including under the Conservative administrations of the 1980s and 1990s and the restrictive governments of both parties in the 1960s and 1970s, which a Blair-specific ideological account cannot explain.

The paper is related to three literatures. The first is the literature on the determinants of international migration. In the canonical selection framework (Borjas, 1987), migration responds to the income differential between destination and origin net of moving costs; Hatton and Williamson (2002) document the empirical qualifications: emigration from the poorest countries is constrained by the cost of moving, networks of previous migrants lower that cost and propagate flows, and demographic pressure in source countries (a large youth share) raises out-migration. Two findings from this literature frame the present exercise. Hatton and Williamson note that destination-country *policy* is the binding constraint that most quantitative models omit, evidenced by queues and irregular entry wherever pressure exceeds permitted volumes; and Mayda (2007), integrating policy regimes into a bilateral panel, finds that restrictions create an asymmetry in which origin-country push factors lose explanatory power while destination-country conditions retain it. That is exactly the environment in which the determinants of the *policy* become the determinants of the flow. On the destination side, Storesletten (2000) establishes the fiscal arithmetic this paper takes as its premise: in a calibrated overlapping-generations model, immigration policy alone can in principle sustain fiscal policy through population ageing, provided entrants are of working age and sufficiently skilled; the United Nations (2000) bounds the same arithmetic from the other side, finding the inflows required to hold potential support ratios at their existing levels implausibly large. That is a demonstration of feasibility, and of its limit, not a test of use. Strikingly, the source-country demographic result still has no destination-side empirical counterpart: the possibility that *ageing destinations* pull migrants through deliberate policy has not been tested. That inverse is this paper's subject.

Within the UK-specific branch, Mitchell and Pain (2003) advocate the ARDL approach adopted here, document the persistence of flows and the measurement error in stock estimates, and find relative incomes and trade links matter; Hatton (2005) finds a large role for the existing migrant stock and reports that unemployment affects migration in changes rather than levels; Mitchell, Pain and Riley (2011), using Bayesian model averaging, conclude that around three-quarters of the rise in net immigration in the decade to 2009 is structural change "attributed to UK and foreign immigration policy", captured by dummy variables and interpreted through the policy disposition of the post-1997 government. This paper supplies the policy determinant those studies proxy with dummies, and offers evidence that it operated well before 1997. Second, the paper draws on the institutional literature on British fiscal governance (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974; Hood, King, McLean and Piotrowska, 2023, separated by half a century yet in substantial agreement) to specify which variables enter the Treasury's information set, and on critiques of the OBR's independence (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021) to argue that the projection-to-policy channel predates the OBR's creation in 2010. Third, the paper connects to the political economy of policy instrument choice and salience: the mechanism proposed here is an application, in a fiscal setting, of the general principle that governments shift adjustment toward instruments with low public visibility.

One clarification of scope. The argument is not a claim of coordinated concealment, still less of intent beyond fiscal management, but a claim about incentives. Each government faced the same constraint set: rising projected age-related spending, a politically expensive tax instrument, a constrained borrowing margin, and a migration instrument adjustable at near-zero parliamentary cost. The hypothesis is that each, independently, found the same margin cheapest. No coordination across governments is required for the policy rule to appear stable, only a stable constraint set. The distinction matters because the observation that migration rose without electoral mandate has been appropriated by conspiratorial narratives; the mechanism proposed here is, by contrast, ordinary public finance conducted through the instrument that ordinary politics made cheapest, and its evidential standing rests on tests it could have failed.

Three limitations should be stated at the outset, and the empirical strategy in Section 5 addresses each as far as the data allow. First, official population projections embed assumptions about future migration, so a projection is not mechanically exogenous to the outcome; I argue, and exploit the demographic arithmetic to show, that the ten-year-ahead old-age population is almost entirely determined by cohorts already resident, which limits the contamination; Section 7 shows the estimates are unchanged when the projections are rebuilt under zero net migration, closing the concern directly. Second, the annual sample is short (55 observations), which constrains the number of regressors, limits the power of unit-root and cointegration tests, and biases the coefficient on the lagged dependent variable downward in small samples. Third, the evidence for the mechanism is institutional and statistical rather than documentary for most of the sample: no Budget speech states that migration policy was set with reference to dependency-ratio projections, and the instrument-choice logic developed below explains precisely why such statements would not be made. The important exception is the turn-of-the-millennium episode, for which released internal drafts document the reasoning directly, and document its removal before publication (Section 2.5). Section 8 sets out the archival and quasi-experimental extensions (projection revisions as information shocks, quarterly visa grants, and text analysis of Budget documents) that would convert the association documented here into a fully identified causal claim.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the institutional setting; Section 3 the model; Section 4 the data, including the reconstruction of the projection series; Section 5 the empirical strategy and identification threats. Section 6 reports the main results and Section 7 the robustness and falsification tests; Section 8 discusses the post-2019 episode and the agenda, and Section 9 concludes this paper.

# 2. Institutional Setting

## 2.1 The Treasury and the fiscal constitution

The plausibility of the mechanism tested in this paper rests on two institutional claims: that a single actor, His Majesty's Treasury, has both the incentive and the authority to treat migration as an instrument of fiscal policy, and that the instrument could be adjusted at low political cost. This subsection documents the first claim; the next documents the second.

The Treasury centralises within a single department powers which other governments distribute across several: budgeting, tax policy, macroeconomic policy, and the financing of the state; the Institute for Government describes its power as exceeding "that of any other part of government apart from 10 Downing Street," with a "pervasive" veto (Institute for Government, 2024b). Two features of this authority matter for the argument. The first is continuity of preferences across elected governments. The department's doctrine of fiscal conservatism, the "Treasury view" (Hansard, 1929), is traceable to Gladstone (Higgs, 1924), and Heclo and Wildavsky (1974), interviewing officials in the early 1970s, and Hood et al. (2023), writing fifty years later, describe substantially the same institution. This continuity is what permits a single policy rule to be estimated over a fifty-five-year period spanning eleven Prime Ministers, from Wilson to Johnson. Heclo and Wildavsky's officials also state the model's behavioural assumption in their own words: the Treasury's chief interest is "to save themselves the unpopularity of having to put up taxes." Their account of process anchors the information set of Section 4: annual budgeting took the previous year's position as baseline and incorporated internal forecasts, many unpublished, over five-year horizons.

The second feature is the department's willingness to pursue its doctrine independently of, and occasionally against, the elected government of the day. The clearest case falls in the middle of the sample. In the sterling crisis of 1976, with the Cabinet divided, the Treasury negotiated the IMF loan on the basis of borrowing figures later found to have been grossly overstated; Edmund Dell, then Paymaster General, later defended the exaggeration in print as necessary to secure the cuts that he and the department believed Britain required, against the platform on which the government had been elected (Dell, 1991; López, 2014). The episode is offered not as typical but as an existence proof: the department could steer major policy outcomes through its control of forecasts and figures, without public announcement. A milder version of that disposition, policy shaped by the fiscal centre's doctrine through instruments below the threshold of public debate, is all the mechanism of this paper requires.

The episode was not isolated, and the disposition it reveals is recorded by participants on every side. Denis Healey, Chancellor through the crisis, later wrote that he could forgive the Treasury the inaccuracy of its forecasts but not its "misleading the Government, the country and the world for so many years about the true state of public spending in Britain", adding that he suspected "that Treasury officials were content to overstate public spending in order to put pressure on governments which were reluctant to cut it" (Healey, 1990). Tony Benn complained that Healey's first Budget had been "written by the Treasury and not by Ministers"; the Cabinet saw it only hours before it became public (Dell, 1991). And the pattern had a documented precedent. In December 1974 the Permanent Secretary, Sir Douglas Wass, advised the Chancellor that the Treasury saw "no alternative" to a formal pay norm that the TUC, the Labour Party, and the Prime Minister had all rejected; through the spring of 1975 the Treasury rationed the Bank of England's support for sterling so that, in its own words, "the main responsibility for depreciation is seen to lie elsewhere than with HMG", and the £6 pay norm followed in July (Rogers, 2010). The department obtained a policy elected politicians had refused by arranging for the constraint to appear external, which is the instrument-choice logic of Section 3 in a different currency.

Samuel Brittan's contemporary account, written in 1969 after some sixty interviews with officials, describes the department the model needs from inside the period the extended sample adds. Departments still regarded the Treasury "as it had been two centuries before, primarily as the body that held down their spending"; its concern to restrain spending and resist tax proposals predicted its behaviour better than "the more sophisticated doctrines which the Department proclaims"; Ministers were rarely able "to question the analysis on which the advice to them is based"; and advice to Ministers was treated "on a par with the secrets of the confessional", the Budget forecasts remaining unpublished until 1968 and the five-year expenditure projection appearing only in 1963 and 1966 (Brittan, 1969). Brittan is a critic of the department's critics as much as of the department, warning against treating it as a single mind and noting that it overran its own spending ceilings, and his book contains no mention of immigration, ageing, or the Government Actuary: the silence the model predicts, and a caution against reading the mechanism into any document of the 1960s that does not state it.

The Office for Budget Responsibility, created in 2010, inherited the Treasury's forecasting functions. Small at its creation, staffed from the Treasury, and assessing the government against targets the Treasury sets in the Charter for Budget Responsibility (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021), it is better read as publishing arithmetic the fiscal centre had long performed internally than as the source of a post-2010 discovery, and the empirical analysis accordingly treats the whole period 1964–2019 as governed by one informed fiscal centre. The full institutional history, including the case studies summarised in this section, is in Online Appendix E.

## 2.2 The salience asymmetry: Budget Day and the Immigration Rules

Tax and spending measures are announced in the Budget, the most scrutinised event in the British political calendar, and enacted through a Finance Bill whose defeat is by convention a matter of confidence (UK Parliament, 2026). Heclo and Wildavsky (1974) describe Budget Days as "great political occasions" on which "the pressures on a Chancellor are intense". Writing in 1969, Brittan observed that "The Cabinet itself hears the Budget only the day before" and that "Budget secrecy has become a cult" (Brittan, 1969). The cost of mispricing their politics recurs, most recently in 2022, when a fiscal statement delivered without the customary OBR assessment ended the Truss premiership within weeks. The fiscal cost of an ageing population, if met through taxation or spending retrenchment, must be announced on this stage and voted upon.

Migration policy faces no comparable exposure. The Immigration Act 1971 delegated the detail of entry policy to the Immigration Rules, which the Home Secretary amends by laying a Statement of Changes before Parliament: secondary legislation requiring no debate, no vote, and no Commons majority, laid several times a year (Home Office, 2026). The shift from primary to secondary legislation accelerated over precisely the period studied. The annual count of statutory instruments stepped up from around two thousand to above three thousand from about 1990, while the number of Acts of Parliament roughly halved (House of Commons Library, 2019; Hansard Society, 2014), a shift that belongs to no single administration and runs alongside the migration expansion rather than after it. A government wishing to expand the inflow of taxpayers could do so through an instrument whose visibility, relative to a Budget measure of equivalent fiscal value, was close to zero.

Coordination between the fiscal centre and the Home Office requires no public act. Collective responsibility forecloses public dissent from positions agreed in Cabinet, and the Treasury sets every department's budget, the Home Office's included. The claim advanced here is therefore not that migration targets were written down in Budget documents (they were not, and Section 3 explains why the logic of instrument choice predicts their absence) but that the institutional machinery for using migration as a fiscal instrument existed, was centralised, and operated below the threshold of parliamentary and public scrutiny throughout the sample period.

## 2.3 Migration policy instruments, 1964–2019

The Immigration Act 1971, which ended Commonwealth free movement two years before EEC accession created its European replacement, is the hinge of the institutional story: it created the delegated rule-making machinery through which all subsequent non-European entry policy has been made (Hatton and Wheatley Price, 1999; Bauer and Salt, 2020). From accession the United Kingdom, as an EEC and later EU member, could not restrict inflows from other member states; control was exercised exclusively over the non-EU margin, through two successive regimes. Before 1973 the same control reached every foreign national: aliens required a work permit under a scheme dating from the Aliens Order 1920 (Bauer and Salt, 2020), and Commonwealth citizens, from the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962, an employment voucher whose issue the government rationed by category and, from the 1965 White Paper, by an announced annual ceiling; the 1968 Act extended control to holders of British passports without a parent or grandparent born in the United Kingdom. The controlled margin of the 1960s is therefore the inflow of all non-British citizens, which is how the dependent variable is defined for those years (Section 4.1).

Under the work-permit system, in force until 2008, a non-EU national required an offer of employment from an employer who had shown, under the Resident Labour Market Test, that the vacancy could not be filled domestically, and a separate entry visa could be refused even after a permit was granted; by the mid-2000s more than eighty routes existed (Home Office, 2006, p. 7). Absent published quotas, this dual approval gave the state continuous discretionary control over volumes. It also dictates the measurement strategy: a Statement of Changes may loosen some routes while tightening others, historical permit data are too fragmentary for statistical use (Bauer and Salt, 2020), and no stringency series spans the period, so policy is measured by its outcomes on the controlled margin, under the maintained assumption, borne out by the post-Brexit episode, that the instrument was effective.

The permit lever's demographic sensitivity, and the preference for operating it quietly, are both visible at the administrative level. Commenting on a draft careers booklet in 1990, the Employment Department's Overseas Labour Section recorded that "because of demographic changes the number of work permits being granted is rising sharply", with approval rates near 75 per cent under the main scheme and 90 per cent for trainees, while asking that "actual figures" be "omitted as they can be misleading" (The National Archives, LAB 109/66). The demography in question was the late-1980s shortfall of young labour-market entrants rather than old-age dependency; what the file documents is the permit lever moving with demographic pressure, and an official preference against publicising the numbers, a decade before the drafting record of Section 2.5.

The points-based system introduced from 2008 consolidated the routes into five tiers and allocated entry by points for salary, qualifications, language and, notably, age: applicants aged 27 or under received 20 points toward the threshold, the credit declining to 5 points and cutting off at 31 (Home Office, 2006), the closest the regime comes to a published statement that the demographic structure of inflows was a policy objective. Volumes were delegated to labour demand subject to thresholds adjustable by statutory instrument, shifting apparent responsibility for them from the government to the labour market.

The dependent variable aggregates all long-term non-EU inflows, including students and family migration, rather than work-visa entries alone. From a fiscal standpoint what matters is the addition of working-age residents, whatever their entry category, and every category's criteria were policy-controlled; students, the youngest entrants, fit the fiscal logic rather than strain it. Aggregation forecloses only category-level estimates, taken up in Section 8.

The post-2019 regime change, in which the points-based system was extended to EU nationals and several of its parameters were liberalised (Migration Observatory, 2021), falls outside the estimation sample and is used in Section 8 as an out-of-sample check.

## 2.4 The information set: National Population Projections

The forecasts at the centre of the analysis are the National Population Projections, produced biennially since 1954 by the Government Actuary's Department (GAD) until 2006 and by the Office for National Statistics (ONS) thereafter. Each vintage projects the population by single year of age on stated assumptions about fertility, mortality, and net migration, and reports the implied dependency ratios for every projected year. The measure used here is the old-age dependency ratio: persons of state pension age per thousand of working age, on the UK definition applied consistently within each vintage. It is the headline ageing measure of the ONS's own bulletins, and fiscally the emphasis is well placed: those aged 65 and over were projected to absorb some 42 per cent of UK health spending by 2021/22 and draw the state pension besides, while education, the principal fiscal cost of children, takes roughly nine per cent of public spending (Full Fact, 2018; Institute for Fiscal Studies, 2024). An anticipated rise in the OADR is, to a close approximation, an anticipated rise in mandatory spending per worker.

Old-age dependency is, uniquely among fiscal pressures, forecastable. Britain's post-war baby boom arrived in two waves, the second and larger over roughly 1955–65, and each reaches pension age on a schedule known decades ahead (Jones, 1980): the people who will be pensioners in ten years are alive, resident, and counted today, so at that horizon the projection error reduces essentially to mortality surprises.

For the government of the day the NPP was not an external publication but an internal product. GAD, established in 1919, described itself as "a Chancellor's department", set its objectives and spending plans with Treasury ministers, and produced the projections as a service to all departments (House of Commons Treasury Committee, 2001); when the advice mattered most, the Chancellor had GAD advise from inside the Treasury (Pemberton, 2018); and the horizon of an official projection could decide which costs entered the fiscal debate at all. Introducing the 1974 White Paper that created the State Earnings Related Pension Scheme, the Secretary of State wrote that its cost "has been very carefully considered in relation to the capacity of the country to support it"; Hemming and Kay (1982) could "find little to indicate that this is a true statement", because the Government Actuary's memorandum projected the scheme only to 2008–09 and "did not measure, and did not claim to measure" the cost of the new commitments, which favourable demographic trends masked within that window, against a long-run cost they put at around 13.4 per cent of qualifying earnings. Nothing suggests the figures were falsified; the point is that the horizon of a projection produced by a department answerable to the Chancellor determined what was visible. That the projections were internal to government in the 1960s and 1970s is not in doubt: the Treasury's own Budget forecasts were unpublished until 1968 and its five-year expenditure projections appeared only twice before 1970, and the medium-term projections begun in the late 1950s to judge what public expenditure the economy could afford took the growth of the working population as one of their two inputs (Brittan, 1969). Toward the end of the sample the link became statutory. The Code for Fiscal Stability (HM Treasury, 1998) required illustrative long-term fiscal projections in the Budget documents over a horizon of "no less than 10 years into the future" (HM Treasury, 1999, p. 87), the horizon at which this paper's projection variable is measured, and from 2002 a dedicated Treasury unit worked more closely with the Home Office on the economics of migration (Eich, 2008). The first such annex also illustrates the instrument problem: among the responses to ageing it advertises, the legislated rise in the female state pension age "will play a key role in reducing long-term spending pressures", while migration appears only among the uncertainties (HM Treasury, 1999, p. 90). The pension-age instrument, legislated fifteen years before it began to bind, is named and claimed; the instrument adjustable by statutory instrument is not.

## 2.5 Documentary evidence: the 2000 Cabinet Office analysis

For one episode, the turn-of-the-millennium expansion at the centre of the structural-change debate, the mechanism can be documented directly, in a drafting record released under the Freedom of Information Act (Cabinet Office, 2010). In 2000 the Cabinet Office Performance and Innovation Unit and the Home Office jointly prepared the government's first public, comprehensive economic analysis of migration, published in January 2001 (Glover et al., 2001). The released drafts permit a comparison of what the government's analysts wrote internally with what the government published.

The preliminary draft of July 2000, classified RESTRICTED–POLICY, reasons precisely in the terms of Section 3. It quantifies the demographic constraint through the zero-migration counterfactual: without net migration, the working-age population would fall by two million over twenty-five years while the over-65 population rose by three million, taking the support ratio from above four to below three. It quantifies the instrument: net migration of roughly 120,000 per year to hold the working-age population constant, and of nearly a million per year to stabilise the support ratio. It states the instrument menu: ageing "will have to be addressed: presumably by some combination of net migration, an increase in the average working life, changes to entitlements, increases in contributions (either tax-based or private), and increases in productivity," adding that "the alternative to migration may often be tax increases, which would also have an adverse economic effect." And it draws the political-economy conclusion: these pressures would recast immigrants "less like a threat and more as a way to avoid the more unpleasant alternatives of tax increases, benefit cuts, and economic stagnation."

None of this survived to publication. The published paper retains the demographic description, the support ratio falling from more than four to less than three, but the quantified migration requirements, the instrument menu, the comparison with tax increases, and the "unpleasant alternatives" passage are all absent; the released November 2000 draft shows the excision had already occurred by that point, dating it to the autumn in which ministers first announced the "managed migration" reframing of policy. The episode thus documents both halves of the model: the reasoning, migration as the fiscally equivalent and politically cheaper substitute for taxation, articulated inside government at the moment the expansion began; and the salience management, observable as the difference between draft and publication. Two limits on the inference should be noted at once: the record covers a single episode under one government; and although the project was commissioned through the Chancellor's cabinet committee and drew on unpublished Treasury research, the drafting hands are the Cabinet Office's and the Home Office's, so the reasoning survives in their voice rather than the Treasury's own, whose parallel paper trail remains to be recovered from the archives (Section 8).

The 2000 record is the clearest instance of the mechanism's paper trail, but not the earliest evidence that the fiscal centre read the projection machinery. The Treasury's 1984 Green Paper on long-term expenditure costed the pensioner numbers implied by the official projections, with the Cabinet Office warning of the 'political risks' of illustrating that arithmetic in public, and by November 1996 the Treasury's own board had been briefed that the old-age dependency ratio would nearly double over fifty years (HM Treasury, 1984; The National Archives, PREM 19/1190; T 733). None of these documents names migration as the response; what they establish is receipt, fiscal reading, and an explicit official sensitivity to the salience of the visible alternatives.

The premise that migration improves the public finances was also stated on the record. In its 2008 evidence to the House of Lords Economic Affairs Committee, the Home Office, the department responsible for entry policy, reported its own estimate that in 1999–2000 first-generation migrants paid 10 per cent more in taxes than they received in public services and benefits, against almost 5 per cent for the UK-born, and cited GAD analysis in which the joint Class 1 National Insurance rate needed to balance the National Insurance Fund in 2060–61 fell from 27.9 per cent under low migration to 26.3 per cent under high migration, a "positive long-term impact of immigration" that "should not, therefore, be overlooked" (Home Office, 2008, paras 2.2.4 and 2.3.3). Whether migration in fact improves the public finances is a separate question; the argument here requires only that the government assumed it did, and the department that set entry policy and the Treasury's own actuary both did.

## 2.6 The institutional argument in summary

The sections above establish three premises on which the model and the empirical design rest. First, a single, doctrinally continuous fiscal centre, the Treasury, possessed throughout 1964–2019 both the information (internally produced demographic projections and fiscal forecasts) and the authority (budgetary control over every department, seniority within an integrated executive) to treat migration as an instrument of fiscal policy. Second, the political price of the alternative instruments was institutionally fixed and high: taxes and spending must be adjusted in the Budget, under maximal scrutiny and a confidence-vote convention, while migration rules were adjustable by statutory instrument at negligible parliamentary cost, a salience asymmetry built into the machinery of government rather than chosen by any administration. Third, the instrument was effective and continuously available: through dual work-permit approvals and adjustable points thresholds the state exercised standing discretion over non-EU entry, while EU entry remained beyond its control, supplying both the treated and the placebo margins the empirical work requires.

Evidence framing His Majesty's Treasury as the principal agent is indirect, but well-founded. Its authority over both fiscal policy and departmental funding gives it the means and motive to influence other areas of government, including immigration policy (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974; Hood, King, McLean and Piotrowska, 2023). Documentary evidence links the Treasury's interest in migration policy to the dependency ratio (Cabinet Office, 2010). The fall of the Truss premiership demonstrated that Treasury institutions can outlast any individual government (Wamsley, 2026) – and are therefore likely capable of producing the consistency of results within a single framework across more than fifty years of government that my model results suggest. A century of scholarship documents the department's independence and an ideological inclination towards fiscal conservatism and austerity (Higgs, 1924; Institute for Government, 2024a). Furthermore, the Dell affair provides a historical example of the Treasury acting independently to determine policy when those principles were in conflict with a democratic mandate (Dell, 1991; López, 2014). A case at the opening of the sample follows this pattern – a 1964 Labour mandate to divide Treasury authority with the creation of a new Department of Economic Affairs was seen as a "power struggle" fought and won by the Treasury, which re-absorbed the DEA within five years (Clifford, 1997). Seen in this light, the continued independence of the Office for Budget Responsibility (which split off from the department as promised by the 2010 Conservative manifesto) can be understood alongside further evidence to characterise the OBR as a continuation of Treasury principles. Indeed, the Charter governing the OBR is dictated by the Treasury and establishes the benchmarks on which their fiscal policy can be judged. The OBR's methodology in this regard suggests an institutional assumption of positive correlation between higher net immigration and fiscal sustainability (Berry and Berry, 2013; Walsh, 2021; OBR, 2014).


# 3. A Stylised Model of Fiscal Instrument Choice

This section sets out a deliberately minimal model whose purpose is to discipline the empirical specification: it delivers the sign and the conditioning set of the estimating equation as ex ante predictions.

Consider an incumbent government that must finance age-related spending each period. Let dₜ denote the old-age dependency ratio and let Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] be the government's own projection of the ratio h years ahead, taken from the current NPP vintage. Mandatory spending per working-age person is g(dₜ), with g′ > 0. The government disposes of three instruments: the tax burden per working-age person τₜ, borrowing bₜ, and controlled (non-EU) migration mₜ, which augments the working-age tax base. The per-period budget identity is

[[EQ]]
τₜ + bₜ = g(dₜ),
[[/EQ]]

and anticipated ageing, Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ > 0, raises the spending requirement the government expects to have to finance within its planning horizon.

Each instrument carries a political cost proportional to its salience. Following the institutional evidence of Section 2, tax changes are maximally salient (they must be announced in the Budget and legislated), borrowing is constrained by bond markets and the Treasury's doctrinal aversion to debt, whereas migration adjustments are minimally salient (proceeding by statutory instrument). Let the period political cost be

[[EQ]]
Cₜ = ½ φ_τ sτ (τₜ − τ̄)² + ½ φₘ sₘ mₜ² + φᵤ uₜ₋₁ mₜ + ½ ψ (mₜ − mₜ₋₁)²,
[[/EQ]]

where sτ ≫ sₘ > 0 are salience weights, τ̄ is the inherited tax burden regarded as politically neutral, uₜ₋₁ is the unemployment rate observed when policy is set (higher unemployment raises the political price of visible foreign inflows), and the final term is an adjustment cost reflecting the administrative and diplomatic frictions of changing entry rules quickly. The government minimises expected discounted political cost subject to the budget identity, a debt limit bₜ ≤ b̄(Bₜ₋₁) that tightens in the inherited debt stock Bₜ₋₁, and the demographic transition law under which migration today lowers the dependency ratio at the projection horizon.

Each element of the cost function has an institutional referent. The salience ranking sτ ≫ sₘ is Section 2.2 in symbols: a pound of fiscal adjustment obtained through the Budget is priced at the full attention of the political system, the same pound obtained through a Statement of Changes at the attention of specialists. The borrowing margin is constrained doctrinally by the Treasury view and externally by market discipline, tightening in the inherited stock, which is why lagged debt, not the flow of borrowing, enters the policy rule. The unemployment term reflects the one circumstance in which the migration instrument's salience rises sharply: visible inflows during visible joblessness. That inflows carry an electoral price at all, so that sₘ is positive rather than zero and the instrument is quiet rather than free, is well established: exploiting the quasi-random allocation of refugees across Danish municipalities, Dustmann, Vasiljeva and Piil Damm (2019) find that local exposure shifts votes toward anti-immigration parties outside the largest cities. The adjustment cost stands in for the administrative machinery of entry policy, through which rule changes propagate over months and years, not instantly.

The first-order condition for migration takes the form

[[EQ]]
φₘ sₘ mₜ + φᵤ uₜ₋₁ + ψ (mₜ − mₜ₋₁) = λₜ · MB(Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ),
[[/EQ]]

equating the marginal political cost of admissions (left side) to their marginal fiscal benefit (right side), where λₜ, the shadow value of fiscal room, rises with the inherited tax burden, through sτ, and with the tightness of the borrowing constraint, through b̄(Bₜ₋₁), and the marginal benefit MB(·) rises with the projected demographic deterioration the inflow offsets. Relief is more valuable the worse the projected position and the more expensive the alternative instruments. Linearising around the inherited position yields a policy rule of the form

[[EQ]]
mₜ = ρ mₜ₋₁ + β₁ (Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ) + β₂ τₜ₋₁ + β₃ Bₜ₋₁ − β₄ uₜ₋₁,
[[/EQ]]

with all coefficients positive. Online Appendix D derives the rule and proves the following.

*Proposition 1.* Suppose that tax changes are more salient than migration adjustments (sτ > sₘ > 0), that adjustment is costly (ψ > 0), that the debt limit tightens in the inherited stock, and that the marginal fiscal benefit of migration rises with the projected deterioration. Then the policy rule for controlled migration has the following properties.

(P1) Controlled migration rises with the projected deterioration of the OADR, because migration is the low-salience margin of fiscal adjustment: β₁ > 0, and β₁ is larger the greater the salience gap sτ/sₘ.

(P2) Migration rises with the inherited tax burden, because a high τₜ₋₁ raises the marginal political cost of further tax increases and induces substitution toward the quiet instrument: β₂ > 0, provided the salience of a further tax increase rises steeply enough with the burden already borne (condition (A2) of Online Appendix D).

(P3) Migration rises when the borrowing position has been deteriorating, because a tightening constraint closes the intertemporal escape route: β₃ > 0. Empirically the constraint is measured by the recent change in the debt ratio rather than its level (Section 4.3).

(P4) Migration falls with lagged unemployment, which raises the political price of inflows: β₄ > 0.

(P5) Adjustment is gradual, ρ ∈ (0, 1), which motivates the autoregressive term in the empirical specification.

(P6) Flows the government cannot ration do not respond: the projection enters the rule only through the instrument, so its coefficient in an equation for uncontrolled (EU) inflows is zero.

(P7) Only fiscally priced dependency matters: a projected change in the total dependency ratio, whose child component carries a small fiscal cost, enters with a coefficient attenuated towards zero.

P1 to P4 fix the signs of the estimating equation before estimation. P6 and P7 are the two falsification tests of Section 7, and they discriminate between the fiscal account and its main rivals: a labour-demand or network account of the same inflows predicts that EU inflows respond to the same pull, and gives no reason to distinguish the old-age ratio from the total.

The framework can be read as organising fiscal capacity along two margins. On the *intensive* margin the government extracts more from the existing working-age population, through the tax rate directly, or through policies raising employment and participation, which is why the unemployment and inactivity rates belong in the information set. On the *extensive* margin it enlarges the base itself. Migration is one extensive-margin instrument; the other is redefinition of the base by raising the state pension age, which converts prospective dependents into prospective taxpayers at the stroke of a statute. The model treats the two as substitutes priced by salience, and the sample contains one large realisation of the second instrument: the Pensions Act 1995, which legislated the equalisation of the female pension age from 60 to 65, phased over 2010–2019, enacted fifteen years before it began to bind, a timing profile itself consistent with salience management, and named in the 1999 Budget as a key means of reducing long-term spending pressures (Section 2.4). Because the projections used here incorporate legislated pension-age changes, the estimated migration response is identified net of this alternative channel; a joint model of the two instruments is beyond the present data but is noted in Section 8 as a natural extension.

The model abstracts from much: migrants are homogeneous fiscal contributors rather than heterogeneous workers; wages and prices do not respond to inflows; salience weights are exogenous rather than shaped by media and opposition strategy; and the projection is taken as the government's belief rather than modelled as a signal-extraction problem. Each abstraction isolates the mechanism under test, and each marks a boundary the conclusions must respect: the estimates below identify a policy response to projected ageing, not the welfare consequences or general-equilibrium incidence of that response.

Two remarks connect the model to the institutions. First, the model predicts silence: because the value of the migration instrument derives from its low salience, no optimising government publicises its use, which is why the mechanism must be detected in outcomes rather than announcements, and why the absence of documentary statements is consistent with, rather than evidence against, the hypothesis. This feature is uncomfortable, since it renders the mechanism difficult to falsify from the documentary record alone, and it is precisely why the falsification tests of Section 7, which the reduced form *can* fail, carry the evidential weight. The prediction is also, in one documented instance, borne out literally: the instrument-choice reasoning appears in the internal draft of the government's 2000 migration analysis and is excised from the published version (Section 2.5). Second, the model predicts which projection should matter, the old-age ratio rather than the fiscally cheap total ratio (P7), and which flows should respond, the controlled margin and not the uncontrolled one (P6). These yield the two placebo tests implemented in Section 7.

# 4. Data

Two estimation samples are carried through the paper and reported side by side wherever an exercise can be run on both. The core sample is annual, 1977–2019 (43 observations): the earliest start the non-EU series and the lagged log change in debt/GDP allow, and the years for which every projection vintage is published in full, so that every check of Section 7 is available. The extended sample is 1965–2019 (55 observations): inflows by citizenship are published from 1964, the first year of the International Passenger Survey's migration tables, and the earliest projection vintage held is the 1963-based, so 1965 is the first year for which every lagged regressor exists. The extended sample opens in the year of the White Paper that capped Commonwealth employment vouchers and closes at Brexit; it spans net emigration and net immigration, two entry regimes, and eleven Prime Ministers. Each sample has a claim the other lacks. The core sample is the cleaner object: a single dependent variable on one basis, annual vintages that publish every horizon and their components of change, publication dates, EU inflows for the placebo, and the years to 2019; every exercise in the paper can be run on it. The extended sample is the harder test: it adds fourteen years under a different entry regime, on a different measurement basis, with a different unemployment source and projections built from five-year age groups with interpolated tenth years, and it spans net emigration as well as net immigration; but its dependent variable is spliced, its earliest projections are coarser, and over 1965–78 the projection declines almost linearly, so those years add stability rather than independent identification (Section 7). Where a check needs data the pre-1975 vintages or surveys do not supply, it is run on the core sample and says so. Table 1 summarises definitions and sources.

## 4.1 Dependent variable: controlled inflows

Annual gross inflows of long-term migrants (intended stay of one year or more) by origin are taken from the International Passenger Survey (IPS), conducted by the ONS at ports of entry since 1961. The controlled margin is defined by citizenship. From 1975 the dependent variable is inflows of non-EU citizens arriving from non-EU countries (ONS, 2020a). Before 1975 the survey did not distinguish EU from other foreign citizens, and the series used is inflows of all non-British citizens (ONS, 2020b), which is the controlled margin in those years: until accession in 1973 every foreign national required a work permit or an employment voucher to enter (Section 2.3). The two bases are spliced without rescaling, since a factor computed from later years would use information no policymaker of the 1960s possessed, and a step indicator for the pre-1975 years enters every specification to absorb the change of definition. Its estimated size, reported in Section 6.2, corresponds to the EU share of non-British inflows in the 1970s and to nothing else. Non-British inflows throughout, and to 2003 (before the accession of the A8 states), are reported as alternative definitions in Section 7. The dependent variable is the log of controlled inflows per thousand working-age residents, with the working-age denominator lagged one year to prevent current-year inflows from entering both sides of the equation.

The IPS is a voluntary interview survey: ONS surveyors, stationed at UK entry/exit ports, approach travellers at fixed sampling intervals, achieving coverage of roughly 90 per cent of passenger traffic, with a 68 per cent response rate among those approached (ONS, 2023). Its limitations are well documented. Migration status is self-reported intention, not realised duration: a respondent intending a year's stay but leaving after ten months is a long-term migrant in the data, a student who remains after graduation on a work visa is recorded only under the status held at entry, and respondents with reasons to misreport, prospective asylum applicants among them, face no verification. Sampling variance is material for thinly travelled routes, which is why origins are published as regional groupings rather than individual countries.

The decennial Census provides the accuracy benchmark. A stock count cannot supply annual flows, so its role is indirect: the ONS uses each Census to validate and, where necessary, adjust the intervening decade of IPS estimates, and the record of those reconciliations (below) is the best available evidence on the survey's reliability.

Three considerations govern the use of the IPS despite these deficiencies. First, and decisively for this paper's question, the IPS was the basis of the official migration estimates used by the government itself throughout the period (Portes and Forte, 2017): it measures the flows as policymakers perceived them, which is the relevant object when the outcome of interest is a policy response. For the same reason the series is used as published contemporaneously, not as retrospectively revised: the government of 1995 responded to the 1995 figures, not to their 2012 corrections. Second, the alternative series is Long-Term International Migration (LTIM), which adjusts the IPS for visa switchers, asylum flows, and the Irish land border, and which the ONS cautions cannot simply be compared with the raw IPS (ONS, 2015). It begins only in 1991; it is used as a robustness check on the overlapping sample. Third, the decennial Census, against which the ONS benchmarks the IPS, has contradicted it materially only once, when the 2011 Census revealed an undercount of post-2004 EU8 arrivals through regional airports and prompted a methodological expansion, and that discrepancy concerned EU rather than non-EU flows. The maintained assumption is that non-EU measurement error, whatever its level, is not systematically correlated with the regressors; classical error in the dependent variable inflates standard errors without biasing coefficients, which works against finding the results reported below.

The gross-inflow basis (rather than net) is preferred because policy rations entry, not exit: the instruments of Section 2.3 operate on admissions, and emigration, of Britons and of previous migrants alike, responds to decisions outside the policy rule. Scaling by the lagged working-age population expresses the inflow in the units the fiscal mechanism cares about (additions per thousand existing contributors); results for gross inflows unscaled by population are reported as a robustness check, so that no population estimate appears on either side of the equation. That the sample covers years of net emigration as well as of net immigration is itself useful: the result no longer rests on a single regime of the balance of flows. All long-term entry categories are aggregated (work, study, family) because every category's entry criteria were policy-controlled and every category adds working-age residents; the category-level responses that aggregation forecloses are taken up in Section 8.

## 4.2 The projection series

The variable of interest is the projected change in the OADR: Δ̂dₜ(h) = Eₜ[dₜ₊ₕ] − dₜ, the difference between the OADR projected h years ahead in the most recent NPP vintage and its current value. The baseline horizon is h = 10; horizons of 1, 3, 5 and 7 years, weighted averages across horizons, and projected levels rather than changes are all examined below. A worked example fixes the construction: for 1997, the operative vintage projected an OADR roughly six points higher in 2007 than its 1997 value, so Δ̂d₁₉₉₇(10) ≈ 6.1, at the time the largest projected ten-year deterioration on record, though the vintages of the early 2000s would soon quadruple it. The variable is best understood as the ageing news confronting the Budget's author: how much worse, in the government's own published expectation, the ratio of pensioners to taxpayers will be a decade hence.

Assembling this series required reconstructing the full history of NPP vintages, and the reconstruction is described in Online Appendix A in the detail required for replication. In brief: the ONS website publishes complete projections only back to 2014; earlier vintages exist as scans on a National Archives site that is neither searchable by its own tools nor indexed by search engines, and direct navigation recovers vintages only to 1998. A machine-readable annex to the 2008-based NPP was found to contain direct links to every predecessor publication back to the first 1954-based projection (ONS, 2016b); through those links the projected old-age dependency ratio at each horizon was extracted for every vintage from the 1975-based projection through the 2018-based, and the six earlier vintages, 1963- to 1973-based, were added for the extended sample (GAD and ONS, 1963–2018). Those six are published in a different form. The 1963- to 1969-based projections give the population by five-year age group and sex for the first seven or eight years and for selected later years only (for the 1963-based: 1973, 1980, 1990 and 2000); the 1971- and 1973-based give working-age and pension-age totals annually. Working age is taken as 16–64 for men and 16–59 for women, with those aged 15 estimated as one-fifth of the 15–19 group, and where the year ten years ahead is not published the projected ratio is interpolated linearly between the two nearest published projection years. Applied to the annual 1971-based projection, the same interpolation across seven-year gaps reproduces the published values with a mean absolute error of 1.4 points and a maximum of 3.8, against a standard deviation of the ten-year change of about 11 points over the sample. Components of change are not published before the 1971-based projection, so the zero-net-migration reconstruction of Section 7 cannot be extended before the 1975-based vintage. In years between biennial vintages, the most recent vintage's projections are carried forward, mirroring the information actually available to policymakers; the same convention supplies the annual working-age population denominators. Multi-vintage records of these projections have been assembled before, but from within the statistical service that produced them (Shaw, 2007) or over the recent, publicly navigable vintages; outside government, the pre-1998 record was effectively unobtainable until the retrieval route documented here. To my knowledge this is the first horizon-complete, machine-readable panel of projected dependency ratios by vintage assembled from public sources, and the first use of the projection vintages as an information-set regressor in a model of policy. It may be of independent use to researchers studying the formation and revision of official demographic expectations.

Two timing conventions deserve note. First, each NPP is labelled by its base year but published with a lag of roughly a year; the baseline assigns vintages by base year, on the grounds that GAD was an arm of government and the fiscal centre had access to the projections in advance of publication, an assumption that is defensible for the internal-information argument of Section 2.4 but is confronted directly, as an identification matter, in Section 5.2. Second, the UK definition of the OADR uses the state pension age, which for women rose from 60 toward 65 over 2010–2019 under legislation enacted in 1995; because the reform was legislated fifteen years in advance, it is embedded in the relevant projection vintages, and the projections therefore already incorporate the principal policy change to the ratio's definition within the sample.

## 4.3 Fiscal and macroeconomic controls

The institutional literature reviewed in Section 2 specifies the Treasury's decision inputs: the fiscal aggregates and the labour market position, observed with a lag at Budget time (Heclo and Wildavsky, 1974). Accordingly the controls are the previous year's values of the following.

Tax per working-age person, in thousands of constant (2024–25) pounds, constructed by dividing national accounts taxes from the OBR's Historical Public Finances Database (OBR, 2026a), deflated with HM Treasury's March 2026 GDP deflators, which run from 1955–56 (HM Treasury, 2026), by the working-age population as per the most recent NPP. The per-working-age-head basis, rather than the tax-to-GDP ratio, is the theoretically appropriate measure: the political cost in the model attaches to the burden borne per taxpayer, and taxes on the working-age population, where income tax and National Insurance alone account for around 45 per cent of National Accounts taxes across the sample and, with VAT, around two-thirds, are where the incidence of an ageing population falls. In the model's terms this variable measures how far the intensive margin has already been used, and therefore the marginal political price of using it further.

*The annual log change in public sector net debt as a share of GDP*, from the OBR databank: the trajectory of the debt ratio rather than its level, measuring the rate at which the borrowing margin is closing. The level appears in the stationarity tests but not in the estimating equation.

*The unemployment rate* (ONS, 2026, series LF2Q, on the ILO definition), extended before 1975 with the Bank of England's break-adjusted historical rate (Thomas and Dimsdale, 2017), which agrees with the ONS series within 0.2 points where the two overlap; the Budget-time reading of the labour market, which enters the model both as a political cost of visible inflows and, in conventional migration economics, as a demand-side determinant.

Candidate variables tested and excluded on statistical grounds during specification development (the economic inactivity rate, real GDP and productivity growth, ten-year and three-month gilt yields (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 2025), and the Budget-time official growth forecast for the year, digitised from HM Treasury Budget reports via the historical fiscal database of the Economic Statistics Centre of Excellence, 2026) are documented in Section 6.1, with the selection protocol stated in Section 5.3. The balance of payments, which Heclo and Wildavsky's officials named among their inputs, is not included: their interviews predate the floating of sterling in 1972, and Edmund Dell, who became the Treasury's second minister in March 1974, judged the Bretton Woods system already dead by then (Dell, 1991). The growth forecasts deserve a note: they are the only official forecasts published consistently alongside Budgets over the sample, but their published timelines shift across eras, and the unemployment forecasts of the earlier decades were verbal rather than numerical, a data limitation that the archival agenda of Section 8 could relax.

## 4.4 The absence of an instrument

No credible instrumental variable for UK migration inflows exists in the literature. The only proposed proxy, National Insurance registrations (Portes and Forte, 2017), fails at the first requirement of relevance, on the ONS's own assessment: registrations do not distinguish short-term from long-term stays and cannot net out emigration (ONS, 2016a). The institutional setting compounds the problem: enforcement of the right-to-work regime attached to National Insurance has long been weak by European standards, so the mapping from registrations to resident inflows is loose in both directions. The identification strategy therefore rests on timing, predetermined regressors, and falsification, as set out next, rather than on exclusion restrictions.
