Original §12 Conclusion (Sam's wording, saved before the 22 Sep 2026 redraft)

My original hypothesis proposed that fluctuations in Britain’s old age dependency ratio affected the rate of migration through UK policy, and this relationship can partially explain why the UK has had such a large influx of immigrants in recent years.

Following in the OBR’s footsteps, I have built a framework in which migration is modelled as a fiscal credit. I base my framework on the Chancellor’s annual budget speech, where the government’s annual fiscal policy is condensed into one document meant to meet the fiscal and political demands of the public. In testing determinants of non-EU migration, my tests indicate major significance for old age dependency ratios in various formats (levels, deltas - both weighted and unweighted) at every horizon from three years out – whether projections are taken at 3, 5, 7 or 10 years in advance. These significance levels persisted even as further controls and alternatives were added. The fit is strongest at the ten-year horizon and the one-year change carries no signal at all – a pattern that matches the horizon the government set itself. The Code for Fiscal Stability required illustrative projections of the key fiscal aggregates “for a period not less than 10 years” (HM Treasury, 1998), so from 1999 the horizon the data select is the one the fiscal framework mandated.  

My preferred log level specification estimates that the rate of non-EU migration increases by approximately 0.81% for every point of difference between the 10-year projected old age dependency ratio and its current level. Even before the modern era of New Labour migration, I can see this relationship manifested with growing non-EU migration in the period 1981-1985. 10-year deltas turned positive for the first time, having been negative since at least 1976.  I also see positive growth in the final years of the 80s, where 10-year deltas were lower but positive, and unemployment was decreasing past the 10% watermark of previous years. 1990 was the turning point – 10-year deltas would not turn positive again until 1996, where non-EU migration rose once more in the final year of the Major administration. Again, these changes under different Conservative governments suggest the migration of recent years was not the product of Blairite ideology. Looking at the demographic projections during Blair’s reign, one might speculate migration numbers would have been largely similar if the election had gone the other way. The 1997 10-year delta of 6.14 set a record high for the time, but was dwarfed by successive growth in following years. 2002 and 2003 averaged 10-year deltas of 25 points, projecting consecutive years of 19% growth irrespective of other factors. This was marked by gross non-EU inflows clearing the 300,000 thresholds for the first time in 2005 – the figure of 324000 was over 3 times greater than the level of migration only 10 years prior. 

 This was partially mitigated by growing unemployment - my model suggests a 1% increase in unemployment in the previous year reduces this year’s migration by approximately 2%, likely reflecting political and economic dynamics. The negative coefficient is corroborated by other studies on UK migration, suggesting it persists both with and without freedom of movement. As such, I can infer that unemployment captures the influence of the business cycle on immigration. Additionally, there is significant evidence that the debt/GDP ratio influences non-EU migration – a one-point faster rise in the debt/GDP ratio in the previous year is followed by roughly 0.6% higher inflows (coefficient 0.57 on the log change).

Without any framework to study on the making of British fiscal policy, my original model focused on the Chancellor’s Budget Day speech, a highly publicised annual event which crystallises fiscal policy and must meet both political and fiscal constraints – otherwise, an unpopular programme can bring down an entire government. I also establish a mechanism for how migration can act as a stealth ‘fiscal credit’, by adding to the working age population and contributing taxes. This involved the dynamic of the Treasury’s well-established influence, using informal and formal powers to influence the policies of other departments, especially thanks to their ability to fund and defund departments. The growing power of the executive since 1979 allowed ministers to change migration policy without parliamentary debate, with the help of statutory instruments.
