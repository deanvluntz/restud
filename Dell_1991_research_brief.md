Passages marked (garbled) should be checked against the physical page before quoting. The OCR has no title/imprint page, so check the citation against the book: Dell, E. (1991) *A Hard Pounding: Politics and Economic Crisis, 1974–1976*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

## 1. Dell's role

- **Paymaster General, March 1974 – April 1976:** Wilson made him effectively number two across the Treasury, but explicitly without responsibility for public expenditure. He was outside the Cabinet (Short Parliament chapter, early pages).
- **Secretary of State for Trade, in the Cabinet, from April 1976 under Callaghan** (pp. 212–13). So during the IMF crisis (Sept–Dec 1976) he was **not a Treasury minister**, and he was never an official.
- **In the crisis:**
  - He backed Healey's call for spending cuts at the Economic Strategy Committee on 3 November (p. 253).
  - He argued against Crosland at Callaghan's private meeting before the 23 November Cabinet (p. 257).
  - He calls Crosland's alternatives a loss of judgement (p. 261).
  - In retrospect he frames his position as deliberate "overkill" that could later be reversed (p. 285).

## 3. The Treasury as an institution: material that helps the thesis

- **"The Mandarins' Revolt"** (ch. 11, from p. 119): in late 1974 the Treasury's permanent secretaries sent the Chancellor a joint minute, copied only to the Governor of the Bank. Dell obtained a copy through his own channels, and Wass and the Governor privately agreed on a wage freeze (pp. 119–20). This shows officials acting collectively and controlling who saw what.
- **Cabinet deference:** even a well-informed Cabinet had to leave some matters to the Treasury (p. 274).
- **1976 constitutional change:** Pliatzky's reforms (the contingency reserve and cash limits) meant Treasury ministers could no longer be overruled in Cabinet committees. Dell calls this very important (p. 184).
- **Dell's own position:**
  - He endorses the Treasury's role as defender of the arithmetic of resources (p. 121).
  - No other department should rival or second-guess it (p. 212).
- **Institutional weakness:**
  - He blames the weakness of the official Treasury for part of the crisis (p. 280).
  - He concedes Healey's distrust of Treasury advice in 1975 was partly justified (pp. 179–80).
- **Independent central bank (p. 287):** he argues for one because the Treasury has several objectives, some of them political, and serves political masters. This links neatly to your OBR/Bank of England material.
- **No. 10 and the Bank:** Callaghan was exasperated by the Treasury's handling of the March 1976 sterling slide (Donoughue, quoted p. 206). He also kept the IMF team waiting almost a fortnight before officials could talk to them (pp. 247–48).
- **Responsibility:** the IMF crisis was Britain's own doing, not its trading partners' (p. 281).

## 4b. Additional points (24 Sep 2026)

- **"Codswallop"** is in Dell's own book, early in chapter 1 (IMG 006b; check the page number, probably p. 4–5):
  - In Opposition, before the February 1974 election, Dell told Healey privately that the Social Contract was "a load of codswallop".
  - He adds that he was understating it. His objection was economic: it committed Labour to the Heath government's wage-threshold indexation.
- **"Overkill"** (p. 285): he argued deliberately for overkill, on the grounds that measures could be added back later. This is ex-ante insurance, not a later admission that the cuts went too far. He then cites the rapid return of confidence as vindication.
- **Pliatzky rule** (p. 184): Treasury ministers could not be overruled in *Cabinet committees*; disputes over the contingency reserve went to full Cabinet, which Pliatzky said "made Cabinet itself the monitoring authority". It took effect during 1976, after Pliatzky became Second Permanent Secretary at the end of 1975.

- **p. 5, NIESR–Treasury link:** not found in the OCR, probably in a garbled area; check the photo. The nearest passage is a note (NIER, May 1974) saying the Treasury, London Business School and NIESR forecasts were all too high for 1973.

  - The Chief Secretary (Joel Barnett, the 1992 reviewer) was made to rank *behind* the Paymaster General, who was outside the Cabinet. Dell reads this as a signal that the government would not pay excessive attention to controlling public expenditure. So Barnett was effectively No. 3, not Dell's No. 2.
  - Legible: the Treasury sits next to No. 10, "the effective centre of government", and Treasury ministers often have more influence than many Cabinet ministers.
  - The lines about attending Cabinet and visiting No. 10 more often than most Cabinet ministers are garbled; check the photo before quoting.
- **p. ≈34, March 1974 Budget:** confirmed.
  - Benn's view that the Budget had been "written by the Treasury and not by Ministers".
  - Stronger context just before it: the Cabinet discussed Healey's Budget only hours before it became public, when it could no longer influence it.
  - Useful for §5.1.3 (Budget Day) as evidence of Treasury control over the Budget.

## 4d. Further extracts (Sam's photos, 25 Sep 2026) and Rogers (2010)

**Dell, pp. 248–285 (IMF chapters), from your photos — text confirmed:**
- p. 248: Callaghan distrusted the Treasury "and he could hardly distrust the Treasury without also distrusting its political head"; he kept the IMF team in their hotel for almost a fortnight before letting Treasury officials talk to them.
- p. 249: the IMF team faced "a divided Treasury"; the rumour, believed by Donoughue and possibly Callaghan, that "parts of the Treasury [were] in very deep cahoots with parts of the US Treasury" to impose tough terms. Dell reports the allegation without endorsing it.
- p. 252: on 3 November the PSBR was estimated at £11bn; "it had not yet been realized what an impact cash limits were having even in 1976/7".
- p. 257: Crosland's case against the cuts, including that "Treasury forecasts of the PSBR were unreliable; other experts' forecasts were much lower".
- p. 271: Barnett: "Edmund Dell, as usual, gave the strongest support"; the IMF "knew they were getting a good deal more than the predominant school of opinion in the Treasury thought was necessary".
- p. 273: the Letter of Intent: PSBR targets £8.7bn (1977/8) and £8.6bn (1978/9); adjustments £1.5bn and £2bn; DCE limits.
- p. 274: "Some matters even this Cabinet … had to leave to the Treasury"; £10.5bn forecast vs £8.5bn outturn, blamed on unknown departmental underspending.
- p. 284: cash limits produced underspends larger than the IMF cuts; "Had Departments known how to control their budgets … the Government could have accepted the original IMF proposals without any difficulty". Lever to Barnett: "Edmund makes you look like a left-winger throwing money away."
- p. 285: "the argument was for overkill. If it proved to be overkill, we could always add back."

**Rogers, C. (2010) 'The Labour Government, the Treasury and the £6 pay policy of July 1975', *British Politics*, 5(2), pp. 224–236.** Archive-based (TNA T 277, T 358, PREM 16, CAB 128). Argument: by December 1974 Wass's Treasury wanted a formal incomes policy that the TUC, the Labour Party and Wilson had ruled out. Wass wrote that the recommendation might become acceptable in "an external crisis which would justify it". The Treasury then pursued "parsimonious intervention" in the foreign exchanges: it authorised only small sums for the Bank of England to support sterling, so that "the main responsibility for depreciation is seen to lie elsewhere than with HMG" (T 358/207, April 1975), because "it is indeed our policy to get the rate down". The resulting slide was presented as a crisis of confidence in counter-inflation policy, and the £6 pay norm followed in July 1975. Rogers frames this as depoliticisation (Flinders and Buller, 2006): a policy the government wanted, made to look imposed by markets.

**Why this matters for the thesis.** It is direct archival evidence of the Treasury engineering the appearance of external constraint to obtain a policy that elected politicians had rejected, one year before the IMF episode. It is exactly the mechanism §5.2.4 asserts, with documents rather than memoirs. Two ways to use it:
1. Add to §5.2.4 as the precedent: one paragraph (about 110 words) before the IMF story, citing Rogers (2010). This also gives the section its missing archival footing and connects to Wass, whom you already cite.
2. Add to §5.3 as an example of visibility management: the Treasury chose an instrument (non-intervention) whose effects would be attributed to markets, which is the same logic you apply to migration.
Reference (academic, no accessed date): Rogers, C. (2010) ‘The Labour Government, the Treasury and the £6 pay policy of July 1975’, *British Politics*, 5(2), pp. 224–236.

## 5. Suggested redraft of the contested sentence (for your decision)

Dell's book does not support a claim that he admitted misrepresentation. It does support a sharper and more defensible point for your argument: the Cabinet acted on a Treasury PSBR forecast about £2bn too high, and ministers could not check it. Possible wording:

> The Cabinet accepted the IMF's terms on a Treasury forecast of the 1976/7 borrowing requirement of £10.5 billion; the outturn was £8.5 billion. Edmund Dell, then Trade Secretary and formerly Paymaster General, attributes the gap to departmental underspending unknown at the time, but concedes that some matters even a well-informed Cabinet "had to leave to the Treasury" (Dell, 1991, p. 274). Healey, by contrast, suspected officials of deliberately overstating public spending to press reluctant governments (Healey, 1990; Dell, 1991, p. 185).
