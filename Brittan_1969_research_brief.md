## 2. Power, doctrine, officials, secrecy, Cabinet

**Power.** Treasury control rests "in the last resort" on the Chancellor's authority in Cabinet; if overborne "the Treasury must comply" (p. 84, para.). Props: Standing Order 78 (1713); any new or increased service "requires Treasury sanction"; Exchequer and Audit/PAC (pp. 84–5). Departments still saw it in the late 1960s "as it had been two centuries before, primarily as the body that held down their spending" (p. 83). Its functions "are not defined by statute" (p. 36). In 1968, 10 of 24 Permanent Secretaries outside the Treasury/Cabinet Office, plus the Cabinet Secretary, had ten-plus years' Treasury service (p. 9); able Treasury men circulate through the Cabinet Office and No. 10, "the centre of the Government machine" (p. 23).

**Doctrine.** The Treasury remains "very much concerned to restrain Government spending and combat tax proposals that threaten the revenue", which predicts its behaviour better than "the more sophisticated doctrines which the Department proclaims" (p. 36). Hopkins between the wars was "the fountainhead of the Treasury view" that spending could not relieve unemployment (p. 37). Bank Governors "have felt passionately about the evils of government spending" (p. 49). Nationalized-industry finance shows the compromise between cash saving and "the return on real resources" (p. 97).

**Continuity and character.** Bridges's departmental inheritance is "something he will ignore at his peril" (p. 20). Policies "change very slowly, and usually in response to external disturbances"; inertia, not ideology (p. 27, para.). Premium on innovations that "do not challenge the basic assumptions of existing policy" (p. 24, para.). "An instinctive reluctance to take seriously contributions from outside" (p. 26); longest adherence to "the constitutional fiction that it had no policies of its own" (p. 26); it "deals with the world through other Departments" (p. 25); papers to Ministers carry no names (p. 24).

**Ministers and officials.** Ministers rarely can "question the analysis on which the advice to them is based" (p. 28); before 1967 they "interfered enough to prevent the Treasury policies being carried out logically and consistently, but were not able to produce a substitute policy of their own" (p. 28). Officials "do not sufficiently push forward unpalatable truths", yet Ministers are "terribly in the hands of the Civil Servants for the analysis of problems" (p. 30).

**"The Secrecy Obsession" (pp. 32–5).** Advice to Ministers is treated as "on a par with the secrets of the confessional" (p. 32). Budget forecasts unpublished until 1968 ("only the tip of the iceberg"); the five-year expenditure projection published only in 1963 and 1966 (p. 32). Secrecy keeps "British MPs in the dark", not the markets; "the belief that silence about a disagreeable prospect will help to change it" is a fallacy (p. 32). Leak-hunting "became even worse after the change of Government in 1964" (p. 33). Ministers guard "the myth that all policy originates with them"; collective responsibility is "the most deeply rooted obstacle of all" (p. 34).

**Cabinet and Budget.** "The Cabinet itself hears the Budget only the day before" (p. 69); "Budget secrecy has become a cult" (p. 69). The Cabinet economic committee "in no sense" instructed the Chancellor (p. 41); Ministers told of the judgment "very restricted" (p. 66). "DEA economists first heard of the Budget decisions at the same time as the rest of the population" (p. 58).

## 3. Budget process and forecasts

- Budget Committee (Permanent Secretary chairing; Revenue chairmen, economic advisers, Finance and Public Sector heads, Bank) meets "continuously from June to March" (p. 60). Table 6 (p. 62): summer forecast; November forecast; January first report, PM consulted; February "Budget judgment"; early-April pre-Budget Cabinet.
- Forecasting "is nowadays at the centre of the whole Budget exercise, whether Chancellors choose to admit it or not" (p. 63); the target is "pressure of demand", optimum unemployment "around 2·0 per cent" (p. 63). Forecasts in February, June, October; June ones in 1961, 1965 and 1966 "led to deflationary packages more important than the Budget itself" (p. 53). Medium-term projections run about five years ahead, moved to the DEA in 1964 (p. 53), begun in the late 1950s to judge affordable public expenditure (p. 54, para.); inputs are productivity and the working population (p. 54) — the only entry of labour supply into the machinery.
- Publication: monthly report from 1967 (p. 52); the 1968 Budget was cast in borrowing-requirement terms "largely to impress overseas opinion" (p. 65). The demand judgment comes first and "determines … the 'borrowing requirement'" (p. 79, para.); the IMF's £1,000m ceiling was "a rough-and-ready way of imposing some discipline on the British" (p. 80).

## 4. Public purse; demography, pensions, migration

- Of 103 Treasury administrators in 1967–8: 51 Public Sector, 44 Finance, 8 Economic (p. 83). The DEA made the Treasury "more of a traditional Finance Ministry" than at any time since 1947 (p. 83).
- PESC: a five-year forward programme "rolled forward every year" (p. 86); all plans "added up and considered together, against a forecast of the probable growth of the national income", first done in 1961 (p. 86). "It is arguable that it is the Treasury's public-expenditure White Papers [that] represent the real 'National Plan'" (p. 86). Year 3 is where change is possible, Years 4–5 are "projections of existing policies" (pp. 86–7); Ministers must cut, raise taxes, or both (p. 87). The 1965–6 non-departmental Ministers' committee lapsed into bilateral bargaining (p. 87).
- Practice: "The chronic weakness of the public expenditure surveys of the 1960s was their tendency to underestimate future costs" — 4¼ per cent ceiling vs ~6 per cent actual; five emergency cuts July 1965–January 1968 (p. 90). The total includes the National Insurance Funds (p. 85) and NI pensions as transfers (p. 91).
- 'Real resources': "Where will the money come from?" is treated as meaningless; the test is overloading the economy (p. 94, para.). REP was the Treasury's "breakthrough" in real-resource costing, forced by irregular advisers and the DEA (p. 95).
- **Search results** (pension, actuar*, population, immigra*, Commonwealth, labour force, manpower, ageing, dependency, work permit, labour supply, birth rate): **no mention** of immigration, the 1962/1968 Acts, work permits, the Government Actuary, ageing or dependency ratios. "Commonwealth" is only the CRO (p. 314), a PM's tour (p. 136), finance ministers (p. 229), the Guildhall speech (p. 194). Fragments: the 4 per cent NEDC target "related to the period 1961–6, when the underlying expansion of the working population averaged 0·8 per cent per annum"; owing to "demographic and other factors" the labour force has stopped growing "and is actually expected to fall in the very early [1970s]" (p. 282; cf. pp. 177, 287). Official fallibility includes "the forecasting of population movements, the emigration of doctors and the demand for steel" (p. 279). Pensions appear only as timing devices: Maudling's abortive 1963 plan to pay higher pensions before raising contributions, beaten by "seemingly irreducible time-lags" (p. 174); the November 1964 Budget offsetting higher benefits (p. 193); June 1967 increases (p. 227). Ministry of Pensions renamed Social Security 1966 (p. 39). Labour shortages are cyclical bottlenecks only (pp. 199, 284).

## 5. The DEA episode 1964–69

- Wilson wanted rival strategies "represented by different Ministers" rather than "argued out in the depths of the official machine" (p. 201). The Concordat (DEA long term/physical; Treasury short term/finance) rested on "pre-Keynesian illusions about the autonomy of finance" and old-fashioned socialist ideas of physical control (p. 201). While the Treasury holds the balance of payments and Bank Rate it "is likely to remain the effective economic ministry" (p. 202, para.). "Two rival economic ministers locked together in uncreative tension", the PM swinging between them "on no known principle" (p. 201).
- Victory: "power gradually shifted back to the Treasury under the pressure [of events] in the sterling market" (p. 58). The DEA "neither had operational control … nor control over the purse strings"; it lost overseas co-ordination 1966–7, incomes policy 1968, projections January 1969 (p. 58). No irregular went to the Treasury (p. 30). Footnote cites "Why the DEA lost to the Treasury Knights", *Sunday Times*, 3 March 1968 (p. 201).
- National Plan: "written off by many people two months before it was published" (p. 199); spending limits fixed January 1965 before the Industrial Inquiry began (p. 278); "the economic howler of leaving the Chancellor of the Exchequer off NEDC" (p. 281); July 1966 the "final blow" (pp. 281–2); planning "postponed for at least six years (1960–6) the real debate on policy" (p. 286). After the débâcle the 1969 Assessment gave "forecasts rather than policy instruments" (p. 287); the Treasury is "extremely reluctant to provide such hostages to fortune" (p. 289).

## 6. Epilogue

Jay quoted: no "strange beings as 'the Treasury'", only "a collection of individuals" (p. 313). "The only appropriate attitude to it is that of ambivalence. It suffers from the defects of most highly inbred organizations of intelligent men" (p. 313); it "was ahead of most other Finance Ministries" in Keynesian technique (p. 313). Opie's "overseas lobby" was the main handicap; "The Treasury was hardly the centre of this lobby" (p. 314). Recommendations: fewer "federal" ministries; one minister for all economic management, whatever his title (p. 316); a tax minister over the Revenue; possibly a Bureau of the Budget (p. 316); junior Ministers on official committees; a public-facing Council of Economic Advisers (pp. 317–18); cash social security under the economics minister (p. 317, para.). Policy guideline: an "external regulator", i.e. exchange-rate flexibility (pp. 318–20).

## 7. Labour 1964–70

- Chronology of crises and "false dawns" 1964–7 in Brittan's aide-mémoire (p. 220).
- **SET (1966):** an orthodox deflationary Budget "prepared by the Treasury" was rejected at the last moment as "a repetition of familiar Treasury medicine" (p. 210). 25s per male employee on employers' NI contributions, refunded to transport, agriculture and the public sector, premium to manufacturing, net £240m (p. 210); about a 3–4 per cent tax on services (p. 211); the poll-tax element hit "unskilled, older or handicapped workers" (p. 212); collection from September 1966 (p. 212).
- **July 1966:** regulator, hire-purchase, £150m cuts, freeze then "severe restraint"; the Treasury used "the standby packages which that Department often has ready"; the "three knights" (Trend, Armstrong, Roll) cut while Wilson was in Moscow, yet "real public expenditure rose by 7 per cent in 1967–8" (p. 217). 'Redeployment' was a euphemism for higher unemployment (p. 218); "July 1966 would have been the ideal time to devalue" (p. 219).
- **REP (1967):** 30s per week per male manufacturing worker in Development Areas, seven years, ~£100m p.a.; from MacDougall (DEA) and Kaldor (Treasury), Armstrong instrumental (p. 224, para.); "roughly equivalent to a partial devaluation combined with a cash transfer" (p. 225).
- **Devaluation:** until summer 1967 there was no "'Treasury view' on devaluation" — Ministers had ruled it out (p. 229); but "a central core of 'regular' economists and officials", present under the Conservatives and persisting "right up to 1967", would defend the parity at ~2 per cent unemployment (p. 229). A small Treasury/Bank group held contingency plans of which "no account was allowed to be taken" (p. 231). The EEC application was "the real origin" of the Treasury's change of attitude (p. 226); further deflation "was no part of the philosophy of the dominant middle-of-the-road wing of the Treasury" (p. 231).
- **"Pound in your pocket"** "did not, however, deserve quite all the obloquy it received" (p. 236); Wilson was "Parity Harold" to an adviser (p. 236 n.). In 1967 the Treasury knew its policy was expansionary but "had no wish to broadcast this fact to the foreign exchange market" (p. 223).

## 8. Ten quotable sentences on Treasury power

1. p. 83 — departments still saw the Treasury "as it had been two centuries before, primarily as the body that held down their spending": continuity of the purse role.
2. p. 36 — restraint of spending predicts Treasury behaviour better than "the more sophisticated doctrines which the Department proclaims": revealed preference.
3. p. 28 — Ministers rarely can "question the analysis on which the advice to them is based": dependence on officials.
4. p. 28 — politicians blocked Treasury policy but "were not able to produce a substitute policy of their own": only source of coherent strategy.
5. p. 20 — the departmental inheritance "is something he will ignore at his peril" (Bridges): doctrinal transmission.
6. p. 27 (para.) — policies "change very slowly, and usually in response to external disturbances": inertia.
7. p. 26 — the Treasury takes "the doctrine that everything is secret until it is publicly announced" more seriously than any other department: low visibility.
8. p. 32 — the fallacy "that silence about a disagreeable prospect will help to change it": secret forecasts.
9. p. 69 — "The Cabinet itself hears the Budget only the day before": Cabinet excluded.
10. p. 58 — power "gradually shifted back to the Treasury under the pressure [of events] in the sterling market": DEA defeated.

## Flags: where Brittan cuts against the thesis's picture

- **Not monolithic.** Jay's "collection of individuals" endorsed (p. 313); devaluation views "varied from person to person" (p. 229); a "dominant middle-of-the-road wing" implies others (p. 231); Finance side leans to the Bank, Economic Section to real forces (pp. 55–6). Attribute doctrine to "the dominant wing".
- **Not simply fiscally conservative.** "Ahead of most other Finance Ministries" in Keynesian management (p. 313); spending overran its own ceilings (pp. 90, 223); public investment "deliberately allowed to increase" in 1967 (p. 223). The classic "Treasury view" is dated to Hopkins between the wars (p. 37).
- **Not unaccountable in law.** Authority rests on the Chancellor in Cabinet (p. 84); PAC, Estimates Committee and the 1966–7 Select Committee exist (pp. 84–5, 34) — the complaint is triviality, not absence.
- **Not the sole locus of power.** The Bank is "an independent self-perpetuating entity with a distinctive viewpoint" on which the Treasury depends (p. 28); the Revenue Departments "report independently to the Chancellor" (p. 70); responsibility for non-devaluation 1964–7 "rested … with the political leaders" (p. 188).
- **No demographic or migration content.** Forward projection appears only as PESC and five-year assessments (pp. 53–4, 86–7). Use Brittan for institutional character, secrecy and projection machinery; a claim that the 1960s Treasury discussed immigration or ageing cannot cite him and must rest on Hemming and Kay and GAD/Treasury sources.
