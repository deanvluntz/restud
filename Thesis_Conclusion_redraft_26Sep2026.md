# 12. Conclusion — full redraft (26 Sep 2026, for Sam's approval; not installed)

This thesis asked why policy-controlled immigration to Britain rose across four decades and governments of every colour, when no party campaigned for it and several campaigned against it. The answer I have defended is fiscal. The old-age dependency ratio is the one fiscal pressure a government can see coming a decade ahead, because the people who will be pensioners in ten years are already resident and counted; and of the three instruments available to meet it, taxation, borrowing, and the admission of working-age taxpayers, the last was the only one that could be adjusted without a Budget, a Finance Bill, or a vote. My hypothesis was that the Treasury's own projections of that ratio would predict the flows the state could ration, and the evidence supports it.

The central estimate comes from an autoregressive distributed lag model of log non-EU inflows per thousand working-age residents on the projected ten-year change in the old-age dependency ratio, conditional on the lagged tax burden per working-age person, the lagged log change in the debt-to-GDP ratio, and the lagged unemployment rate. Each additional point of projected ten-year deterioration is associated with 0.81 per cent higher non-EU inflows in the year, and with roughly two per cent in the long run once the small-sample bias in the persistence term is corrected (1.8 per cent by Kendall's correction, 2.1 per cent by a moving-block bootstrap, with a 95 per cent interval of 0.9 to 3.0). In the units of the sample, a one-standard-deviation rise in the projection, about nine points, raises inflows by close to eight per cent in the year and by about sixteen per cent once the persistence term has worked through. The relationship holds at every horizon from three years out, in levels as in changes, and fits best at ten years, the horizon the Code for Fiscal Stability prescribed for the government's own long-term projections, while the one-year change carries no signal at all. The fiscal controls behave as the salience argument predicts: a heavier inherited tax burden and a faster rise in debt are each followed by larger inflows, at about 4.8 per cent per additional thousand pounds of tax per working-age person and 0.6 per cent per point of faster debt growth, and a point of unemployment in the previous year reduces inflows by about two per cent.

The evidential weight of the thesis rests less on that coefficient than on the tests it could have failed. A fiscal mechanism makes two predictions that a demographic or labour-market story does not. Only the pensioner-specific ratio should matter, because pensioners are expensive to the state in a way children are not, and only the flows the government could ration should respond. Both hold. Substituting the projected change in the total dependency ratio eliminates the result (0.0015, p = 0.26) and does not earn the degree of freedom it costs; estimating the same equation on EU inflows, which no British government could restrict, returns nothing (0.0028, p = 0.42). Rebuilding the projections from each vintage's published components of change under zero net migration, so that the regressor cannot contain the government's own assumptions about the dependent variable, leaves the estimate at 0.0075. The projection also moves the instrument and not merely the outcome: work permits and first permissions, the lever the Home Office actually operated until 2008, rise by about 540 a year for each projected point (p = 0.010), and the total-dependency placebo is null for permits as it is for inflows. The relationship is stable over time, with expanding and rolling samples settling on the full-sample value and no evidence of a break (OLS-CUSUM p = 0.94; sup-F p = 0.46), and it survives the supply-side determinants the migration literature would substitute for it, relative income, origin-country youth, growth, and networks, which neither predict the projections' revisions nor displace their coefficient. Realised age-related spending, added in the same form, is never significant and never improves the fit: controlled migration tracks projected costs, not incurred ones. One placebo gives a less tidy answer. British citizens arriving from outside the EU, whom no rule could exclude, show a weak response that is concentrated in arrivals from former parts of the Empire and absent among returners from Oceania and North America; a portion of that flow is first-time migration of passport holders by descent, itself a product of nationality law, and controlling for it directly leaves the projection at 0.0057, which I take as a lower bound on the policy response.

The result also bears on the leading account of the expansion. The policy-era dummies of the structural-change literature add nothing once the projections are included, because the dummies were standing in for them: the projected ten-year change averaged fifteen points inside the 1998–2007 window and was close to zero or negative outside it. Non-EU inflows rose in 1981–85 and again in 1996, under Conservative governments, when the projections turned positive, and the counterfactual the model implies is that the post-1997 expansion would have occurred under either party, in different rhetoric.

Three things make the mechanism credible beyond the statistics, and Section 5 sets them out. The Treasury has held the information and the authority throughout: the projections were produced by a department that described itself as the Chancellor's, and the department that set every other department's budget could obtain policy it wanted without announcing it, as the IMF episode, the £6 pay norm, and Healey's own account of the forecasts he was given all show. The salience asymmetry is institutional rather than chosen: tax and spending must be defended on Budget Day, while the Immigration Rules are amended by statutory instrument on any afternoon. And for one episode the reasoning is on paper: the 2000 Cabinet Office drafts set out migration as the alternative to "the more unpleasant alternatives of tax increases, benefit cuts, and economic stagnation", and the published version removed the passage.

To my knowledge this is the first study to separate policy-controlled from uncontrolled migration to the United Kingdom in a test of this kind, the first to reconstruct the government's own demographic projection vintages as the information set behind policy, and the first to test whether an ageing destination pulls migrants through deliberate policy rather than through the labour market, the inverse of the source-country demographic result the migration literature has long documented. The reconstructed vintage series is itself reusable by anyone who wants to ask what governments do with their own forecasts.

The limits are set out in Section 11 and are worth restating. The sample is one country and forty-one annual observations, which caps the number of regressors and the power of the cointegration tests, whose moderate support for a level relationship is read accordingly. The projections are the government's own and embed migration assumptions, a concern the zero-migration reconstruction addresses but only under the approximation that all migrants are of working age. Outside the 2000 drafting record the evidence for the mechanism is institutional and statistical rather than documentary, for a reason the model itself supplies: an instrument valued for its silence is not announced. The association I document is robust to every falsification I have been able to devise, but a fully causal account awaits the projection-revision, administrative, and archival designs proposed in Section 11.

The implication reaches beyond migration. Democracies facing slow-moving fiscal pressures possess instruments that differ enormously in visibility, and Britain's experience suggests that consequential policy can flow through the least visible of them for decades without any government having to defend it, the concealment supplied not by any actor but by the architecture of the state. As populations age across the developed world and the arithmetic Britain confronted becomes general, when governments reach for quiet instruments, and what that choice does to democratic accountability, seems to me the most important question this research raises.
